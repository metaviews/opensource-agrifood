## The problem, in plain terms

Canada's farmers are getting older, and fewer young people are taking their place. The
average farm operator is 56. About six in ten are 55 or older. Fewer than one in ten is
under 35. Only about 12% of farms have a written plan for who takes over — and even that
number is disputed between surveys.

Meanwhile the land itself is getting more expensive faster than a new farmer can save:
farmland values have risen roughly 9% a year. So the asset that has to change hands keeps
moving out of reach of the people who might farm it.

When a farm does change hands, the rules assume it goes to a child. The tax breaks, the
government guides, and the whole way the sector talks about succession are built around
a family successor. But the demographics imply many farms will not have a child who wants
to farm. And the public cannot see who is actually buying the land — there is no open
record of who owns what, or what they paid.

This is being called the largest private wealth transfer in Canadian agricultural
history. The evidence for it is public. The mechanisms that decide where the land goes
are not.

## What we found

**1. The help already exists — it is just locked.** Every province publishes a
succession guide. Model legal documents exist. Financing models that work in other
countries are documented. But almost none of it is published under a licence that lets
anyone adapt it and share it. Reading is free; adapting it sends you back to a paid
professional. We found exactly one organization that publishes its legal work openly, and
one openly licensed succession workbook — and it is American. The sharpest finding: a
Canadian organization open-licenses its guide to finding land, but not its succession
toolkit. So the lock is a choice, not a necessity. The gap is licensing, not writing.

**2. The default helper is a lender.** The federal government's own answer to "how do you
support farm transfer?" points farmers to Farm Credit Canada — a lender — whose tools
cannot legally be adapted. Yet the people farmers actually ask first are accountants and
lawyers, not lenders. The lender is the biggest content producer, not the trusted first
stop. Any open alternative has to be independent of that lender.

**3. Nobody handles the farm's records when it is sold.** Farm software can export data,
but there is no documented process anywhere — open or commercial — for handing the farm's
history (yield maps, soil tests, spray records, herd data) to the next operator. The one
real sale contract we found explicitly left the records out of the deal. Some commercial
terms say you own your data but lose access to it when your subscription ends — exactly
when the farm changes hands. This is the one thing that genuinely needs building, not just
unlocking. Open-source farm software is the natural place to start, because it already has
the export plumbing and no reason to withhold.

**4. Open technology helps with running costs, not the price of entry.** Open-source tools
can cut recurring costs — service, energy, repair. They do not lower land prices or the
down payment. Canada does not even publish an itemized picture of what it costs to start
farming, so the exact share open tools could remove cannot be calculated. The honest role
for open technology: cheaper to stay in farming, not cheaper to get in. The heavy lifting
on entry belongs to tenure, financing, and policy.

**5. The rules are built for family, and almost nobody plans otherwise.** The tax
exemption and the tax deferral shelter a transfer from parent to child. Nothing equivalent
shelters a transfer to a beginning farmer, an employee group, or a co-operative. One
professional essay reports that only about 4% of succession plans even consider a
non-family successor — a striking figure, though we could not fully confirm it against the
underlying data. The architecture and the imagination both assume a child.

**6. The people making these decisions are often women, and they live a long time
afterwards.** About 58.6% of Canada's widowed farm operators are women. A woman who reaches
65 can expect about 22 more years — longer than a man. But the transfer vehicles are
designed for a transaction, not for the seller's remaining life. Son-preference is real
(one US study found 57% of chosen successors were sons versus 8% daughters) but not fixed:
who is doing the transferring changes the odds. Whether widowhood translates into real
control is still a hypothesis — no study has tested it, and Canadian research on women and
farm succession is largely absent.

**7. Conflict needs people, not just documents.** Succession is a family-conflict process.
Where public mediation is funded and names farm transition as an eligible topic, it works —
one US state reports about 70% of its mediation caseload is farm transition. Canada's
public mediation service handles insolvency only, and no farm-succession ombudsman exists
anywhere. Open documents can supply good dispute-resolution clauses, but the trust work
needs a person. The professional training layer is locked behind paid credentials.

**8. The story is told as a crisis, and that is part of the problem.** Media coverage of
farming has been measured as "overwhelmingly negative and fatalistic." Canadian farm press
leads with the plan-gap crisis. Nobody is telling succession as an opportunity. And who
farmers actually listen to is still unknown — there is no survey of it online. That is a
question to ask people, not to search for.

**9. We cannot see the wave — but the method to see it already works.** Outside British
Columbia, there is no public registry of who really owns land behind companies and trusts,
and no province publishes open sale-price data. But a Saskatchewan study turned public
land-titles records into a clear picture: 84 large owners holding 1.44 million acres,
concentration up 140% in five years, and no evidence of foreign ownership — correcting a
political panic. The method works wherever the data is public. Ontario, where the dollar
stakes are highest, cannot repeat it, because its ownership data is sold commercially.

## What could be done

These are proposals, grounded in the findings and set out in full in the program's
[design document](../docs/policy/succession-visibility-design.md). None is costed, piloted,
or agreed by anyone beyond this project.

- **Build an open Canadian succession commons.** Adapt the one openly licensed workbook
  for Canadian law and for French. Rebuild the closed legal templates under a licence that
  lets anyone adapt and share them, passing the same freedom on. Add three annexes:
  dispute-resolution clauses, provisions that care for the seller through their remaining
  life, and a records-handover package. House it somewhere independent of lenders — a
  university extension, a co-op federation, a farm-organization consortium — and fund its
  upkeep, not just its creation. Open assets die without maintenance.
- **Make the records handover real.** A standard export profile plus a model contract
  clause that moves the farm's records with the farm. The open-source farm software
  community is the natural first mover. This is the one genuinely new thing to build.
- **Repeat the Saskatchewan method elsewhere.** An open farmland observatory — code and
  method published so any province, university, or newsroom can run it — starting where the
  data allows, and naming Ontario's commercial lock-up as the policy problem it is.
- **Ask for the data and the shelter.** Open sale-price data and a public beneficial-
  ownership registry, following the UK and BC models. A tax shelter for non-family
  transfers, with guardrails against abuse — and a named uptake channel, because a good
  instrument nobody uses produces almost nothing (a well-designed US pilot produced two
  guarantees).
- **Tell it as an opportunity.** A successor-neutral information service through public
  extension, in both official languages, that competes on framing and not just on channel.
- **Make publicly funded farm-link services accountable.** Any service taking public money
  publishes its matching criteria and its real outcome numbers. This is accountability, not
  a promise of more matches — matching is a trust problem openness cannot solve by itself.
- **Open the output when public money pays for it.** When governments commission succession
  guides or legal work, require an open licence on the result. Publicly produced, publicly
  adaptable.

### Who could use this

- **Extension services and farm organizations** get a ready adaptation base and a clear
  licensing argument.
- **Beginning farmers and non-family successors** get instruments and knowledge that do not
  assume they are somebody's child.
- **Co-operatives and community land vehicles** get legal templates and financing models
  that already work elsewhere.
- **Policymakers** get the family-shaped gap named, the visibility ask, and the uptake
  caution.
- **Journalists and researchers** get a proven, repeatable open-analysis method.
- **The open-source farm software community** gets a concrete, buildable gap: the records
  handover.

## What we are not claiming

- Openness will not lower land prices, change tax law, or create successors. Those are the
  two biggest forces, and they are mostly beyond what open source can touch.
- Nothing here is costed, piloted, or agreed. The commons is a design grounded in findings,
  not a plan anyone has committed to.
- The disputed numbers stay disputed. We do not average them into a comfortable single
  figure. The link between widowhood and control stays a hypothesis, not a finding.
- Indigenous land jurisdiction is an interface to respect, not a problem to design around.
  Any ownership-visibility work is scoped to private-title farmland.
- The hardest questions — who farmers trust, whether an open instrument gets used — need
  conversations and fieldwork, not more web searches.
