## 0. The headline

**Two findings and one honest wall.** Finding one: the online evidence about who
farmers consult is *advisor-side and lender-mediated* — the strongest available
pattern is accountants and lawyers first, bankers and lenders second, with the
lender also running the sector's largest succession-content operation. Finding two:
the media environment around farming (US evidence) is measurably crisis-framed and
fatalistic, and Canadian trade press packages succession through that same
crisis-avoidance lens. The wall: **no succession-specific farmer-side trust survey
exists online, and no audience/reach data exists for any farm-transition media
series** — who farmers actually *listen to* for succession remains a correspondence
question, exactly as the cycle map predicted.

## 1. Cell 1 — who farmers consult (all lender-mediated or adjacent-topic)

- **Advisor engagement is low and the pattern is professional-services-first:**
  FMC research as quoted on FCC's own site (verified): "only 32 per cent of Canada's
  farmers are seeking help from business advisors and consultants," and "when it
  comes to seeking professional business advice, farmers usually turn to their
  accountants and lawyers, followed by their bankers and lenders." The lender is
  consulted but not first — which complicates C1's "default source is the lender"
  finding: the lender may be the default *content producer* without being the first
  *advisor*.
- **The plan-adoption disagreement widens, and all three numbers now trace:** 12%
  with written plans (2021 Census, as used by FCC's transition content); 8.4% (FMC's
  own 2019 conference paper, verified quote); 66% of producers *lack* a plan (RBC
  2023, via the Glacier FarmMedia article — verified verbatim). The program's
  no-averaging rule now holds four disagreeing figures (this pass adds the RBC
  reporting), recorded side by side, measures and years unreconciled.
- New Zealand case-study research: "accountants, lawyers and bankers are routinely
  involved, [while] farm consultants are often overlooked as a source of succession
  planning advice" (S).
- Adjacent-topic trust studies (technology adoption, climate adaptation) show peer
  farmers top-ranked and Extension most-trusted-where-measured — suggestive, never
  tested for succession (S, honestly labelled).

## 2. Cell 2 — how media frames it

- **The US framing analysis (FrameWorks Institute, 2020; verified from the PDF):**
  "The media often portray farmers as victims struggling under the weight of
  macroeconomic forces outside their control," and "The tone of these stories is
  overwhelmingly negative and fatalistic." General farming coverage, not succession
  — the transfer to succession framing is an inference, recorded as such.
- **Canadian trade press leads with the plan-gap statistic and the crisis-avoidance
  frame** (Glacier FarmMedia, verified): "According to a report released by RBC in
  April 2023, 66 per cent of Canadian producers don't have a succession plan" —
  with the sector's coach framing it as terminal: "Procrastination and conflict
  avoidance is killing agriculture" (Froese, verified). Opportunity framing appears
  only at the edges (the same article's younger-producer openness to "partnerships,
  cooperative models and public-private collaboration").
- **'Farmtok' is real but studied as climate dialogue, not succession** (Journal of
  Rural Studies, S); EU dissemination frames social media as career inspiration, not
  transition planning (S). No succession-specific social-media research was found.

## 3. Cell 3 — the open-publication model against the lender baseline

- **The lender baseline, now verbatim:** FCC runs a monthly podcast whose third
  season is an entire transition series — and its Terms of Use forbid derivatives:
  "or create any derivative works based on such content, without FCC's prior written
  consent" (verified, §08(b)). The sector's largest succession-media operation is
  read-only.
- **The counter-model:** American Farmland Trust's LANDED — a documentary podcast
  series entirely about farm transition, distributed free and offered to radio
  stations at no cost ("Radio stations are welcome to contact AFT about airing this
  show at no cost" — verified). Media-as-knowledge-production by a sector nonprofit.
  But **no licence grant and no audience figures** — free-to-access is not open-licensed,
  and reach cannot be compared against FCC's.
- **The licence contrast inside one organization (the pass's sharpest datum):**
  Young Agrarians publishes its Land Access Guide under an explicit, in-document
  licence — "licensed under the Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike
  2.5 Canada license. We hope that you'll share it, reference it, remix it" (verified
  from the PDF text layer) — while its sibling BC Transition Toolkit (the succession
  document) carries no licence line at all. The org that knows how to open-license
  its knowledge leaves its succession knowledge unlicensed. The pattern matches the
  program's C3 finding: succession-adjacent material is systematically the *least*
  openly licensed layer even inside open-minded organizations.

## 4. The finding

The decision environment is **adviser-led, crisis-framed, and licence-silent**:
the professionals consulted first (accountants, lawyers) own no succession knowledge
commons; the media frame is crisis-avoidance; and the one organization demonstrating
open licensing publishes its succession toolkit without a licence. For the program,
C11 converts the design document's P6 (successor-neutral information service) from a
service proposal into a **media-framing proposal**: the framing evidence says
opportunity-framed, publicly owned succession content has no incumbent. Honest wall:
the pass cannot say who farmers actually listen to — that is correspondence-bound
(who-to-ask: FMC's research team for the underlying advisor survey; FCC's audience
team; AFT's LANDED producer for reach data).
