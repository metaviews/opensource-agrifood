## 0. The honest headline

**The headline number cannot be computed, and the reason is itself a finding.** Canada
publishes no itemized beginning-farmer startup cost structure — the federal record
names the categories ("beginning farmers need to purchase land, equipment, inputs,
quota, etc." — AAFC consultation record, verified) without ever costing them, and the
program's own data gap ("little has been measured", C10) extends to entry economics.
What CAN be said, from verified pieces, is directional — and it relocates the open
stack's value from entry capital to operating cost.

## 1. Cell 1 — the cost floor, from Canadian sources

- **Land, the dominant line:** farmland values rose 9.3% in 2025 (verified from FCC's
  release), and FCC's own borrowing guidance puts the land-loan down payment at
  "25% of the purchase price," with pledging family land as the common workaround —
  the entry floor is family-collateral-shaped by default. FCC's Transition Loan
  (down payment "often not required", verified) is the lender's own answer to the
  floor it describes: "more than 30 years of increasing farmland values is good news
  for current owners, [but] can present a significant challenge to those who want to
  enter the agriculture sector."
- **The national survey evidence (verified):** Laforge et al.'s survey of 1,326
  new/aspiring/exited Canadian farmers (Canadian Food Studies, open access) finds the
  two dominant entry barriers are "affordable land and financing their early farm
  businesses" — **not technology**.
- **Quota, the acute case:** the DFO dairy quota price cap stood at $24,000/kg
  butterfat at the August 2026 exchange (verified) — implying ~$576,000 of quota
  capital for a 24-kg minimum entry (derived) — an exchange that was **cancelled with
  zero kilograms sold**, so realized entry cost cannot be read from it.
- **Working capital as timing risk:** Manitoba's new-farmer resource: "Significant
  investment is required months or years before income is received."
- **The non-ownership route is documented in the federal record itself:** young
  Saskatchewan farmers "started by renting, rather than buying their land and their
  equipment."

## 2. Cell 2 — what the corpus's own cost spines say about open substitutes

Synthesis over the corpus's paid-for evidence (all figures carry the corpus's own
provenance labels):

- **Open substitutes bite hardest on recurring cost, not entry capital.** The dairy
  robot spine: purchase is $400,000+ (aggregator-sourced, unverified), but the
  recurring maintenance/software stream runs $16,000–$38,000/yr — half to more than
  the purchase price over a 12-year life — with the contestable share (30–50%,
  assumed) worth $4,800–$19,000/yr to independent service. The CEA northern spine:
  control strategies alone recover 17–43% of a $435–$745/m²/yr energy bill.
- **Verified capital savings are narrower and real:** the used-robot on-ramp (a used
  Astronaut A4+ at $80,000 vs $186,308 new — 57% off, from Lely's own listings);
  AgOpenGPS open RTK autosteer at ~€1,660–2,190 against $10,000+ commercial entry
  (~78% reduction, order-of-magnitude); FAST Lab's open wood-based vertical PV
  racking modelled 22% below commercial metal racking (CERN-OHL-S-2.0, OSHWA
  certified — but "Made: No | Replicated: No").
- **The honesty check holds:** FarmBot is genuinely open (MIT) yet costs more than
  comparable closed systems — open does not automatically mean cheap for complex
  machines at low volume. And the strongest farmer-governed open-hardware
  cooperative, L'Atelier Paysan, entered judicial liquidation in April 2026 —
  governance-model strength does not solve the maintenance-funding problem.

## 3. Cell 3 — shared capital and non-ownership routes

- **Machinery sharing has a dated but concrete Canadian number:** three Saskatchewan
  1,500-acre grain farms combining equipment cut machinery cost per acre from
  $44.66 to $28.75 (conventional seeding) — roughly a 36% reduction (verified from
  the Iowa State Extension page citing Harris & Fulton; dated study, transferability
  unknown).
- **Quebec's CUMAs are the operating layer, with a shareable licence:** 64 active
  CUMAs, 2,025 member enterprises (2021, verified from the MAPAQ portrait, which
  states reproduction is permitted with attribution) — "réduire les coûts de
  production en favorisant l'utilisation, à moindre coût, d'équipements
  performants."
- **Custom hiring has a live price signal:** Ontario's 2024 OPACA survey of 200
  custom operators puts corn combining at an average $65/acre ($54–$75, 15th–85th
  percentile, verified) — the market rate at which an entrant rents machinery
  services instead of owning them.

## 4. The finding

**Directional reading, labelled as interpretation: open substitutes address a
materially larger share of recurring and operating cost than of entry capital.** The
verified capital-side savings (used-equipment on-ramps, ~78% on guidance hardware,
22% on modelled racking, ~36% via machinery sharing) are real but narrow against a
land+quota entry floor measured in hundreds of thousands of dollars; the recurring-
side savings (30–50% of the dairy service stream assumed contestable; 17–43% of
northern CEA energy) are proportionally larger and recur annually. **The program's
centre of gravity therefore stays with tenure and visibility (L3/L6), and the open
stack's honest role in succession is lowering the cost of *staying in* — operating,
maintaining, repairing — more than lowering the cost of *getting in*.** This also
sharpens P5's design: the non-family transfer shelter matters more than hardware
subsidies, and C8's financing models matter more than open machines.

## 5. What this pass does not do

- It does not compute the headline fraction (the Canadian data to do so does not
  exist publicly — recorded absence); it does not verify the dairy spine's
  aggregator-sourced figures (the corpus records the contradiction); the machinery-
  sharing figures are dated (1990s Harris & Fulton); the StatCan FFS young-operator
  debt-to-asset figure is snippet-only (S); custom-hiring rates bound what renting
  replaces, not what ownership costs an entrant.
