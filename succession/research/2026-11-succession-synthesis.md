## The claim, in one sentence

Canada's farm-succession enclosure is a licensing problem, not a tooling problem: the
knowledge, legal documents, financing models, and data-egress plumbing for non-family
transfer all already exist free-to-read, and what is missing almost everywhere is the
right to adapt and redistribute them — plus one genuine production gap nobody has built
(farm-data continuity at transfer) — so the buildable open object is an open-licensed
Canadian adaptation commons with three annexes, and its honest ceiling is lowering the
cost of staying in farming, while the load-bearing layers (tenure and visibility) remain
policy problems openness can inform but not solve.

## 0. What this synthesis is

Eleven bounded cycles, run 2026-11-18/19, curated 29 records into a collection of 170,
and resolved every one of the six candidate layers the frame named
(`../concept.md`; the cycle tree and each resolution in `research-mapping.md`). The
program consumed the land sectoral scan (G-OSA-35,
`../../research/2026-09-land-sectoral-scan.md`) as its evidence base. This document is
the join: what the eleven findings say together, what they license building, what they
forbid claiming, and what would change them.

The founding observation is the land scan's cross-cell synthesis 5: "Canada's farmland
transition will be the largest private wealth transfer in its agricultural history, and
the public cannot see it." The framing numbers were re-verified at StatCan source on
2026-11-19 (land scan Sources §1): 189,874 farms, 262,455 operators, average operator age
56.0, 60.5% aged 55+, 8.6% under 35, and 12.0% of farms with a written succession plan
(up from 8.4% in 2016) — all **verified**. Cultivated farmland value rose 9.3% in 2025
(FCC, **verified** at source by C4). The transfer projections are **verified as
reported** and now located (land scan Sources §2): FCC's >$50B is a stated lower bound
with a partially-open exit-rate methodology; the CBA's $53B/88% traces to a 2015 media
article and a census complement; the industry's ~75% of $785B is investor-authored
(Crescero's CEO, AIMA Journal, 2026-06-22). None is an open-methodology measurement, and
the synthesis carries them as direction, not magnitude.

## 1. What the evidence shows

Six findings, each earned in a named cycle.

**First, every cycle found the same shape: the assets exist; the rights do not.** C1
found Canadian succession knowledge rich and free-to-read but nowhere open-licensed —
provincial guides, the FMC national toolkit, FCC's toolset, CRA's rollover explainers —
while the sector's real adaptation practice is one-off private permission (Alberta's
Agdex 812-22: "Adapted with permission from the Manitoba Government", **verified**), and
the one open-licensed succession asset found anywhere is Land For Good's CC BY-SA 4.0
workbook (**verified** in-document). C3 found the legal layer the same: model documents
exist free-to-read across the sector, but the right to adapt and redistribute them
exists at exactly one organization (SELC, site-wide CC BY-SA 4.0, **verified**); Farm
Commons grants CC BY-ND only to paying members, The Farmers Land Trust's "open-source"
templates carry an adoption covenant, the UK CLT model rules forbid their own
reproduction, and the Canadian state layer is Crown-copyrighted or licence-silent
(**verified** from document text layers). C7 found model dispute-resolution clauses
free-to-read but unlicensed. C8 found the world's non-family financing models operating
and documented, none open. C11 found the media counter-model (AFT's LANDED) free-to-air
but unlicensed and unmeasured. The sharpest datum is C11's licence contrast: Young
Agrarians' sibling Land Access Guide carries an explicit CC BY-NC-SA 2.5 Canada licence
with a remix invitation (**verified** in-document) while its own succession toolkit is
licence-silent. **The licence gap is a choice, not a capacity limit.** Across knowledge,
legal instruments, conflict clauses, financing, and media, the enclosure is
legal-institutional — confirming the land scan's cross-cell finding 1 at the scale of a
whole human system.

**Second, the one genuine production gap is farm-data continuity at transfer (C5).**
The open stack has full egress but no handover: farmOS exports CSV/API with its own
completeness warning ("CSV exports do not include all data", **verified**), and LiteFarm
asserts ownership and deletion but publishes no transfer workflow (**verified**). The
closed stack has ownership language without continuity: FieldView's export right is
limited to "the same format as uploaded" (**verified**), and Trimble's terms grant
ownership while stating "Customer will not have access to Customer Data after
termination or expiration of the Subscription Term, unless otherwise indicated…"
(**verified**) — at farm sale the data is owned by someone who cannot reach
it. The one executed agreement examined (Northland/Fifield, SEC-filed, **verified**
verbatim) expressly excluded "corporate books and records" from conveyance. Nebraska
LB525 (2026) is the first statute making ag data producer-owned property (**verified as
reported** via NALC analysis, second-hand). Three matching absences were recorded with
search evidence. **Farm-data continuity at transfer is nobody's documented process** —
and the open stack is plausibly the natural first mover, because it has the egress
plumbing and no structural reason to withhold. This is the program's most novel claim;
it is a search-verified absence, not a survey of every contract.

**Third, the honest ceiling: the open stack lowers the cost of staying in, not getting
in (C4).** The headline fraction — what share of a new entrant's startup cost the open
stack removes — is **uncomputable**: Canada publishes no itemized beginning-farmer cost
structure (recorded absence). Laforge et al.'s 1,326-farmer survey says the barriers are
affordable land and early financing, not technology (**verified**). The verified pieces
point the same way: farmland +9.3%, a 25% land-loan down payment, the DFO quota cap at
$24,000/kg with the August 2026 exchange cancelled at 0.00 kg (**verified**), 64 Québec
CUMAs / 2,025 members under a reproduction-with-attribution licence (**verified**),
machinery sharing ~36%/acre (Harris & Fulton, dated). The corpus's own cost spines (dairy
service stream, CEA energy) show open substitutes bite hardest on **recurring** cost,
not entry capital. FarmBot (open but costlier) and L'Atelier Paysan's liquidation are the
standing honesty checks. **Resolution: the program's centre of gravity stays with tenure
(L3) and visibility (L6).**

**Fourth, the policy architecture is family-shaped, and the wave is still unseen (C6).**
The LCGE ($1.25M for qualified farm property), the tax-deferred rollover, and the
five-year reserve are built for parent-to-child transfer; the inclusion-rate increase
was cancelled; and the federal government's own answer to succession routes farmers to
FCC's learning programs and loan products (**verified** from AAFC's QP note, OFA, PMO) —
the policy-level statement of C1's lender finding. Nothing shelters non-family transfer
to entrants, co-operatives, or community vehicles. No federal public beneficial-ownership
registry operates (ISED's consultation is live); BC's LOTR remains Canada's only public
land registry of its kind; Ontario's parcel data is commercially locked. But the JSGS
brief "Who Owns Saskatchewan's Farmland?" proves the open-analysis method from public
titles data: 84 large-scale private owners holding 1.44M acres (2.4% of SK farmland,
+140% in five years), CPPIB fully divested by 2023, and "no evidence of non-Canadian
ownership" (**verified** from the PDF) — an evidence-based correction to the
foreign-ownership frame. The Sources completion surfaced one more datum: the CBA essay
reports **only 4% of farm succession plans contemplate non-family successors**
(**verified as reported**, footnoted to the census succession table; the table dimension
not independently confirmed) — the first named quantification of the non-family plan gap
the design document's P5 addresses.

**Fifth, the human system is the design constraint, not the background (C7, C9, C10,
C11).** Conflict support is a human-service layer with an open-document floor: the USDA
certified mediation programs make "Family farm transition" an eligible public topic with
published criteria (**verified**), Ohio reports ~70% of its docket is farm transition
(**verified as reported**), Canada's public layer (FDMS) is insolvency-only, no
farm-succession ombudsman exists anywhere, and the professional competency layer is
enclosed in paid credentialing (IFTN $900–$999, **verified**). The post-sale window is
long and longer for women: life expectancy at 65 is 22.3 years for females vs 19.7 for
males (StatCan 2023, **verified**); the seller-longevity failure mode is documented
inside installment-sale guidance; Austria's Leibrente remains the only verified
care-in-transfer instrument. The population is female-controlled and longevity-stressed:
9,685 widowed farm operators, Women+ 58.6%, 95.5% aged 55+ (**verified**, recomputed from
the open Agriculture–Population Linkage CSV); female operators 30.4% (first rise since
1991); son-preference measured at 57% sons vs 8% daughters, with the transferor's gender
doubling daughter-choice probability (Iowa State, **verified as reported**). But
widowhood → effective control is **hypothesis, not finding** — no study tests it, and
Canadian peer-reviewed work on women and farm succession is absent (the pass's central
recorded absence). The decision environment is adviser-led (only 32% of farmers seek
business advisors; accountants/lawyers precede lenders — FMC research as quoted on FCC's
site, **verified**), crisis-framed (FrameWorks 2020: "overwhelmingly negative and
fatalistic", **verified** verbatim), and licence-silent. Who farmers actually listen to
remains correspondence-bound.

**Sixth, the financing design space is real, operating, and closed (C8).** Kulturland eG
(interest-free member capital), Iroquois Valley Farmland REIT (131 investments, 36,000+
acres, lease-to-purchase), Fordhall Farm (community shares bought the freehold; 100-year
tenancy), Latvia's ALTUM purchase-lease-back and France's SAFER lease intermediation
(state intermediation, via the FAO study — the pass's only open-licensed artifact,
CC BY-NC-SA 3.0 IGO), and Austria's Leibrente (care labour priced into the transfer) —
all **verified** as operating models, none open. The one statutory near-exception is New
Zealand's Sharemilking Agreements Order 2011: a complete statutory standard income-share
contract published openly, its 21% share floor protected against erosion except through
the agreement's own dispute-resolution mechanism (**verified** verbatim) — the template
candidate for a Canadian standard non-family transfer agreement. The caution is
empirical: the USDA FSA Land Contract Guarantee's pilot produced **2 guarantees**.
Instruments without uptake infrastructure produce 2 guarantees, not 140.

## 2. What the findings license building

The shared build object, named after C1+C3 and extended after the full cycle set: **an
open-licensed Canadian adaptation commons** — a vehicle that takes the free-to-read
knowledge and legal documents that already exist, adapts them to Canadian jurisdictions,
and republishes under share-alike terms the sector's own practice already implies
(Alberta adapting Manitoba "with permission" is the behaviour; the licence is its
generalization). It carries three named annexes:

- **Conflict clauses (C7):** model dispute-resolution clauses for tenure/transfer
  instruments — Purdue's mediation clause, the NZ Order's in-clause mechanism, the
  Schumacher lease as instances. The open-document floor under a human-service layer.
- **Care-in-transfer provisions (C9):** designed for the seller's remaining life, not
  the transaction; Leibrente the only verified instance.
- **Data-continuity profile (C5):** a handover export profile plus a model
  records-conveyance clause. The one annex that is production, not adaptation — the
  program's novel build.

Design constraints carried by every cycle: the C1 control/funding/value-capture test;
lender independence (the default tools are FCC-authored and forkless by terms, and the
federal answer routes to FCC); the Indigenous-land interface (OCAP and jurisdiction —
interface, never design-around, per the land scan's sub-cell 4); maintenance funding from
the start (the corpus's maintenance-funding profiles; the Terre de Liens lesson that
commoning does not spontaneously produce open assets); the population constraint
(female-controlled, longevity-stressed); and the information-environment constraint
(crisis-framed, no opportunity-framed incumbent — the commons must compete on framing,
not just channel).

The second object is **the visibility method**: the JSGS open-analysis pattern
generalized as a Farmland Transition Observatory (design-doc P1) under CC BY-SA, starting
where the data allows (Saskatchewan's ISC, BC's ParcelMap open subset) and naming the
blocker where it does not (Ontario's Teranet lock-up) as the policy ask it is.

The design document (`../../docs/policy/succession-visibility-design.md`) carries seven
proposals; the cycle set refined three in place — P7 as accountability infrastructure
rather than a match-rate lever (C2), P5 with the NZ template and the uptake caution (C8),
P6 as an information-environment problem (C11). The administrative/statutory split
stands: P1/P3/P4/P6/P7 administrative; P2/P5 statutory. **The synthesis adds no new
proposal.** It confirms the two load-bearing objects (the commons, the observatory) and
the honest ceiling on the rest.

## 3. The strongest things a skeptic can say back

Kept in the document rather than footnotes, because they are the honest test of the claim.

- **Openness touches the two biggest stresses least.** It does not lower land prices,
  change tax law, or create successors (concept §4). The capital wall and the demographic
  cliff are where the crisis lives; the open surface is the mechanism enclosure, the
  knowledge, and the non-land cost of staying in. If the question is "will openness save
  the family farm?", the honest answer is: not by itself, and mostly not at the entry
  step.
- **The licence gap may be a symptom, not the disease.** Documents stay closed because
  adaptation carries liability and the professional layer is billable-hour; Farm Commons'
  members-only no-derivatives licence is a business model, not an oversight. An open
  commons still needs a lender-independent host and funded maintenance — the corpus's
  failed-infrastructure cases (Underground Meats, L'Atelier Paysan's liquidation, Sharing
  Excess's licence-null repos) are the caution. Opening the licence is necessary, not
  sufficient.
- **Matching is trust-and-volume, not information (C2).** The documented failure modes
  are trust, readiness, external conditions, and follow-through — not criteria opacity.
  Openness helps least at the step where the crisis bites hardest (getting a successor
  onto a farm). An open farm-link platform might improve accountability without improving
  match rates; the honest alternative finding stands.
- **The crisis framing itself is contested.** Plan coverage disagrees four ways (12%
  Census / 8.4% FMC / 66% no-plan RBC / 8% FMC toolkit — register C-OSA-10, unreconciled;
  the CBA 88% is the census complement, and the RBC figures share one source). A written
  plan is not a successful transfer; the 4% non-family figure is unconfirmed at table
  level; the widow-to-control mechanism is hypothesis. The framing numbers are the
  best-verified part of the record, but the narrative built on them is measured as
  fatalistic (FrameWorks) — and the synthesis must not reproduce the fatalism it
  documents.
- **The international models may not import.** NZ's Order is statutory in a dairy
  sharemilking tradition Canada lacks; Kulturland's member capital depends on German
  cooperative law and culture; SAFER is a state monopoly on rural land intermediation.
  The template candidates are design inputs, not transplants. The 2-guarantee caution is
  the empirical warning.

## 4. What would change the synthesis

The claim is falsifiable, and the frontier is partly correspondence-bound by the method
constraint:

1. A succession-specific farmer-side trust survey showing farmers do not want adaptable
   or open knowledge — would overturn the information-environment diagnosis (who-to-ask:
   FMC research team, FCC audience team, AFT LANDED producer).
2. An open-licensed Canadian succession asset appearing — the gap is a choice, so someone
   could choose differently; would move L1 from curation-and-adaptation to a study of why
   it did not spread.
3. A documented farm-data handover process anywhere, open or closed — would move C5 from
   production gap to adoption gap.
4. An itemized Canadian beginning-farmer cost structure — would make C4's headline
   fraction computable and could move the centre of gravity.
5. The census succession table's successor dimension confirming or refuting the 4%
   non-family figure — would harden or drop P5's quantification.
6. Uptake evidence for a non-family transfer instrument working at scale — would overturn
   the 2-guarantee caution.
7. A measured widow-to-control study — would move C10's central hypothesis to finding, or
   drop it.

## 5. What this synthesis does not claim

- It does not claim the commons will be built, cost anything, or has support beyond this
  project. The build object is a design grounded in findings, not a coalition position or
  a pilot.
- It does not claim openness solves the capital wall or creates successors.
- It does not resolve the Indigenous-jurisdiction interface; it carries it as a standing
  constraint (interface, never design-around).
- It does not average the contested figures, promote the widow hypothesis to finding, or
  count the 4% figure as verified.
- It does not claim the absence claims are exhaustive: C5's three absences are
  search-verified, not a survey of every contract; the land scan's honest residuals
  (Sources §7) remain unverified.
- It does not treat the event as the program's purpose. The program sits outside the
  November 2026 event's scope by user direction; it feeds future events and work.

## 6. Where the evidence stands

**Earned:** the census framing figures (re-verified at StatCan source 2026-11-19); the
licensing-gap finding across knowledge, legal, ADR, financing, and media layers
(C1/C3/C7/C8/C11, asset-by-asset against primary documents); the data-continuity absence
with its legal shape (C5, primary docs plus the SEC filing); the family-shaped tax
architecture (C6, AAFC/OFA/PMO); the JSGS open-analysis method (C6, verified from the
PDF); the demographic and gender arithmetic (C9/C10, recomputed from the open CSV); the
NZ Order's text (C8, verified verbatim); the YA licence contrast (C11, verified
in-document); the entry-cost resolution (C4, verified pieces plus a recorded absence).

**Thin:** the 4% non-family-plan figure (verified as reported; table dimension
unconfirmed); the Ohio ~70% docket (verified as reported); Nebraska LB525 (verified via
NALC analysis, second-hand — the statute text not read directly); the Iowa son-preference
finding's generalizability to Canada (a US study; Canadian peer-reviewed work absent);
the financing models' importability (design inputs, not transplants); and the "open stack
is the natural first mover" claim (a structural argument, not a demonstrated adoption).

**Not yet earned:** who farmers actually listen to (correspondence-bound); the entry-cost
fraction (uncomputable from public data); the widow-to-control mechanism (hypothesis); any
measured outcome of an open succession intervention (none exists); and the
maintenance/durability profile of the proposed commons (the corpus's profiles are a
method, not a guarantee).

The not-yet-earned items are the program's honest frontier. The first three are
correspondence-bound by the method constraint; the last two are what building the commons
would itself produce. The program's ending is not "the numbers say it should work" — it
is "the enclosure is specific, named, and in most cases a licence choice; the one
production gap is buildable; and whether it matters at scale is exactly what a built
commons would test."

## 7. The program's answer to its founding question

The question asked how open source could address the transfer as knowledge,
infrastructure, tenure design, technology, and visibility. The cycle set's answer,
bounded:

- **As knowledge:** curation and adaptation, not build-from-zero — the commons forks the
  one open workbook and rebuilds the closed models under share-alike terms (C1/C3).
- **As infrastructure:** the data-continuity annex (the one production gap) and the
  observatory method (the proven open-analysis pattern) (C5/C6).
- **As tenure design:** open legal assets plus the financing template candidates plus
  care and conflict clauses — the non-family pathway the family-shaped architecture lacks
  (C3/C7/C8/C9).
- **As technology:** honestly bounded — recurring cost, not entry capital; the open
  stack's role is lowering the cost of staying in (C4).
- **As visibility:** the method works where the data allows; the policy ask is the data
  (price-paid, beneficial ownership) and the non-family shelter (C6; design-doc P2/P5).

The deeper answer the program earns: the succession crisis is usually discussed as
demographics and money — the two stresses openness touches least. The tractable open
surface is the mechanism enclosure, and the cycle set converts it from "everything is
closed" into a specific, named, mostly-licence-shaped object with one buildable
production gap. That is a different political object than "the crisis": it is askable.
The licence gap is a choice (the YA sibling proves it); the data gap is buildable (the
egress exists); the visibility gap has a proven method (JSGS). What remains closed by
structure — the capital wall, the tax architecture, the trust-based matching step — is
named as policy, not as software. **The program's contribution is the precision, not the
salvation.**
