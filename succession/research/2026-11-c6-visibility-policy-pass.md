## 0. The question

The land scan (G-OSA-35, cross-cell synthesis 5) ended on "the largest private wealth
transfer in Canada's agricultural history, and the public cannot see it." Layer L6 asks
whether that is still true in late 2026 — and what a policy-design response would
contain. The user-framed stake: the succession crisis is compounded by the fact that the
transfer's destinations (investors, mega-farms, consolidation) are visible only to those
who do the parcel-by-parcel work.

## 1. Cell 1 — ownership-transparency infrastructure, current state

- **The federal public beneficial-ownership registry still does not exist.** ISED's
  live consultation page asks Canadians "on making beneficial ownership information
  more transparent by **establishing a public registry**" — the consultation itself is
  the evidence that none operates. The land scan recorded a Budget-2022 commitment
  (end-2023 delivery, later end-2025) as unverified; this pass records it as
  **not delivered as of 2026-11-18, in active consultation**. (The land scan's CBCA
  significant-control facts stand: since January 22, 2024, all CBCA corporations must
  hold individual-with-significant-control information — but it is not a public
  registry.)
- **Québec launched a beneficial ownership registry** (per the BC government's
  corporate-ownership-transparency page, last updated April 21, 2026) — a second
  provincial register beyond BC's LOTR, though for corporations via the enterprise
  register rather than for land specifically. **Unverified at primary-source level this
  pass**; flagged as the next check.
- **BC's LOTR remains the only public land-beneficial-ownership registry in Canada**
  (statute current to November 10, 2026 per BC Laws), and its enforcement opacity
  persists: reporting on the province being "tight-lipped on compliance with property
  ownership registry" questions (BIV, undated clipping surfaced this pass) suggests the
  registry's disclosure powers are exercised rarely — consistent with the land scan's
  zero-known-penalties finding.
- **Provincial land registries remain title-not-beneficial** ("Most provincial land
  registries are for legal title and not beneficial ownership", ICLG Real Estate 2026
  Canada chapter) and no province publishes open transaction-level price data (land
  scan finding unchanged).

**Cell 1 result: the land scan's "one provincial registry deep" holds, extended by
Québec's corporate registry and darkened by the federal registry's continued absence.
The public cannot see the transfer wave, two years on.**

## 2. Cell 2 — the open-analysis precedent: Saskatchewan

Johnson Shoyama Graduate School of Public Policy, **"Who Owns Saskatchewan's Farmland?
An updated analysis"** (Magnan, Qualman, Duncan, Desmarais; March 30, 2026) — curated
as `examples/records/jsgs-saskatchewan-farmland-brief.md`. It is the working proof of
concept for L6: a small team turned **public land-titles records** (complete ISC
records for all Saskatchewan rural municipalities, December 31, 2023 data; methodology
pioneered in 2014) into an open picture of the transfer wave:

- Large-scale private owners (10,000+ acres each, non-investor): **36 owners holding
  601,448 acres in 2018 → 84 owners holding 1.44 million acres in 2023** — 2.4% of all
  Saskatchewan farmland, **+140% in five years** (verified from the brief PDF).
- Investor ownership increased 2014–2018, then declined 2018–2023 ("seems to have
  stabilized"); CPPIB, which had reached 157,000+ acres, **completely divested
  Saskatchewan farmland by 2023, netting $390 million** (reported 2022) — this
  refines the land scan's CPP Investments entry, which recorded the retreat but not
  the completed divestment. Avenue Living is now the second-largest investor owner.
- "We found no evidence of non-Canadian ownership of farmland among investors or other
  large-scale owners" — a direct, evidence-based correction to the "foreign ownership"
  frame dominating the province's Fall 2025 legislature debates (which followed the
  Fall 2024 Auditor General report on residency-requirement verification).
- Mega-farm concentration is the actual growth story: Monette Farms (SK/AB/MB/BC/
  Arizona/ND), Primrose Livestock, Kambeitz Farms — with the brief noting that owned
  figures understate total land base, which "typically also includes rented land."

The brief's own layer is **free-to-read, unlicensed** (no copyright or licence line in
the PDF text layer — consistent with this program's C1/C3 finding, now extended to the
policy-analysis layer). Its method, however, is exactly what an open layer would
productize: titles data → named owners → open analysis. **L6's build object is this
method generalized and licensed** — a repeatable, open-licensed provincial
farmland-ownership analysis, of which the Saskatchewan brief is the unpublished-licence
prototype. It also answers the land scan's honest absence ("no public Canadian dataset
identifies investor/institutional farmland ownership nationally") with a method, not
just a lament.

## 3. Cell 3 — the tax-treatment layer, current state

All figures verified from primary sources: AAFC's Question Period Note
AAFC-2025-QP-00152 (December 11, 2025 — itself published on open.canada.ca) and the
Ontario Federation of Agriculture's income-tax fact sheet.

- **Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption: $1.25 million** for qualified farm or fishing
  property (raised from the indexed $1,016,836 for dispositions on or after June 25,
  2024, in Budget 2024; indexation paused for 2025, expected to resume 2026; per-person
  lifetime, shared across QFFP and QSBC shares).
- **Capital gains inclusion-rate increase: cancelled.** Proposed June 2024 (⅔ above
  $250,000 for individuals), deferred January 2025, never received Royal Assent, and
  cancelled by the Prime Minister on March 21, 2025; Budget 2025 confirmed the rate
  remains 50%. The OFA fact sheet records what the episode demonstrated: the proposal
  "caused significant concern for farm succession, especially for incorporated family
  farms" — the transfer wave is now a standing constraint on capital-gains policy.
- **Rollover and reserve provisions** (tax-deferred transfer of qualified property;
  five-year reserve on sale proceeds) — the core intergenerational mechanics. Bill
  C-208 (2021) ended the treatment of family-farm share transfers as dividends rather
  than capital gains.
- **The new friction is the Alternative Minimum Tax:** OFA's ongoing advocacy (with the
  Canadian Federation of Agriculture) presses for LCGE-eligible gains to be excluded
  from AMT calculations "so the LCGE functions as a true exemption rather than simply
  deferring tax through the AMT system," plus further LCGE increases and indexation
  that better reflects farm asset values — noting many modern Ontario farms now carry
  gains well above $1.25 million.
- **The policy answer routes farmers to the lender.** The AAFC QP note's own non-tax
  supports are "loan guarantees for farm transfers to beginning farmers under the
  Canadian Agricultural Loans Act program, and Farm Credit Canada offers free learning
  programs regarding succession planning and targeted loan products." The federal
  government's canonical answer to the succession question is, at the knowledge layer,
  the lender (verified — this closes the loop with cycle C1's lender-independence
  finding: the lender-dependence is not an accident of the market, it is stated
  federal policy).

**Cell 3 result: the tax architecture for family intergenerational transfer is
relatively favourable and just survived an existential scare (the inclusion-rate
episode), but it is family-shaped** — rollover and LCGE are built for parent-to-child
transfers. The non-family transfer (the majority case the demographic numbers imply)
has no equivalent shelter, and the policy layer that would serve it (open visibility of
who is actually buying; support vehicles for co-operative and community succession) is
where the program's design work sits.

## 4. What this changes about the frame

- **L6's build object is now a method, not a demand.** The Saskatchewan brief proves
  the pipeline works at provincial scale with public inputs. The design question for a
  `docs/policy/` document: what would it take to run that pipeline (a) in provinces
  with closed parcel data (Ontario's Teranet lock-up, per the land scan), (b) under an
  open licence so the analysis itself is forkable, and (c) sustained beyond one
  university team?
- **The tax layer is family-shaped, and the program should say so.** A design document
  can name the gap: intergenerational rollover/LCGE shelter parent-to-child transfer;
  nothing equivalent shelters transfer to entrants, employees, co-operatives, or
  community vehicles — the successors the demographic arithmetic actually requires.
- **The federal knowledge answer is the lender** — stated in the government's own
  open-published QP note. This is the policy-level statement of C1's finding and
  belongs in any design document's premise.
- **The beneficial-ownership fight is live, not settled:** ISED's public-registry
  consultation is an open policy window that a design document could address directly.
