## The question

What does it actually cost to build and run a northern community greenhouse, and what does the verified record imply per square metre and per kilogram of greens?

## Finding 1: the rung-B population exists, and the cost data is in the open literature — verified

Lamalice, Haillot et al. (2018), "Community gardens and greenhouses in northern Canada" (*Canadian Food Studies* 5(2), open access, read in full): a systematic inventory of **53 initiatives — 36 community gardens and 17 greenhouses** across Labrador, Nunavik, Nunavut, Yukon and the NWT, with 64% in communities under 1,000 residents. Breakdown: 36 initiatives in the NWT, 10 Yukon, 3 Nunavut, 2 Nunavik, 2 Labrador. This is the rung-B population the case's ladder describes: real, numerous, small-community, and almost entirely unmeasured in energy terms.

The three structurally important cases in the inventory:

- **Iqaluit Community Greenhouse** (1,000 ft², opened 2007): **annual operating costs estimated at $6,000, covered by donations and fundraising**; $75 seasonal memberships; switched to communal operation in 2014 "to maximize efficiency"; surplus distributed to the soup kitchen and shelters (Varga 2014, Nunatsiaq). The case's cleanest operating-cost anchor.
- **Inuvik Community Greenhouse** (4,000 ft², a 1999 hockey-arena conversion): 174 individual plots at **$50/year**, $25 membership plus 15 volunteer hours, Elders exempt; and — the architecture that matters — **a second-floor commercial greenhouse producing bedding plants and hydroponic produce whose sales offset operational costs**, on top of federal/territorial and corporate funding. A proven cost-recovery model: plot revenue + a commercial layer on the same building.
- **Kuujjuaq Greenhouse Project** (Nunavik): operating 20+ years (May–October), two facilities, lottery-distributed plots, funded by the Kativik Regional Government with health-board funding for education programs; composting grocery-store waste; home-garden and cold-frame instruction. Institutional longevity evidence: the community greenhouse form survives its founders when regional government funds it.

Context verified in the same paper: electricity at **74.9 ¢/kWh in Nunavik vs 11.8 ¢/kWh in Montréal** (citing CCA 2014 — independently consistent with QEC's posted rates); a healthy food basket in Nunavut costs **six times** the southern price; **$10/kg celery**; Nutrition North spent **$68 million in 2016**, including **$21 million to subsidize 7.4 million kg of fruits and vegetables**.

**Derived (labelled):** that last pair divides — **$21M ÷ 7.4M kg ≈ $2.84/kg of permanent public subsidy** on imported produce. This is the fiscal comparator the case has been missing: every kilogram grown locally does not just avoid the retail premium ($10/kg celery, food at 52% above the Quebec average in Nunavik per Herrmann et al. 2021), it avoids a subsidized import stream. A check-off or commons levy has an existing public-money benchmark to be compared against, and it is nearly three dollars a kilogram.

## Finding 2: Naujaat's capital cost is on the record — verified as reported figures

- **$164,000** for the geodesic dome itself (The Eyeopener, Ryerson, November 2015 — the year of construction)
- **More than $250,000 raised** in donations, sponsorships and in-kind gifts by June 2016, including paid local positions (CBC News, June 14, 2016)
- Operating design verified across the two articles: eight months solar-powered; **one to three hours of sunlight heats the dome 30°C above outside**; a wood-pellet combined-heat-and-power unit tested for the dark winter; subscription baskets planned "well below the cost of purchasing vegetables shipped from southern Canada"; hydroponic towers at 2,000+ plant capacity

**Derived (labelled):** a 42-ft dome is roughly 1,385 ft² ≈ 129 m², so the dome capital is ≈ **$1,270/m²** — and that figure excludes the volunteer construction labour and community-donated tools, meaning it is an upper bound on cash cost and an understatement of true cost. Both readings matter: the cash number is far below the $400K+ of a proprietary dairy robot installation; the labour number is the community's in-kind contribution, which is precisely the labour question (§5) wearing a hard hat.

Cross-check against the case's verified energy arithmetic: even a fraction of the 700–1,200 kWh/m²/yr cold-climate band (continuous-operation greenhouses) applied to 129 m², at 62.08 ¢/kWh, is $3.5K–$9.6K/yr — the same order as Iqaluit's entire $6,000/yr budget, which is why the design layer (thermal storage, solar geometry, seasonal operation) is not an optimization at rung B, it is the viability condition. The two passes' numbers now close against each other.

## Finding 3: the Allen cost paper is not openly readable — recorded

Allen, T. (2014), "Costs and benefits of a northern greenhouse," in Seefeldt & Helfferich (eds.), *Sustainable Agriculture and Food Security in the Circumpolar North*, MP 2014-16, pp. 58–74 (8th Circumpolar Agricultural Conference, Girdwood, Alaska). The proceedings PDF at uarctic.org (**16.9 MB, downloaded and text-extracted 2026-09-21**) contains the conference program listing the talk but **not the paper body**; the usask.ca URL in the Lamalice bibliography is dead (404). The full paper is print-proceedings-only as far as the open record shows. **Recorded as located-but-unreadable**; the Lamalice inventory plus the CBC/Eyeopener figures now cover the cost question without it.

Also located in the Piché bibliography, unread: AAFC (2013) *Understanding sustainable northern greenhouse technologies* tech. rep.; Holzman (2011) U of Guelph thesis on Nunavut community greenhouses; CCHRC (2017) *Biomass-Heated Greenhouses* manual (Alaska Energy Authority; PDF timed out on retrieval).

## What this file changes in the case

1. The cost spine's "community greenhouse capital" row upgrades from unverified to **verified-as-reported**: $164K dome / >$250K raised (Naujaat), with derived ≈$1,270/m² (upper-bound cash).
2. An operating anchor exists: **$6,000/yr (Iqaluit, 1,000 ft², volunteer-run, seasonal)** ≈ $65/m²/yr derived — with the caveat that this is a no-artificial-lighting, donation-covered operation, not a year-round CEA facility.
3. The case gains its fiscal comparator: **≈$2.84/kg of permanent Nutrition North subsidy** on imported produce (derived), against which any local production, and any commons levy, should be priced.
4. The cost-recovery architecture (Inuvik: plot fees + commercial layer) and the institutional-longevity evidence (Kuujjuaq: 20+ years, regional-government funded) give §6's funding question two working precedents instead of a hypothesis.
5. The remaining honest gaps sharpen to two: **no northern greenhouse has published kWh/kg** (the measurement gap), and **no open-licensed design files or control profiles exist** (the publication gap). Both are the case's own thesis, now with the cost data to make them concrete.
