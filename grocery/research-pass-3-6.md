## Target 3 — OFN governance, now well-grounded

OFN governance is now documented in detail from three sources: the "A new way of working" page, the "How do we take and document our decisions" Discourse thread (Rachel, Feb 2021), and the P2P Foundation wiki governance section (by Theresa Schumilas).

### 3a. How OFN describes itself

From "A new way of working" (openfoodnetwork.org/a-new-way-of-working):

- "Open Food Network is not only creating a new food system. We are also developing a new way of working as a global community, in a truly open and collaborative way."
- "The Open Food Network is a distributed collaborative organisation run by committed people all over the world. It blends aspects of sociocracy and holacracy with governance elements we have developed over time in response to specific challenges and tensions in those models. Deliberation is emphasised, so everyone who is interested to contribute to the shaping of a decision can do so, while agile and open-source software development concepts like lazy consensus are incorporated to make sure things get done!"
- "We take inspiration from permaculture, paying attention to the resilience and sustainability of the organisation. As such, we encourage everyone to develop their own leadership within the community, and we try to avoid over-dependence on any specific individuals, for instance by ensuring that every role can be done by at least 2 or 3 people, and that each person can fulfil at least 2 or 3 roles."
- "We have therefore documented the way we work and are happy to share with you our OFN handbook! Because Open Food Network is constantly improving the way it organises, this handbook is always open to revision, wiki-style."
- "Open Food Network members cooperate globally but organise locally through the local entities they create, called instances. These instances collaborate with one another around the development and maintenance of shared open-source resources to support the development of local, sustainable food systems. The relationships between these local entities are formalised via a mutual agreement document: our community pledge." (community pledge v2.0, May 2019, at community.openfoodnetwork.org/t/community-pledge-v2-0-may-2019/1666)
- "Open Food Network aims to be a living example of an alternative way of organising and working. We are constantly experimenting with new structures and forms of collaboration, and have been refining our current model ever since we set up the Open Food Foundation in 2012."

**Finding.** OFN's governance is a deliberate experiment in distributed, deliberative, lazy-consent, sociocracy/holacracy-blend, permaculture-inspired organizing, with formal local instances connected by a community pledge. This is the closest existing governance model to the concept's "open structure" aspiration — and it is a working, documented, experimental model, not a slogan. The handbook is wiki-style and open to revision.

### 3b. The decision process, concretely

From the "How do we take and document our decisions" Discourse thread (Rachel, Feb 2021), the community pledge describes the process for decisions affecting more than one affiliate or the broader community:

- "Someone opens a thread on [the community forum] and share about the situation, the tension, and the proposal he/she asks the community to decide upon."
- "The person who opens up the thread is in charge of facilitating it and asking specific people to contribute, or can ask someone to facilitate."
- "In particular, the facilitator needs to make sure the people/partners impacted by the decision are asked to contribute to the discussion."
- "If a consensus or a consent emerges from the discussion and no one objects, the decision is taken through 'lazy consent'. This means that we will assume agreement in the absence of any objection."
- "If no consent can be reached, then a vote can be called. In that case, every Affiliate has one voice, and a decision requires 2/3 of support to be adopted. [NB. Voices of other Members to be considered on a case by case basis e.g. depending on the issue]"

The thread then documents real difficulties OFN was encountering as it grew:

1. "we don't know who are the people impacted by the decision"
2. "we are afraid decisions are being made without us"
3. "we read discourse posts but at the end we don't know if a decision has been made" (and a related problem: "we have unresolved questions" — topics where only a few people participate and they never reach a conclusion)
4. "if a vote needs to happen between people who are not representing instances, how does it work? Who gets a voice, who gets more weight?"

Rachel proposed ideas (not necessarily adopted at that time): for decisions involving several circles, at least 2 people facilitate the proposal; introducing time frames and deadlines (e.g., no decisions involving several circles in less than 48 hours, or a week; a D / D+8 / D+16 model used in Open Food France & CoopCircuits where a post is shared, opinions start at D+8, decision at D+16, with a 2-week discussion window); always conclude something after the deadline (either extend, or document that no decision was made and why); create a list of handles to ping per circle.

**Finding.** OFN's decision process is documented in action, including its real friction points: impact-awareness, fear of decisions made without people, unclear decision-closure, unresolved questions, and the question of who gets a voice in votes. This is exactly the kind of structural-participation documentation the concept wants to learn from. The lazy-consent + 2/3 vote fallback is a concrete model. The three-level subsidiarity governance (below) is a concrete structure.

### 3c. Three-level subsidiarity governance

From the P2P Foundation wiki (wiki.p2pfoundation.net/Open_Food_Network), by Theresa Schumilas, the OFN project is organized "according to a principle of subsidiarity: mutualisation and related decisions are made at the most appropriate level according to the nature of the common." There are three "levels of common" governed by three communities:

- **Community of national/regional affiliates** (blue): governs jointly the OFN open-source software.
- **Community of food hubs of a country/region** (green): governs jointly the national/regional affiliate — the "cooperative" kind of entity which deploys and offers the OFN software in SaaS access.
- **Community of producers and buyers** (final eaters, chefs, etc.) (yellow): governs jointly the local food hub.

"This organization makes it possible to keep the governance of the resource 'in common' at the appropriate level, thus ensure the sovereignty of the users over the resource they depend on. It also enable to optimize the management and finance processes necessary for the preservation and the development of this resource."

**Finding.** This three-level subsidiarity model is a concrete existing structure for a federated food-system operation: a global commons layer (software), a regional/cooperative layer (SaaS deployment), and a local food-hub layer (producers and buyers). The concept's federation question — what does a node join, what does it agree to, what can it disagree about, what can it leave with — is informed by this: OFN already has a layered federation with a community pledge connecting instances, a subsidiarity principle for where decisions sit, and a commons ownership model. The concept is not inventing federation from scratch; it is synthesizing existing precedents.

### 3d. Structural transparency at the consumer level

From the P2P Foundation wiki (Schumilas): "The core defining feature is transparency. For example, the end consumers can see who grew their food, how their food was grown and how much the producers at the start of complex 'chains' were paid and how much 'mark up' was taken by the aggregator/hub/store." And from the Characteristics section: "It is transparent, relying on peer to peer traceability. Production methods are made transparent to consumers through a system of labels, and prices are also made transparent. So a consumer can see how much of their payment went to the producer, and how much covered other costs (e.g. transportation, packing, administration)."

**Finding.** This is the closest existing statement of sourcing-as-structural-transparency at the eater level in the scan — an operation where the eater can see who grew the food, how, and the economics of the chain (producer pay, markup). This is directly relevant to the concept's sourcing layer and transparency layer, and it's real in OFN, not aspirational. The concept's distinctiveness is not that OFN lacks this — it's that OFN does this as a platform feature for community food enterprises, not as a grocery operation with a continuous participatory information environment and a culture layer.

### 3e. The "meritocratic" / "benevolent dictatorship" tension

From the P2P Foundation wiki (Schumilas): "The global OFN community describes its governance system as 'meritocratic' and following the 'subsidiarity rule'. In this approach those who are best skilled to do a particular task take the leadership role, and each 'affiliate', service provider or platform user determines their own decision making process for the decisions made at the local level, and evolves their own self-governing processes based on the skills assembled." The US instance setup "might be described as a 'benevolent dictatorship,' where the community tried to keep hierarchy to a minimum, but temporarily used strong leadership to avoid project stagnation. Benevolent dictatorships are common in CBPP where participants try to manage the tensions between hierarchy versus equality, and authority versus autonomy." And: "The OFN community however, tries to resist the emergence of top down decision making... 'I plan to take a less proactive role for the time being... when people in the USA are ready to move... I am happy to offer the experience I have from other OFN countries, but I want to leave it to you to decide the momentum of this.'"

**Finding.** OFN's governance contains an acknowledged tension between meritocratic/subsidiarity leadership and the risk of benevolent dictatorship / top-down decision-making — a real structural-participation tension that the concept's structure layer should be aware of. Any federated operation has to name how it handles the tension between skilled-leadership subsidiarity and the risk of unaccountable leadership.

## Target 4 — DFC current state, now well-grounded and more substantial than the earlier pass suggested

### 4a. The DFC Standard has its own site and a major release

From dfc-standard.org:

- "The DFC Standard is a data interoperability standard working in the short food systems space that is intentionally designed to support regenerative, agroecological food systems. Our name derives from the original (French) project implementing our standard: the Data Food Consortium."
- "The DFC Standard is a standardized way to represent the data needed to supply, sell and deliver products through a short supply chain. Requests for and offers of products, customer and purchase orders, and data required by logistics providers to ship products, are all examples of data the DFC Standard supports. By standardizing the representation of this data, we facilitate the exchange of data between platforms and organizations without having to undertake custom development for each exchange of data. This simultaneously increases potential market size and reach for good food growers and producers, whilst reducing the associated administrative burden of working on a platform-by-platform basis."
- "Crucially, there is no one platform to rule them all – the DFC Standard instead fosters diversity at scale, ultimately empowering good food systems to achieve greater resilience in an ever-changing landscape."
- "The standard ensures the data is represented in specific ways that computers (and software engineers) can understand... Work is ongoing in Australia, Canada, France and the UK implementing the DFC Standard in a variety of contexts and integrating a diverse range of platforms."

A "DFC v2.0.0 Upgrade Guide" post (Garethe Hughes, February 19, 2026): "We have recently released our first major release of the DFC Standard. There are a significant number of changes in this major release version, this guide will give an overview of the changes and cover what's required to comply with V2.0.0 of the standard."

A "DFC and BECKN protocols – a comparison" post (Garethe Hughes, April 23, 2025): "The DFC Standard and the Beckn protocol both aim to enable interoperability in digital ecosystems, but they have different focuses, approaches, and application domains. Both are open-source initiatives that aim to reduce digital monopolies and create more equitable..."

A "What is the DFC Standard?" post (admin, November 13, 2024).

**Finding.** The DFC is not "rough around the edges and in active development" in the sense of being preliminary — it has a v2.0.0 major release (Feb 2026), its own documentation site, an ontology, taxonomies, a prototype, a connector tool, and named implementations in multiple countries. The earlier pass's characterization (from the 2021 Discourse thread and the "still rough" quote) was accurate for 2021 but understates the current state. The concept should treat the DFC Standard as a real, ongoing, multi-country interoperability standard at v2.0.0, not as a nascent experiment.

### 4b. The ontology, taxonomy, prototype, and connector tool

From fooddatacollaboration.org.uk/what-does-it-mean-to-implement/:

- The DFC has defined an ontology that provides "a general knowledge framework that maps the concepts used within Short Foodchain Systems in a machine readable format." Source of truth: github.com/datafoodconsortium/ontology/; published to docs.datafoodconsortium.org/ontology/; a NextOntology branch for pre-release/development versions; taxonomies (product type, units, etc.) in a separate repository (github.com/datafoodconsortium/taxonomies/); a ShowVOC tool at showvoc.dfc-standard.org.
- The DFC built a prototype that "facilitates product data testing for platforms and is currently the primary interface between the systems." "In the longer term it is intended that any member of the Data Commons will be able to directly push reads and changes (record create, update and delete statements), for the data they own and control, to all other platforms within the Commons, via a JSON-LD API that conforms to the DFC ontology." The prototype is published under an MIT licence; code at github.com/datafoodconsortium/prototype; current instance at proto.datafoodconsortium.org.
- The DFC developed a Connector Tool (github.com/datafoodconsortium/connector-codegen) using Acceleo to generate code libraries to facilitate implementation of the DFC Ontology as a model within applications — TypeScript, Ruby, PHP versions; designed to be implemented in other languages; "Development costs are anticipated at £1,500-£3,000, and to be borne by the associated project."
- FDC Platforms are currently implementing standard version v1.9.

**Finding.** The DFC is a real technical interoperability stack: ontology, taxonomy, prototype, JSON-LD API, connector code-generation tool in three languages, with implementations at v1.9 and a recent v2.0.0 major release. The "data they own and control" language echoes OFN Canada's data-sovereignty framing and the concept's access-vs-control distinction. The concept's interoperability layer is informed by a real, specific, existing standard — not a vague aspiration.

### 4c. The UK Food Data Collaboration pilots, concretely

From fooddatacollaboration.org.uk/what-does-it-mean-to-implement/:

Three Phase 1 pilots:

1. "The first pilot is with the Hodmedod's trade store, testing our advanced Orders functionality (beyond the scope outlined here) between Shopify storefronts... allows us to test our interoperability functionality against a single platform and provides value to our pilot users by automating updates to the product catalogue (including pricing) and placing of purchase orders/stock management."
2. "The second pilot involves linking a small producer using a Shopify store with an Open Food Network hub for integrated Ordering."
3. "Our third pilot will link a group of OFN Hubs with the Hodmedod's trade store to allow combined bulk orders to reduce costs to hubs whilst automating the complexity of managing group orders (including split cases)."

The implementation steps (Phase 1): (1) interface platforms with an Open ID Connect provider (Les Communs Keycloak server); (2) create and publish a CRUD API for Products data using the DFC JSON-LD standard; (3) exposure/consumption of Product data from other platforms in the shopfront.

FDC Commons governance requirements for partners: "We require partner organisations to align with the values outlined in our Vision and Mission" and "We also invite all platform partners to consider whether they'd be willing (and have capacity) to participate in our Governance circle." And: "FDC Commons Governance requires all platforms to have the ability to exclude individual users from the Commons (in cases of extreme or repeated breaches of Commons Rules). The FDC does not seek to control who can or cannot use individual platforms, however we have a duty to protect the integrity of the Commons."

**Finding.** The Food Data Collaboration is a funded, multi-pilot interoperability project in action, with named participants (Hodmedod's, small producers on Shopify, OFN hubs) and a governance layer that requires value alignment and a Commons Rules enforcement mechanism (the ability to exclude users who breach Commons Rules). This is a concrete existing governance/interoperability model for the concept's structure layer — including the question of how a federated commons enforces its rules without controlling who uses individual platforms.

## Target 5 — Meaning/culture, now substantially grounded (Indigenous food sovereignty)

This is the thinnest category from the earlier scan, and this pass fills it substantially — but only from the Indigenous food sovereignty angle. That is itself a finding: the concept's culture/meaning layer has a strong existing intellectual and movements base in Indigenous food sovereignty, and a weaker one in the broader "food as cultural substance" framing the concept wants.

### 5a. NAFSA six pillars — food sovereignty as systems, power, and cultural continuity

From nativefoodalliance.org/blog/6-pillars (Native American Food Sovereignty Alliance, NAFA), the six pillars are: Food Security, Food Access, Food Justice, Food Policy and Governance, Food Sovereignty, Land Access/Justice/Sovereignty.

Key framing: "Indigenous food sovereignty is about systems. It's about power. It is about who decides where and how food is harvested, gathered, hunted, fished, grown, prepared, distributed, and shared, and who benefits from those decisions." "We organize our work and advocacy around six connected areas... Each one rebuilds control, creates healing, and deepens the roots of Indigenous decision-making related to our foodways, economies, and ecosystems."

**Food Access (with the culture point):** "In Indigenous communities, access also means having a relationship with foods that hold cultural and historical meaning. These foods are not always available in stores or permitted under state regulation." This is the concept's culture/meaning layer in a sentence: access is not just proximity and affordability; it is relationship to foods that hold cultural and historical meaning, and those foods are often not available in stores or permitted under regulation. A grocery operation that wants to be legible as food to Indigenous eaters has to engage this, not just stock diverse products.

**Food Justice (with the seed rematriation point):** "A lesser-discussed but ongoing issue is the institutional control over Indigenous-origin seeds. Many of these seeds are stored in federal germplasm repositories, university seed banks, and private collections... bureaucratic or legal barriers when trying to access them... Restoring access to these seeds, referred to as rematriation, has become an area of increasing attention in food justice work... essential for restoring nutrition, biodiversity, and seed stewardship knowledge that was interrupted through policy and extraction." This is directly relevant to the concept's sourcing layer and the access-vs-control distinction: the same pattern (institutions holding seeds, with bureaucratic/legal barriers to access) that the concept's open-source-seeds reference touches is here framed as a food-justice and cultural-continuity issue, with rematriation as the response.

**Food Policy and Governance:** "Seed laws may restrict sharing. Food assistance programs may define nutrition in ways that ignore cultural diets. Funding structures often prioritize large-scale, non-Native models and require Tribal programs to conform to external formats... There is no standardized legal or policy framework that guarantees Indigenous communities the right to recover these ancestral seeds." This is a structure/economics finding: existing food-policy and funding structures are designed around large-scale, non-Native models and actively limit traditional food practices. An open source grocery operation has to operate inside this environment and may have to actively counter it.

**Food Sovereignty:** "Food sovereignty is about the right of Indigenous communities to make decisions about their own food systems and to define success on their own terms... Indigenous food sovereignty builds on this foundation but is distinct in its grounding in relationships, responsibilities, and place-based cultural knowledge. It emphasizes the restoration of food systems that are interconnected with language, ceremony, land, kinship, and community governance. It is not only about access or production, but about cultural continuity and decision-making authority, as scholar Charlotte Coté has written." "Key components of Indigenous food sovereignty include the protection of ancestral knowledge, the revitalization of traditional foods, the restoration of Indigenous decision-making, and the integration of cultural protocols into food systems." "Indigenous food sovereignty explicitly calls for the rematriation of seeds, the decolonization of food systems, and the return of inherent governance over land, water, and food. These aren't abstract ideals, they are lived responsibilities shaped by community consensus, cultural practices, and ancestral knowledge." Quote: "Our seeds are our relatives... they're living beings, so we need to treat them with the utmost respect" — Shiloh Maples (Anishinaabe seedkeeper).

**Land pillar:** "Land is the foundation of Indigenous food systems... This sixth pillar affirms that restoring land access and Indigenous land governance is essential to any food sovereignty effort... This pillar uplifts the vision that land is not a commodity — it is a relative. Indigenous food sovereignty cannot exist without right relationship to land, and right relationship requires consent, recognition, and the rematriation of land and governance." Examples: Sogorea Te' Land Trust (Lisjan Ohlone territory, urban rematriation, Shuumi Land Tax), Ekvn-Yefolecv (Maskoke ancestral territory, Alabama, ecovillage, traditional lifeways, language immersion, eco-cultural restoration), NEFOC Land Trust (BIPOC-led, Northeast), First Light (Wabanaki territory, Maine). Karuk Tribe eco-cultural restoration; Alaska Native marine mammal co-management; All Pueblo Grows seed lending library.

**Why the framework matters:** "These six areas are connected. Addressing one without the others will not lead to lasting change. Improving food access without shifting policy will not sustain progress. Supporting local food producers without addressing justice ignores the reasons access was lost in the first place. Indigenous food sovereignty is not a single outcome. It is an approach grounded in autonomy, accountability, and cultural continuity."

**Finding.** Indigenous food sovereignty is a fully developed framework for thinking about food as cultural substance, governance, and power — and it treats food access, food justice, food policy, food sovereignty, and land as interconnected. It is the strongest existing reference for the concept's culture/meaning layer and for the idea that a grocery operation has to be legible as food (not just stock diverse products) and has to engage the governance and policy environment rather than just the product selection. The concept should engage Indigenous food sovereignty seriously as a design reference, not as a values add-on. The seed rematriation point connects directly to the concept's open-source-seeds reference and the access-vs-control distinction.

### 5b. Canada-specific Indigenous food sovereignty (Mercille scoping review)

From PMC (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11639103/), Mercille (Université de Montréal): "Exploring Indigenous food sovereignty and food environments characteristics through food interventions in Canada: a scoping review."

Key points:

- "Heterogeneous, culturally diverse and representing 5% of the population, Indigenous peoples are Canada's first inhabitants. Over the past centuries, colonisation has profoundly disrupted Indigenous food systems, resulting in significant changes brought about by the nutrition transition and the loss of traditional food environments such as increased dependence on grocery stores and increased consumption of ultra-processed foods, resulting in higher rates of household food insecurity and diet-related chronic diseases for Indigenous Peoples compared to non-Indigenous Canadians."
- "In Canada, the concept of IFS is not new to Indigenous peoples... Indigenous nations must define food sovereignty for themselves, or it must be described rather than defined... IFS goes beyond food access and encompasses political, territorial and relational dimensions between humans, nature and the Creator."
- The review documents interventions that include grocery stores as a food environment type alongside hunting/fishing/picking, gardens, and community gardens — i.e., grocery stores are in the picture for Indigenous food sovereignty in Canada, and in some interventions they are a problematic environment (community stores that exclusively sell processed foods; dependence on store-bought food; deterioration of social relations) and in some they are a site of intervention (food preservation to reduce reliance on grocery stores; re-education/re-skilling of youth through programs supporting revitalization of traditional foods).
- The principle of participation: "Indigenous peoples work together in the community to support IFS initiatives." The principle that food is sacred: food as a gift from the Creator, a connector, a link to ancestors and the Creator; humans not more important than plants, fish, birds, animals; food gives its life to sustain humans and humans must give it respect and prayers; offering food to spirits; some traditional foods culturally inappropriate to sell. The principle of autonomy: "an assertion of autonomy for Indigenous peoples to make decisions about their entire food system in order to control the community health."

**Finding.** The Canadian Indigenous food sovereignty literature directly names grocery stores as a food environment that Indigenous food sovereignty engages with — often as a problematic environment (increased dependence on grocery stores, ultra-processed foods, community stores selling only processed foods) and sometimes as a site of intervention. This is directly relevant to the concept: a grocery operation in Canada that wants to be legible as food to Indigenous eaters and to carry cultural meaning has to engage this literature, which names the grocery store as a site where Indigenous food sovereignty is contested, not as a neutral retail environment. The "food is sacred" / "not culturally appropriate to sell" point is a direct challenge to the commodity frame the concept wants to move beyond.

### 5c. Systematic review of successful IFS interventions (US and Canada)

From ScienceDirect (sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2475299123247964), a 2024 open-access systematic review: "Elements of Successful Food Sovereignty Interventions within Indigenous Communities in the United States and Canada." Four categories of successful elements:

1. Transmission of knowledge and skills within the community through workshops.
2. Cultural connectedness through cultivation practices.
3. Preparation and consumption of traditional foods through community programs.
4. Community-based partnerships and collaborations.

**Finding.** This is a concrete, evidence-based framing of what "success" looks like in Indigenous food sovereignty interventions — and it is community-based, knowledge-transmission, cultivation, and traditional-food preparation/consumption centered, not retail-prototype centered. The concept's culture/meaning layer should be aware that the strongest existing evidence base for food-as-cultural-substance work is in community programs and partnerships, not in grocery operations — which is an argument for the concept to think about how a grocery operation connects to, rather than replaces, those community programs.

## Cross-cutting findings from this pass

### On NCG and the co-op sector's current state

The NCG figures are stable and corroborated across multiple public sources, and the sector is still growing modestly (4.7% in 2024, 6.2% in 2025 vs ~2.9-3% benchmark). The grocery.coop 2025 report page confirms the report series is ongoing. NCG's About page now says 169 co-ops, 241 stores, 40 states, $2.8B, 1.3M consumer-owners — slightly up from the 2024 report summary. The concept's viability framing should use these as current, corroborated figures, not treat the 2024 PDF's unreachability as a data gap.

### On OFN governance as the closest structure reference

OFN governance is now well-grounded as a deliberate, documented, experimental distributed-governance model (sociocracy/holacracy blend, deliberation, lazy consent, community pledge, three-level subsidiarity, per-ma-culture-inspired role redundancy). The decision process is documented in action, including its real friction points. The three-level subsidiarity model (global software commons, regional/cooperative SaaS layer, local food-hub layer) is a concrete existing structure for a federated food-system operation. OFN's structural transparency at the consumer level (who grew the food, how, and the economics of the chain) is the closest existing statement of the concept's sourcing/transparency layer at the eater level. The "meritocratic"/"benevolent dictatorship" tension is a real structural-participation tension the concept should be aware of. The concept's federation question is informed by existing precedents, not invented from scratch.

### On DFC as a real, ongoing, multi-country interoperability standard at v2.0.0

The DFC is not a nascent experiment; it has a v2.0.0 major release (Feb 2026), its own documentation site, an ontology, taxonomies, a prototype, a connector tool in three languages, and named implementations in Australia, Canada, France, and the UK. The Food Data Collaboration's three named UK pilots (Hodmedod's trade store with Shopify; small producer on Shopify with OFN hub; group of OFN hubs with Hodmedod's trade store for combined bulk orders) are concrete. The FDC Commons governance requires value alignment and a Commons Rules enforcement mechanism (ability to exclude users who breach Commons Rules) without controlling who uses individual platforms. The "data they own and control" language echoes the concept's access-vs-control distinction. The concept's interoperability layer is informed by a real, specific, existing standard.

### On meaning/culture — Indigenous food sovereignty as the strongest existing reference

Indigenous food sovereignty (NAFSA six pillars, Mercille Canadian scoping review, systematic review of successful IFS interventions) is a fully developed framework for food as cultural substance, governance, and power — and it treats food access, food justice, food policy, food sovereignty, and land as interconnected. It names grocery stores as a food environment that Indigenous food sovereignty engages with — often as a problematic environment (increased dependence on grocery stores, ultra-processed foods) and sometimes as a site of intervention. The seed rematriation point connects directly to the concept's open-source-seeds reference and the access-vs-control distinction. The "food is sacred" / "not culturally appropriate to sell" point is a direct challenge to the commodity frame. The strongest existing evidence base for food-as-cultural-substance work is in community programs and partnerships, not in grocery operations — an argument for the concept to think about how a grocery operation connects to, rather than replaces, those community programs.

### On Canadian co-op infrastructure

There is a real Canadian co-op directory infrastructure (CMC "Co-operation across Canada" map, ~7,500 co-ops, DEI tagging including Indigenous, procurement coding including food) and named Canadian food co-ops (Karma Co-op, Kootenay Co-op, East End Food Co-op, Ottawa Valley Food Co-op, Co-op Atlantic). The concept has a concrete place to go for a Canadian grocery-co-op survey, and the DEI tagging is directly relevant to the culture/meaning and food-justice layers.
