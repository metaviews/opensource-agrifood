# Open Source Grocery

A conceptual exploration of what a grocery operation might look like when it takes the internet era seriously.

## The premise

Contemporary grocery operations are media environments that owe more to television than to contemporary media.

They assume the consumer is passive, easily manipulated, and making decisions based on superficial marketing. The arrangement is broadcast-shaped: a small number of curated choices, presented with limited context, optimized for throughput and margin, with the shopper positioned as someone to be moved along rather than someone with questions worth answering.

The internet era offers a different possibility. An eater is an active participant — someone who can read, compare, ask, and comment. An internet-shaped grocery operation treats sourcing as something that can be made transparent rather than hidden behind branding and placement. It treats information as something that can be interactive — news, comments, provenance, alternatives — rather than something that arrives pre-packaged and one-directional.

An open source grocery operation employs the notion of *open* on multiple levels at once.

## What this is

This is a concept development space. It is not a research project (we are not verifying claims against sources) and it is not an example catalog (we are not documenting existing operations). It is an attempt to think through what a grocery operation would look like if it were designed from the internet era outward, with openness as a structural principle rather than a label.

The work here is speculative but grounded. It draws on the existing corpus — particularly the food-side frames in the Future Herd record, the structural omissions around retail concentration, and the consumer-behaviour frames that refuse the biochemical-reductionist model of food choice — but it does not treat those frames as a research base to be cited. It treats them as a vocabulary the concept can use.

## What "open" means here

Open is not a single switch. In the grocery context it operates at several levels, and the concept is stronger when those levels are held together rather than collapsed into one:

- **Open sourcing.** Where does the food come from, who grew or produced it, under what conditions, at what cost, and through what chain? Sourcing is treated as information that can be surfaced, not a trade secret to be protected.

- **Open information.** What do eaters know about what they are buying — not just nutrition labels and ingredient lists (the biochemical frame), but the social, economic, and ecological context that the existing record suggests actually shapes food choice? Comments, news, producer context, alternatives, and the ability to ask questions are part of the information environment, not decorations.

- **Culture.** Food is not only a material substance with a sourcing chain and an information environment. It is also a cultural substance — it carries identity, memory, tradition, community, ritual, and belonging. A grocery operation has to be legible as food, not only as a set of commodities with transparency labels attached. This includes the cultural contexts in which food is produced (Indigenous foodways, diaspora traditions, regional food cultures, family and community practices) and the cultural meanings food carries for the people who eat it. This level is the one the operation has to be most careful with, because culture is lived, specific, and often sensitive, and a grocery operation has to handle food cultures that are not its own without flattening or appropriating them.

- **Open participation.** The eater is not a passive recipient of a broadcast. They can contribute — questions, corrections, local knowledge, ratings grounded in experience, community knowledge about what is worth buying and why. The operation is a media environment that invites participation rather than managing a one-way presentation.

- **Open structure.** The operation itself is not a black box owned and operated by a single consolidated player. Its rules, its economics, its decision-making, and its relationship to the broader food system are things that can be understood and, where appropriate, shaped by the people who participate in it. This is where the concept meets the structural-omissions frame: concentration in retail (five chains controlling 80% of the market) is not a neutral starting point, and an open source grocery operation is, among other things, a counter-concentration proposition.

These levels are not a checklist. They are a way of keeping the concept from collapsing into "a grocery store with a website" or "a transparency label on conventional retail." The concept is strongest when the levels reinforce each other — when open sourcing makes open information meaningful, when open information invites open participation, and when open structure makes the whole thing more than a marketing layer on a closed operation.

## Where this sits in the project

The existing `opensource-agrifood/` project has three reference layers:

- `future-herd/` — the editorial and voice authority
- `agrifood-ai-field-guide/` — the structural reference for the agrifood domain
- `opensource-agrifood/` — the open-source context

The grocery concept draws on all three but is not subordinate to any of them. It is a distinct conceptual thread. It may eventually influence the event (the food side of the value chain is already part of the event's inherited frames), and it may eventually connect to a concrete initiative, but at this stage it is concept development: thinking through what the operation would be, not building it or documenting it.

## What lives here

- `concept.md` — the working concept document (this file)
- `levels.md` — a more detailed working-through of the multiple levels of open in the grocery context
- `tensions.md` — the unresolved questions and contradictions the concept has to carry rather than resolve
- `scenarios/` — concrete imagined situations that test the concept against real food-chain moments (a produce aisle, a meat counter, a pantry staple, a new product arriving, a supply problem, a price shock)

These are working documents. They are allowed to be rough, contradictory, and incomplete. The goal is to develop the concept, not to produce polished artifacts.
