# Grocery concept — Canadian grocery co-op survey

Date: 2026-08-28
Scope: A real inventory of Canadian grocery/food co-ops, their structures, and
their participation models — filling suggested-target #6 and grounding the
concept in the corpus's home jurisdiction.

This is a survey pass, not a verification pass. Sources are extracted from
directory sites, federation sites, co-op sites, and press. Where a figure is
single-sourced or a snapshot, it is flagged.

## Method

The CMC "Co-operation across Canada" directory (crm.canada.coop/en/map) was
identified as the intended primary source but could not be enumerated directly
through extraction (the directory is a filtered search application, not a
static listing). The survey was instead assembled from: the Grocery Story
Food Co-op Finder (Steinman) directory and its Canada notes, the Wikipedia
list of food cooperatives (Canada section, itself flagged "not exhaustive"),
the federation sites (FCL, Arctic Co-operatives, FCNQ, FCAQ), named co-op
sites (Karma, Fireweed, OVFC), and press coverage (Toronto Star, CBC,
Nunatsiaq, Food Secure Canada). This is a structured inventory of the
notable and federated operations, not an exhaustive enumeration of every
food co-op in Canada — the CMC directory enumeration remains a follow-up
(see "what remains thin").

## The shape of the Canadian sector

Canada's grocery co-op sector has two very different populations:

1. **Large federated retail systems** — mostly in Western Canada, the North,
   and Québec — where co-op grocery is a mass-market presence (hundreds of
   stores, millions of members), operating largely on a conventional retail
   model with co-op ownership and member payouts.
2. **Small community food co-ops** — independent single-store operations in
   cities and towns (Karma, Kootenay, East End, Fireweed, OVFC) — where the
   co-op is a values-driven alternative model, usually natural/organic/local
   focused, with a few hundred to a few thousand members.

The concept's five-layer "open" is not realized in either population, but the
small community co-ops are the closest Canadian analogue to the concept's
participation ambitions, and the federated systems are the counter-example
that shows co-op ownership alone does not produce openness.

## Population 1: the federated retail systems

### Federated Co-operatives Limited (FCL) / Co-op — Western Canada

- Co-operative federation providing procurement and distribution to member
  co-operatives in Western Canada, established 1944, headquartered Saskatoon.
- Owned by about 160 member co-operatives across the region — from large
  urban co-ops (Saskatoon Co-op) to small-town co-ops (Abernethy, SK).
- Revenue CA$11.695 billion (2025); net income CA$0.462 billion; 27,500
  employees (Wikipedia, flagged snapshot).
- Operates as "Co-op" retail banner: food, fuel, pharmacy, liquor, home
  centres. Grocery retail here is conventional-format supermarket retail
  under co-op ownership.
- In 2009 FCL was ranked the largest co-operative in Canada by total sales.

### Calgary Co-op — the defection case

- One of the largest retail co-operatives in North America (founded 1956),
  Calgary, Alberta. Groceries, fuel, pharmacy, liquor, cannabis.
- Formerly in the FCL system; began cutting ties in 2019 and since April
  2020 its groceries are supplied by the Pattison Food Group (owner of
  competitor Save-On-Foods), for "long-term sustainability." FCL's CEO
  called it "sad to see them moving away from the Co-op family."
- Significance for the concept: even a large, successful consumer co-op
  found the co-op supply chain insufficiently competitive and re-attached
  to a consolidated private supplier. Co-op ownership did not shield it
  from the concentration forces the concept is countering — it joined them.

### Arctic Co-operatives Limited — the North

- Supports more than 30 independent, member-owned co-ops across Nunavut,
  Northwest Territories, and Yukon (arctic-coop.com).
- Owned and controlled by Inuit, Dene, and Métis members; co-ops operate a
  wide range of businesses, with retail food stores central in most
  communities — in many Arctic communities the co-op IS the grocery store.
- Member stores are consumer co-operatives — "each member store is a source
  of democratic empowerment" (NCBA CLUSA, Due North, 2020).
- Significance for the concept: the strongest Canadian case of grocery
  retail as community-owned infrastructure where the alternative is no
  store at all. Also the case closest to Indigenous food sovereignty
  frames already in the research tree (meaning-and-culture.md) — though as
  retail operations, not food-sovereignty programs. Worth a dedicated
  engagement pass (see below).
- Pressures exist: Arviat's Padlei Co-op sold its retail store in 2022
  after 50+ years (Nunatsiaq, CBC); Food Secure Canada's 2024 op-ed notes
  Arctic Co-op member economics were "stable or declining."

### La Fédération des coopératives du Nouveau-Québec (FCNQ) — Nunavik

- Federation of co-operatives in Nunavik (Northern Québec), founded 1967;
  member co-ops operate retail stores (general merchandise, groceries,
  fuel) across the region (fcnq.ca). Named in the Grocery Story directory
  as the Nunavik counterpart to Arctic Co-operatives. Details not yet
  deep-read — flagged.

### FCAQ — the Québec food co-op federation (Population 1 and 2 bridge)

- La Fédération des coopératives d'alimentation du Québec (FCAQ,
  fcaq.coop) — "notre réseau regroupe aujourd'hui plus de 65 coopératives
  alimentaires à travers le Québec et le Nouveau-Brunswick" (LinkedIn:
  "plus de 70"). Operates under the "Manger coop" movement banner
  (manger.coop) and the Réseau COOP. Some member co-ops run "plus d'une
  centaine de points de vente" (Grenier: ~70 co-ops operating 100+ points
  of sale).
- This is the largest organized community-grocery co-op network in Canada
  and the closest thing to NCG in the Canadian context — a federation of
  food co-ops, not a wholesale conglomerate. NOT yet deep-read; its
  governance, member structure, and participation model are a high-value
  follow-up (see below).
- The FCAQ network connects to the participatory-supermarket movement:
  the 2023 VertigO study (Romdhani et al.) on Québec "supermarchés
  coopératifs et participatifs" (SCP) traces models inspired by Park Slope
  Food Coop and La Louve (Paris) — member-work grocery co-ops in Québec.

### Co-op Atlantic — the defunct federation

- Many food co-ops in the Atlantic provinces were once affiliated with
  Co-op Atlantic, now defunct (Grocery Story directory note). The
  federation's failure and what happened to its members is unexamined in
  this pass — a caution case worth a follow-up.

## Population 2: the community food co-ops

### Karma Co-op (Toronto, Ontario)

- Founded 1972; one of Canada's oldest food co-ops; Annex, Toronto.
- Member-owned, democratic, not-for-profit grocery store; "ethically
  sourced, fairly priced" food, waste reduction, local growers
  (karmacoop.org).
- ~500 working member households and ~389 non-working member households
  (LinkedIn, flagged snapshot) — i.e., a working-member model where
  members contribute labour, plus non-working tiers. BlogTO (2023):
  "owned by its 500+ members." Toronto Star: prices "10 to 15 per cent
  less" than chains, surviving 50 years.
- Governance: board of directors, president, committees members can
  freely join.
- Historical struggle with membership and finances attributed to being
  "a closed system" (Karma Chronicle, 2012) — the participation-versus-
  closure tension in its own words. An "open letter from the founding
  president" on the site discusses a predecessor co-op (started 1937,
  lasted to ~1986) in which "democratic governance became at best an..."
  (truncated — worth reading in full).
- Recent near-death: a 2022 Gleaner News piece titled "Life support"
  (Dec 2022) — the co-op has been through viability crises.
- Significance for the concept: the closest Canadian analogue to the
  concept's participation layer, with 50 years of real history including
  the participation-gap problems the co-op-sector.md deep dive
  documents. The strongest candidate for the single-co-op deep dive
  (target #7).

### Kootenay Co-op (Nelson, BC)

- Member-owned natural foods co-op, Nelson BC (Wikipedia list). One of
  the larger and more successful Canadian community co-ops (not
  quantified in this pass — flagged).

### East End Food Co-op (Vancouver, BC)

- Community-focused store, local and fairly traded products (Wikipedia
  list). Details not yet gathered.

### Fireweed Food Co-op (Winnipeg, Manitoba)

- Non-profit multi-stakeholder co-operative: producer members AND consumer
  ("supporter") members, "combining these two groups... creates an
  opportunity for more creative and collaborative problem-solving"
  (fireweedfoodcoop.ca).
- Membership tiers: Supporter $15/$35/$50 sliding-scale annual; Producer
  $60 annual. Benefits: vote at AGMs, run for board and committees,
  members-only newsletters, opportunity to join the Buying Club (supporters)
  or sell through the Food Hub and become a weekly vendor (producers).
  Financials and yearly reports on a members-only page.
- Runs a Food Hub with 30+ farmers and food producers, and the South
  Osborne Farmer's Market; also an online localfoodmarketplace storefront
  with ordering cycles.
- Sliding-scale membership and explicit "we are dedicated to making
  membership accessible" language.
- Significance for the concept: the clearest Canadian multi-stakeholder
  structure (eaters and producers as co-governors), and the most explicit
  Canadian instance of access-framing in membership design. Also an
  online-ordering co-op — a partial internet-era adaptation, though the
  information environment is a standard marketplace UI, not an open one.

### Ottawa Valley Food Co-operative (OVFC)

- Incorporated as a not-for-profit co-operative under Ontario's Co-operative
  Corporations Act, December 19, 2007.
- "Provides an internet-based ordering system to facilitate the buying and
  selling of local food" — an online co-op with no retail floor; ordering
  cycles and drop-off locations (localfoodmarketplace storefront).
- Customer membership: one-time lifetime fee of $50 applied to first order.
  Producers apply via questionnaire.
- Core values: healthy communities, environmental stewardship, social
  justice, vibrant local economies; supports food literacy.
- Significance: a Canadian co-op that was born internet-native (ordering
  system as the operation), but the internet layer is a purchasing
  pipeline, not an information/participation environment. Exactly the
  "data-level openness without operation-level openness" shape the
  research tree keeps finding.

### Bella Coola Co-op (Bella Coola, BC)

- Included in the Grocery Story directory with a note "Primarily
  conventional foods" — a small-town co-op grocery, the FCL-style model in
  miniature outside the FCL geography. Minor; listed for completeness.

### Quadra Island Pantry (Quadra Island, BC)

- Listed as a startup-stage food co-op in the Grocery Story directory —
  one of the small number of Canadian startups in their development
  pipeline. Worth watching as a live formation case (follow-up).

### Other named operations (thin, single-sourced)

- Muskoka North Good Food Co-op (Huntsville, ON) — named in a Facebook
  answer as a member-owned store with 1,700+ member-owners (unverified).
- Supermarchés coopératifs et participatifs (SCPs) in Québec — Park Slope/
  La Louve-inspired member-work grocery co-ops studied in VertigO 2023;
  named examples in the study (Breizhicoop is French; the Québec SCPs are
  the relevant cases) — not yet enumerated.
- Bizz Coop (Québec, 2020) — local food co-op formed via "reprise
  collective" (CDRQ) — not yet detailed.

## What the survey says for the concept

- **Co-op ownership is common in Canada; open structure is not.** Canada
  has mass co-op grocery (FCL system) that operates as conventional
  retail, and community co-ops that are participatory in governance but
  pre-internet in information environment. The five-layer open is not
  realized anywhere in the sector — consistent with the cross-cutting
  research finding.
- **The federation layer exists in Canada and is instructive in both
  directions**: FCL (wholesale federation at scale, now losing members to
  private suppliers) and FCAQ (food co-op federation, smaller, closer to
  the concept's values) are two different federation answers to the same
  problem — small co-ops that cannot achieve scale alone. The concept's
  open-structure layer would need a position on federation.
- **Participation has Canadian precedents with hard-won limits**: Karma's
  working-member model and its "closed system" struggle; Fireweed's
  sliding-scale multi-stakeholder design; the Québec SCP movement. The
  Canadian evidence supports co-op-sector.md's participation-gap finding
  while showing real design variety.
- **The North is the concept's most distinctive Canadian context**: Arctic
  Co-operatives and FCNQ are grocery-as-community-infrastructure owned by
  Indigenous members, in the corpus's home jurisdiction — the place where
  the concept's structure and culture layers meet existing Canadian
  practice most directly.
- **Internet-native Canadian co-ops exist but are purchasing pipelines**:
  OVFC (and Fireweed's marketplace) put ordering online without opening
  the information environment. No Canadian co-op found realizes the
  open-information or open-participation layers online.

## What this is for

This survey grounds the concept's home-jurisdiction claim: Canadian
grocery co-ops exist at both mass and community scale, with real
participation precedents and real failure modes, and none of them realize
the concept's multi-layer open. The concept's synthesis remains novel in
Canada as elsewhere. The survey also identifies the Canadian deep-dive
targets (Karma, FCAQ, Arctic Co-operatives) that would give the concept
concrete home-jurisdiction reference cases.
