# Grocery concept — meaning and culture research

Date: 2026-08-26
Scope: The meaning/culture layer of a grocery operation, grounded in Indigenous
food sovereignty.

This is a dedicated pass on the thinnest category from the environmental scan. It
is not a verification pass. Sources are extracted pages and follow-up search
results. Claims are grounded where the source supports them and flagged as thin
where they are not. Cross-cutting findings live in `research-mapping.md`.

---

## This pass is grounded in one frame

The concept's culture/meaning layer wants food treated as a cultural substance —
identity, memory, tradition, community, ritual, belonging — and wants a grocery
operation to be legible as food, not just stock diverse products. This pass fills
that in substantially, but only from the Indigenous food sovereignty angle. That
is itself a finding: the concept's culture/meaning layer has a strong existing
intellectual and movements base in Indigenous food sovereignty, and a weaker one
in the broader "food as cultural substance" framing the concept wants. A broader
pass is warranted.

---

## NAFSA six pillars — food sovereignty as systems, power, and cultural continuity

From nativefoodalliance.org/blog/6-pillars (Native American Food Sovereignty
Alliance, NAFA).

The six pillars are: Food Security, Food Access, Food Justice, Food Policy and
Governance, Food Sovereignty, Land Access/Justice/Sovereignty.

Key framing: "Indigenous food sovereignty is about systems. It's about power. It is
about who decides where and how food is harvested, gathered, hunted, fished, grown,
prepared, distributed, and shared, and who benefits from those decisions." "We
organize our work and advocacy around six connected areas... Each one rebuilds
control, creates healing, and deepens the roots of Indigenous decision-making related
to our foodways, economies, and ecosystems."

### Food Access (with the culture point)

"In Indigenous communities, access also means having a relationship with foods that
hold cultural and historical meaning. These foods are not always available in stores
or permitted under state regulation."

This is the concept's culture/meaning layer in a sentence: access is not just
proximity and affordability; it is relationship to foods that hold cultural and
historical meaning, and those foods are often not available in stores or permitted
under regulation. A grocery operation that wants to be legible as food to Indigenous
eaters has to engage this, not just stock diverse products.

### Food Justice (with the seed rematriation point)

"A lesser-discussed but ongoing issue is the institutional control over Indigenous-
origin seeds. Many of these seeds are stored in federal germplasm repositories,
university seed banks, and private collections... bureaucratic or legal barriers when
trying to access them... Restoring access to these seeds, referred to as rematriation,
has become an area of increasing attention in food justice work... essential for
restoring nutrition, biodiversity, and seed stewardship knowledge that was interrupted
through policy and extraction."

This is directly relevant to the concept's sourcing layer and the access-vs-control
distinction: the same pattern (institutions holding seeds, with bureaucratic/legal
barriers to access) that the concept's open-source-seeds reference touches is here
framed as a food-justice and cultural-continuity issue, with rematriation as the
response.

### Food Policy and Governance

"Seed laws may restrict sharing. Food assistance programs may define nutrition in ways
that ignore cultural diets. Funding structures often prioritize large-scale, non-Native
models and require Tribal programs to conform to external formats... There is no
standardized legal or policy framework that guarantees Indigenous communities the right
to recover these ancestral seeds."

This is a structure/economics finding: existing food-policy and funding structures are
designed around large-scale, non-Native models and actively limit traditional food
practices. An open source grocery operation has to operate inside this environment and
may have to actively counter it.

### Food Sovereignty

"Food sovereignty is about the right of Indigenous communities to make decisions about
their own food systems and to define success on their own terms... Indigenous food
sovereignty builds on this foundation but is distinct in its grounding in relationships,
responsibilities, and place-based cultural knowledge. It emphasizes the restoration of
food systems that are interconnected with language, ceremony, land, kinship, and
community governance. It is not only about access or production, but about cultural
continuity and decision-making authority, as scholar Charlotte Coté has written."

"Key components of Indigenous food sovereignty include the protection of ancestral
knowledge, the revitalization of traditional foods, the restoration of Indigenous
decision-making, and the integration of cultural protocols into food systems."

"Indigenous food sovereignty explicitly calls for the rematriation of seeds, the
decolonization of food systems, and the return of inherent governance over land, water,
and food. These aren't abstract ideals, they are lived responsibilities shaped by
community consensus, cultural practices, and ancestral knowledge."

Quote: "Our seeds are our relatives... they're living beings, so we need to treat them
with the utmost respect" — Shiloh Maples (Anishinaabe seedkeeper).

### Land pillar

"Land is the foundation of Indigenous food systems... This sixth pillar affirms that
restoring land access and Indigenous land governance is essential to any food
sovereignty effort... This pillar uplifts the vision that land is not a commodity — it
is a relative. Indigenous food sovereignty cannot exist without right relationship to
land, and right relationship requires consent, recognition, and the rematriation of
land and governance."

Examples: Sogorea Te' Land Trust (Lisjan Ohlone territory, urban rematriation, Shuumi
Land Tax), Ekvn-Yefolecv (Maskoke ancestral territory, Alabama, ecovillage, traditional
lifeways, language immersion, eco-cultural restoration, collective land governance), NEFOC
Land Trust (BIPOC-led, Northeast), First Light (Wabanaki territory, Maine). Karuk Tribe
eco-cultural restoration (salmon fisheries, Indigenous ecological calendars, cultural
burning, river stewardship); Alaska Native marine mammal co-management; All Pueblo Grows
seed lending library (via Pueblo Extension).

### Why the framework matters

"These six areas are connected. Addressing one without the others will not lead to
lasting change. Improving food access without shifting policy will not sustain progress.
Supporting local food producers without addressing justice ignores the reasons access was
lost in the first place. Indigenous food sovereignty is not a single outcome. It is an
approach grounded in autonomy, accountability, and cultural continuity."

**Finding for the concept.** Indigenous food sovereignty is a fully developed framework for
thinking about food as cultural substance, governance, and power — and it treats food
access, food justice, food policy, food sovereignty, and land as interconnected. It is the
strongest existing reference for the concept's culture/meaning layer and for the idea that a
grocery operation has to be legible as food (not just stock diverse products) and has to
engage the governance and policy environment rather than just the product selection. The
concept should engage Indigenous food sovereignty seriously as a design reference, not as a
values add-on. The seed rematriation point connects directly to the concept's open-source-
seeds reference and the access-vs-control distinction.

## Canada-specific: Mercille scoping review

From PMC (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11639103/), Mercille (Université de Montréal):
"Exploring Indigenous food sovereignty and food environments characteristics through food
interventions in Canada: a scoping review."

Key points:

- "Heterogeneous, culturally diverse and representing 5% of the population, Indigenous
  peoples are Canada's first inhabitants. Over the past centuries, colonisation has
  profoundly disrupted Indigenous food systems, resulting in significant changes brought
  about by the nutrition transition and the loss of traditional food environments such as
  increased dependence on grocery stores and increased consumption of ultra-processed foods,
  resulting in higher rates of household food insecurity and diet-related chronic diseases
  for Indigenous Peoples compared to non-Indigenous Canadians."
- "In Canada, the concept of IFS is not new to Indigenous peoples... Indigenous nations
  must define food sovereignty for themselves, or it must be described rather than defined...
  IFS goes beyond food access and encompasses political, territorial and relational
  dimensions between humans, nature and the Creator."
- The review documents interventions that include grocery stores as a food environment type
  alongside hunting/fishing/picking, gardens, and community gardens — i.e., grocery stores
  are in the picture for Indigenous food sovereignty in Canada, and in some interventions they
  are a problematic environment (community stores that exclusively sell processed foods;
  dependence on store-bought food; deterioration of social relations) and in some they are a
  site of intervention (food preservation to reduce reliance on grocery stores; re-
  education/re-skilling of youth through programs supporting revitalization of traditional
  foods).
- The principle of participation: "Indigenous peoples work together in the community to
  support IFS initiatives." The principle that food is sacred: food as a gift from the
  Creator, a connector, a link to ancestors and the Creator; humans not more important than
  plants, fish, birds, animals; food gives its life to sustain humans and humans must give it
  respect and prayers; offering food to spirits; some traditional foods culturally
  inappropriate to sell. The principle of autonomy: "an assertion of autonomy for Indigenous
  peoples to make decisions about their entire food system in order to control the community
  health."

**Finding for the concept.** The Canadian Indigenous food sovereignty literature directly names
grocery stores as a food environment that Indigenous food sovereignty engages with — often as a
problematic environment (increased dependence on grocery stores, ultra-processed foods, community
stores selling only processed foods) and sometimes as a site of intervention. This is directly
relevant to the concept: a grocery operation in Canada that wants to be legible as food to
Indigenous eaters and to carry cultural meaning has to engage this literature, which names the
grocery store as a site where Indigenous food sovereignty is contested, not as a neutral retail
environment. The "food is sacred" / "not culturally appropriate to sell" point is a direct
challenge to the commodity frame the concept wants to move beyond.

## Systematic review of successful IFS interventions (US and Canada)

From ScienceDirect (sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2475299123247964), a 2024 open-
access systematic review: "Elements of Successful Food Sovereignty Interventions within
Indigenous Communities in the United States and Canada."

Four categories of successful elements:

1. Transmission of knowledge and skills within the community through workshops.
2. Cultural connectedness through cultivation practices.
3. Preparation and consumption of traditional foods through community programs.
4. Community-based partnerships and collaborations.

**Finding for the concept.** This is a concrete, evidence-based framing of what "success" looks
like in Indigenous food sovereignty interventions — and it is community-based, knowledge-
transmission, cultivation, and traditional-food preparation/consumption centered, not retail-
prototype centered. The concept's culture/meaning layer should be aware that the strongest
existing evidence base for food-as-cultural-substance work is in community programs and
partnerships, not in grocery operations — which is an argument for the concept to think about how
a grocery operation connects to, rather than replaces, those community programs.

## Items thin or unverified in this pass

- This pass is grounded in Indigenous food sovereignty but is not grounded in broader "food as
  cultural substance" framings outside the Indigenous food sovereignty movement — e.g., diaspora
  food traditions, regional food cultures, family and community food practices as structural layers
  of a grocery operation. The concept's culture layer is still thinnest outside the Indigenous food
  sovereignty frame.
- The NAFSA six-pillars page is a single organizational blog post; the Mercille scoping review and
  the ScienceDirect systematic review are academic sources, which is good, but the concept has not
  engaged Indigenous food sovereignty organizations or practitioners directly.
- The concept's "engage Indigenous food sovereignty as a design reference" commitment is asserted
  here but not yet operationalized — what the concept actually takes from NAFSA's six pillars, the
  Mercille review, and the systematic review, and what it commits to in relation to Indigenous food
  sovereignty as a design constraint (not just a solidarity nod), is not yet worked through.
