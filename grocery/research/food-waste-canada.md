## K21. Canadian food-waste regulations and diversion programs

### The federal frame

- Canada committed to UN SDG 12.3 (halve per-capita food waste at retail and
  consumer levels by 2030). ECCC's "Taking stock: Reducing food loss and
  waste in Canada" (2019, still the reference federal document) documents
  the state of play: an estimated 20% (11 million tonnes) of food produced
  in Canada becomes avoidable food loss or waste (VCMI 2019); ~12% of
  avoidable FLW occurs at the retail stage; 5 major grocers command 80% of
  the market (the same concentration figure the concept's structural frame
  uses).
- There is NO nationwide mandatory organics diversion law in Canada
  (wastelogic.com and multiple sources agree). The federal role is
  measurement frameworks (ECCC/AAFC/Statistics Canada developing a data
  collection framework), the SDG commitment, methane-reduction strategy,
  and a Local Food Infrastructure Fund ($50M over five years, Budget 2019,
  for farmers' markets, food banks, community food projects).
- The food recovery hierarchy (reduce > recover/feed people > recycle >
  dispose) is the organizing frame at every level of Canadian policy. ECCC
  explicitly notes recovery "is not proposed as a solution to food
  insecurity" — the same dignity/dual-purpose caveat in the first-pass
  scan.

### Provincial regulation — the real regulatory layer

Canada's food-waste regulation is provincial and municipal, and it is
asymmetric:

- **Nova Scotia** — first in Canada: banned landfill disposal of all
  compostable organics in 1997 (effective November 30, 1998); leaf/yard
  waste banned 1996. Nearly three decades of operation; grocery stores and
  institutions operate under diversion requirements.
- **Ontario** — the most detailed IC&I framework. The Food and Organic
  Waste Policy Statement (April 30, 2018, under the Resource Recovery and
  Circular Economy Act, 2016) sets sector-specific targets: industrial and
  commercial facilities (including retail shopping establishments) at 70%
  (policy 4.14) or 50% (4.15) waste reduction and resource recovery by
  2025; large generators (300+ kg food waste/week) must source-separate
  organics, and retail establishments generating 300+ kg/week "should
  identify where food waste occurs... conduct regular food waste audits to
  quantify the amount and type" and take reduction measures (policy 3.4).
  The policy also directs imperfect-produce availability (3.5) and surplus
  food rescue. Enforcement is via policy-statement direction rather than
  hard penalty regimes; a second source (powerknot, secondary) describes a
  January 1, 2025 enforcement date for an organics landfill ban in Ontario
  — flagged, needs primary confirmation.
- **British Columbia** — no single provincial ban; regional/municipal
  organics disposal bans (Metro Vancouver since 2015; Vancouver's 50%
  surcharge on contaminated waste). BC's Climate Leadership Plan set a
  30% food-waste prevention target by 2050. BC Ministry developed the
  Retail Food Waste Prevention Toolkit — one of the few government
  resources aimed directly at food retailers.
- **Québec** — Politique bioalimentaire 2018-2025 commits to reducing
  waste/food losses and promoting donation; Recyc-Québec action plans
  target household and industry organics; the Residual Materials
  Management Policy frames organics diversion. Québec also repealed its
  Fresh Fruit and Vegetable Regulations (2016) which had prohibited sale
  of below-aesthetic-standard produce — a deregulation-for-waste-reduction
  move unique in Canada.
- Regional District of Nanaimo (BC) requires grocery stores, school and
  hospital cafeterias to have food-waste diversion systems (RDN Organics
  Diversion Strategy) — an example of grocery-specific obligations at the
  regional level.

### Tax and liability infrastructure for donation

- Four provinces provide farm-donation tax credits: British Columbia,
  Ontario, Québec, Nova Scotia (ECCC Taking stock; Québec's extends to
  processors).
- Every province and territory has liability protection for food donors
  (provincial/territorial legislation) — the Canadian equivalent of the US
  Bill Emerson Act, in ten-plus pieces rather than one.
- Safety requirements for donated food are provincial; guidance exists
  (BC CDC Industry Food Donation Guidelines; Québec MAPAQ best practices;
  National Zero Waste Council Guidelines for Food Donations aggregating
  the regulatory landscape).

### The diversion ecosystem, Canada-specific

- **Flashfood** — the Canadian-founded discount app, now live in 900+
  Loblaw stores; Loblaw reports 21M+ pounds diverted in 2025 (Loblaw's own
  figure — self-reported, flagged). This is the largest
  retailer-integrated discount-diversion operation in Canada and it is
  proprietary: the data (what was discounted, what sold, what still went
  to waste) is not public.
- **Second Harvest** — the largest food rescue organization; FoodRescue.ca
  platform (Ontario) matching businesses to charities; with Food Banks
  Canada rescued ~10 million kg of food in 2018 (ECCC).
- **Food Banks Canada / Banques alimentaires Québec / Moisson Montréal**
  retail recovery programs with Loblaw, Longo's, Sobeys, Metro, Walmart —
  refrigerated infrastructure partnerships with the consolidated chains.
- **Loop** (juices/beer/dog treats from surplus), **Goodly Foods** (GVFB
  social enterprise soups/stews/sauces) — upcycling examples.
- Community fridges (Sauve ta bouffe directory, Québec) — the
  informal/participatory end.

### The measurement layer — a significant 2026 development

- **CSA K100:26, "Food loss and waste — terminology and measurement"** —
  published April 2026 (CSA Group announcement May 12, 2026), a
  first-of-its-kind NATIONAL standard creating common terminology and
  measurement guidance across the value chain (farms, processors,
  grocery, food service). Motivation per CSA technical committee vice
  chair Lori Nikkel (Western Producer, Aug 27, 2026): "Every part of the
  chain has been measuring different things, calling them different names
  and reporting in different units... Until everyone speaks the same
  language, we cannot compare set baselines or measure progress."
  Structure: define, measure, reduce. Food waste accounts for $58 billion
  in annual losses in Canada (CSA figure).
- K100 is non-binding but "80% of CSA standards end up referenced in
  regulation or by industry" (Nikkel), and CSA is advocating government
  adoption. It aligns with (and Canada-localizes) the FLW Protocol.
- Significance for the concept: a national, defensible,
  Canada-specific measurement language now exists. An open grocery
  operation could measure and publish its waste and diversion in
  K100-aligned form — domestically legible in a way FLW-alone would not
  be. This materially strengthens the food-waste.md finding that
  FLW-aligned public reporting would be "unusual and credible": it is now
  also nationally standard-backed.

### Findings for the concept (K21)

- The Canadian regulatory patchwork (NS ban, Ontario IC&I targets and
  audit expectations, BC regional bans, Québec policy) creates real
  compliance pull toward measurement — but the measurement results are
  private. The concept's legibility move (publish the audits and
  destinations) would convert a compliance cost into public value.
- CSA K100 gives the concept a Canada-specific, defensible reporting
  language for its waste-and-diversion surface. This is the concrete
  Canadian anchor the first pass said was missing ("Flashfood is the
  hint").
- The donation infrastructure (Second Harvest, FoodRescue.ca, Food Banks
  Canada retail programs) is mature but platform-fragmented and
  chain-proprietary; the fragmentation caution from food-waste.md holds
  in Canada in a specific form: the dominant diversion relationships run
  through the five concentrated chains the concept is countering.

## K22. Foodsharing.de in detail

### What it is

- German platform (foodsharing.de) for saving and distributing surplus
  food, managed by the association foodsharing e.V.; founded December 12,
  2012 (idea 2011, from the film Taste the Waste's campaign team); free,
  volunteer-run, ad-free. Also active in Austria, Switzerland, and other
  European countries; 500,000+ users across Europe (foodsharing.de,
  2026); Wikipedia snapshot (March 2020): 316,621 "foodsharer" users and
  71,797 volunteer "foodsavers" in Germany/Austria.
- Scale claims (foodsharing.de/statistik, self-reported): 334 million kg
  of food saved from the bin (cumulative); 18,354 businesses cooperating
  continuously.
- Structure: three mechanisms — (1) **Fairteiler**: community shelves/
  fridges where anyone can drop off or take food freely; (2)
  **Essenskörbe** (food baskets): individuals share their own surplus;
  (3) **business cooperation**: registered businesses (supermarkets,
  restaurants, canteens) post surplus, and trained volunteer foodsavers
  collect and redistribute. Communities are organized in Bezirke
  (districts).
- **foodsharing Städte** (cities): a formal partnership status when a
  local team exists AND the city administration signs a motivation
  declaration — institutional recognition of the movement by municipal
  government (foodsharing-staedte.org).

### The open source question — answered

- The code IS open: "Die Website und Beta-App ist open source"
  (foodsharing.de, own claim); Wikipedia lists content license MIT;
  source code published 2018-2019. The development stack (Vue.js
  frontend, Symfony/PHP backend, Bootstrap) is documented in public
  devdocs (devdocs.foodsharing.network) with contribution guides. This
  confirms the first-pass scan's conditional ("whether the code is
  actually open") — yes.
- Governance: volunteer-run, legally an eingetragener Verein (registered
  association), funded by donations and sponsorship (they note some
  costs — legal advice, insurance, events — are not fully sponsored).
  Foodsavers require training/approval (a QA gate on the business-
  collection role); Fairteiler use needs no gate.

### Legal and cultural context

- German/European legal context: foodsavers collect under association
  arrangements with businesses (liability handled between association
  and business; Germany's law is generally permissive for food
  donation); the movement frames itself as an environmental- and
  education-policy movement, not a charity — "Wertschätzung" (valuing
  food) is the core vocabulary.
- Cultural fit: the first-pass scan asked about German context; the
  model depends on dense urban volunteer networks, a strong
  associational culture (Verein), and municipal willingness to host
  Fairteiler. Transferability to Canada is untested — no Canadian
  Foodsharing-equivalent with business integration was found; the
  closest Canadian analogues are community fridges (small, informal) and
  Second Harvest's FoodRescue.ca (charity-mediated, not peer-to-peer).

### Findings for the concept (K22)

- Foodsharing.de is the proof that an open-source-coordinated,
  participatory waste-diversion layer can operate at national scale with
  real business integration — but it is NOT a grocery operation; it is a
  movement-platform that sits alongside retail. Its QA gate (trained
  foodsavers) and its city-partnership instrument are directly
  transferable design ideas for the concept's participation layer: a
  community-governed diversion role with earned trust, and formal
  municipal partnership as a structure-level achievement.
- Its statistics (334M kg saved; 18,354 businesses) are self-reported
  and unaudited — a contrast with PCFWC's audited, anonymized,
  market-share-gated reporting (K23). The concept's waste surface would
  want Foodsharing's openness with PCFWC's measurement discipline.
- The Canada gap: no peer-to-peer business-integrated foodsharing
  operation exists in Canada. The concept could be the first — but the
  German associational culture that sustains it is not present by
  default; the multi-stakeholder co-op structures (Fireweed etc.) are
  the closest Canadian host form.

## K23. PCFWC methodology and dataset in detail

### What it is

- The Pacific Coast Food Waste Commitment (PCFWC, from the Pacific Coast
  Collaborative, 2016): a public-private partnership of West Coast US
  jurisdictions, food businesses, and nonprofits targeting 50% food
  waste reduction by 2030. Its successor/sibling, the U.S. Food Waste
  Pact (ReFED + WWF), extends the model nationally. "Target, Measure,
  Act" framework.
- The dataset: retail signatories have measured and reported food waste
  annually since 2019 — "the longest year-over-year aggregated
  [retail food waste] dataset in the country," plus first-ever national
  retail and foodservice datasets (2024 report, using 2023 data).

### Methodology (from the 2024 Year-End Data Report)

- Data collection is centralized through **ReFED sector-based
  Calculators** with measurement guidelines, technical support, and data
  review for accuracy/completeness/gaps. Data are anonymized and
  aggregated; publication is gated on strict market-share and
  contributing-entities criteria (so no single company is identifiable).
- Core metric: the **unsold food rate** — share of inventory retailers
  fail to sell, by grocery department, including edible food AND
  inedible scraps. Chosen over tonnage because it reflects waste
  practices rather than market fluctuations.
- The **Unknown Destination Rate**: proportion of unsold food whose
  final destination cannot be accounted for in a retailer's tracking.
  ReFED's model assumes unknown = landfill (so emissions may be
  overestimated). The report names this as a critical metric: a high
  unknown rate means actual donation/waste amounts are likely much
  higher than reported; a low rate indicates better tracking.
  Destinations tracked include donation, compost, anaerobic digestion,
  animal feed, landfill, unknown.
- Headline results: unsold food rates down 30% for reporting retailers
  2019→2023 (PCFWC regional); PCFWC 2023 unsold rate 3.12% (587,596
  tons; $3.92B lost sales; 23 lbs per capita); Pact national 2023: 4.1%;
  Produce is the largest unsold tonnage source (38.9%); Prepared Foods
  the fastest-growing and highest-value-per-pound (33% of tonnage
  increase, 23.1% of unsold retail value).
- Methodological honesty in the report itself: early-year changes may
  reflect improving measurement practice as much as actual reduction.

### Findings for the concept (K23)

- This is the model for what a comparable measurement and reporting
  layer looks like: department-level granularity, destination tracking
  with an explicit unknown-destination metric, anonymization gates,
  pre-competitive data pooling. The concept's open operation could
  adopt the metric structure (unsold rate by department, destinations
  including unknown) while INVERTING the transparency model: PCFWC
  anonymizes and gates publication; an open grocery operation would
  publish its own full detail — its identity is not a secret to
  protect. The unknown-destination rate in particular is a metric an
  open operation could drive to zero and show — a legibility claim
  no chain makes.
- The methodological honesty (measurement improvement vs. real change)
  is a caution the concept should import: its published waste numbers
  should carry the same caveat.
- No Canadian equivalent of PCFWC/Pact pre-competitive retail reporting
  exists. CSA K100 (K21) provides the language; a Canadian open grocer
  reporting in K100 terms would be first.

## K24. Culturally appropriate food in the waste-to-food-bank connection

- The Canadian food-security frame now explicitly includes cultural
  appropriateness: Canada's National Pathways document (agriculture
 .canada.ca) names "culturally appropriate foods, teaching traditional
  food-ways" in the context of Indigenous food insecurity; Nutrition
  North Canada's programs include "culturally appropriate retail and
  community-based nutrition education" in northern communities; HRW
  (2020) recommends First Nations children receive "traditional or
  other culturally appropriate food" through programs.
- Food-bank sector practice: Food Banks Canada describes its work as
  respectful of Indigenous rights and pursuing "culturally-appropriate
  and impactful solutions"; Second Harvest and partners emphasize
  "timely, culturally appropriate food with dignity." The first-pass
  scan's finding — the food-bank turn toward culturally specific foods
  as institutional practice — is confirmed at the national-organization
  level.
- The structural tension (from meaning-and-culture.md, carried forward):
  culturally appropriate donation is framed within the charity/
  redistribution model, while Indigenous food sovereignty frames
  (NAFSA pillars; the sacred/not-for-sale point) locate the problem in
  the retail-commodity system itself. A grocery operation that makes
  its waste stream legible and participatory will meet this boundary:
  some surplus food is appropriate for redistribution; some foods and
  food knowledge are not commodities at any stage of their lifecycle,
  including at end-of-life. CARE/FAIR (governance of Indigenous data/
  knowledge before its opening) applies to waste-diversion data too:
  where food came from and to whom it is redistributed can itself be
  culturally governed information (e.g., community harvest sharing is
  not "donation").
- The Northern dimension: Arctic Co-operatives' member stores (the
  North's grocery infrastructure, canadian-co-op-survey.md) operate in
  the same communities Nutrition North serves; waste and diversion in
  remote northern grocery has materially different economics (shipping
  in, little diversion infrastructure, high cost of waste) — flagged as
  unexplored; no Canadian source found addressing northern grocery
  waste diversion specifically.

### Findings for the concept (K24)

- The concept's waste-and-diversion layer needs a cultural-appropriateness
  gate, not just a donation pipeline: what may be redistributed, to
  whom, under whose governance. The national policy language now
  supports this; the sovereignty frame demands it go further than
  charity practice.
- Diversion data is also governed data: publishing where surplus went
  may need community consent in Indigenous contexts (CARE applied to
  operational data). This is a design constraint the first pass did not
  see.

## What this is for

This second pass grounds the concept's waste-and-diversion layer in the
Canadian regulatory reality (provincial patchwork, no federal mandate,
new national measurement standard), proves the open-source coordination
model works at scale (Foodsharing.de — code open, movement-governed,
city-partnered), gives the concrete measurement/reporting template
(PCFWC: department granularity, destination tracking, unknown-destination
metric) the concept would invert into full openness, and names the
cultural-governance constraint (CARE, not-for-sale) that diversion
design must carry. The waste layer is now the concept's most concretely
specified surface: measurable in a national standard, unprecedented if
published openly, and bounded by cultural governance.
