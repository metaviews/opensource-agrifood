# Grocery concept — Open Food Network and Data Food Consortium research

Date: 2026-08-26
Scope: OFN and the DFC as the closest existing test of the concept's
federation/interoperability layer.

This is a deeper dive, not a verification pass. Sources are extracted pages
and follow-up search results. Claims are grounded where the source supports
them and flagged as thin where they are not. Cross-cutting findings live in
`research-mapping.md`.

## Open Food Network — what it is, concretely

- **Identity.** OFN is "an international not-for-profit focused on creating the
  tools and resources needed to build food systems that are fair, local, and
  transparent." Its flagship project is "an open source software platform that makes
  it easy to create innovative, independent, community food enterprises."
  (openfoodnetwork.org/about-us)
- **Open source and Commons.** "Open Food Network operates on an open and transparent
  governance model. This is why we create open source solutions, communicate with
  transparency, and hold everything we create as part of the Commons — it doesn't
  belong to a single entity, but instead to a community."
  (openfoodnetwork.org/about-us)
- **Global and locally-led.** "Open Food Network was founded in 2012 in Australia but
  is now an international community that is locally-led across the world."
  (openfoodnetwork.org/about-us) OFN Canada launched in 2017 and is most active in
  Ontario. (openfoodnetwork.org/find-your-local-open-food-network)
- **Scale (one snapshot).** Openfoodnetwork.net shows a running count: 1,039 food
  producers, 363 food shops, 7,693 food shoppers, 115,728 food orders; described as
  "a free and open source software platform." This is a live public dashboard figure —
  useful as a scale indicator, but it is a snapshot and should not be treated as a
  verified financial or governance statement.
- **Code.** OFN is on GitHub at github.com/openfoodfoundation/openfoodnetwork. The
  README says: "Supported by the Open Food Foundation and a network of global
  affiliates, we are proudly open source and not-for-profit - we're trying to
  seriously disrupt the concentration of power in global agri-food systems..."
  (github.com/openfoodfoundation/openfoodnetwork) This is a direct statement of the
  concentration-disruption intent.
- **Business model.** "Our code is Open Source and freely available to anyone to
  install, adapt and add to, but it does cost money to pay for servers and to
  compensate developers." (about.openfoodnetwork.net, OFNUSA pricing page) The
  LocallyGrown comparison piece: "If open source governance, community ownership, and
  platform independence are core values that outweigh the extra work required, OFN
  aligns with your mission." (locallygrown.net/why-not-open-source) — explicit
  statement that the open-source + community-ownership + platform-independence set of
  values is optional and costly.
- **What the platform does.** OFN's software platform page describes enabling "new,
  ethical supply chains." The platform is an ecommerce/order-management tool for
  community food enterprises — food hubs, online farmers' markets, co-ops, independent
  producers. (openfoodnetwork.org/software-platform)

**Finding for the concept.** OFN is the closest existing thing to the concept's stack
in one body: an open-source platform for community food commerce, with a Commons
ownership model, an explicit concentration-disruption intent, a global but locally-led
community, and real scale. It is not, however, a grocery operation in the concept's
sense — it is a platform that community food enterprises use. The concept wants to move
from platform to operation, and from platform interoperability to operational
federation. OFN is the best available reference point for the platform and community
parts; it does not yet embody the sourcing-transparency-as-structural, participation-as-
continuous-environment, or culture layers in the way the concept imagines.

## OFN governance — now well-grounded

OFN governance is documented in detail from three sources: the "A new way of working"
page, the "How do we take and document our decisions" Discourse thread (Rachel, Feb
2021), and the P2P Foundation wiki governance section (by Theresa Schumilas).

### How OFN describes itself

From "A new way of working" (openfoodnetwork.org/a-new-way-of-working):

- "Open Food Network is not only creating a new food system. We are also developing a
  new way of working as a global community, in a truly open and collaborative way."
- "The Open Food Network is a distributed collaborative organisation run by committed
  people all over the world. It blends aspects of sociocracy and holacracy with
  governance elements we have developed over time in response to specific challenges and
  tensions in those models. Deliberation is emphasised, so everyone who is interested to
  contribute to the shaping of a decision can do so, while agile and open-source software
  development concepts like lazy consensus are incorporated to make sure things get done!"
- "We take inspiration from permaculture, paying attention to the resilience and
  sustainability of the organisation. As such, we encourage everyone to develop their own
  leadership within the community, and we try to avoid over-dependence on any specific
  individuals, for instance by ensuring that every role can be done by at least 2 or 3
  people, and that each person can fulfil at least 2 or 3 roles."
- "We have therefore documented the way we work and are happy to share with you our OFN
  handbook! Because Open Food Network is constantly improving the way it organises, this
  handbook is always open to revision, wiki-style."
- "Open Food Network members cooperate globally but organise locally through the local
  entities they create, called instances. These instances collaborate with one another
  around the development and maintenance of shared open-source resources to support the
  development of local, sustainable food systems. The relationships between these local
  entities are formalised via a mutual agreement document: our community pledge."
  (community pledge v2.0, May 2019, at community.openfoodnetwork.org/t/community-pledge-
  v2-0-may-2019/1666)
- "Open Food Network aims to be a living example of an alternative way of organising and
  working. We are constantly experimenting with new structures and forms of collaboration,
  and have been refining our current model ever since we set up the Open Food Foundation
  in 2012."

**Finding.** OFN's governance is a deliberate experiment in distributed, deliberative,
lazy-consent, sociocracy/holacracy-blend, permaculture-inspired organizing, with formal
local instances connected by a community pledge. This is the closest existing governance
model to the concept's "open structure" aspiration — and it is a working, documented,
experimental model, not a slogan. The handbook is wiki-style and open to revision.

### The decision process, concretely

From the "How do we take and document our decisions" Discourse thread (Rachel, Feb 2021),
the community pledge describes the process for decisions affecting more than one affiliate
or the broader community:

- "Someone opens a thread on [the community forum] and share about the situation, the
  tension, and the proposal he/she asks the community to decide upon."
- "The person who opens up the thread is in charge of facilitating it and asking specific
  people to contribute, or can ask someone to facilitate."
- "In particular, the facilitator needs to make sure the people/partners impacted by the
  decision are asked to contribute to the discussion."
- "If a consensus or a consent emerges from the discussion and no one objects, the decision
  is taken through 'lazy consent'. This means that we will assume agreement in the absence
  of any objection."
- "If no consent can be reached, then a vote can be called. In that case, every Affiliate
  has one voice, and a decision requires 2/3 of support to be adopted. [NB. Voices of other
  Members to be considered on a case by case basis e.g. depending on the issue]"

The thread then documents real difficulties OFN was encountering as it grew:

1. "we don't know who are the people impacted by the decision"
2. "we are afraid decisions are being made without us"
3. "we read discourse posts but at the end we don't know if a decision has been made"
   (and a related problem: "we have unresolved questions" — topics where only a few
   people participate and they never reach a conclusion)
4. "if a vote needs to happen between people who are not representing instances, how does
   it work? Who gets a voice, who gets more weight?"

Rachel proposed ideas (not necessarily adopted at that time): for decisions involving
several circles, at least 2 people facilitate the proposal; introducing time frames and
deadlines (e.g., no decisions involving several circles in less than 48 hours, or a week; a
D / D+8 / D+16 model used in Open Food France & CoopCircuits where a post is shared,
opinions start at D+8, decision at D+16, with a 2-week discussion window); always conclude
something after the deadline (either extend, or document that no decision was made and why);
create a list of handles to ping per circle.

**Finding.** OFN's decision process is documented in action, including its real friction
points: impact-awareness, fear of decisions made without people, unclear decision-closure,
unresolved questions, and the question of who gets a voice in votes. This is exactly the
kind of structural-participation documentation the concept wants to learn from. The lazy-
consent + 2/3 vote fallback is a concrete model. The three-level subsidiarity governance
(below) is a concrete structure.

### Three-level subsidiarity governance

From the P2P Foundation wiki (wiki.p2pfoundation.net/Open_Food_Network), by Theresa
Schumilas, the OFN project is organized "according to a principle of subsidiarity:
mutualisation and related decisions are made at the most appropriate level according to
the nature of the common." There are three "levels of common" governed by three
communities:

- **Community of national/regional affiliates** (blue): governs jointly the OFN open-
  source software.
- **Community of food hubs of a country/region** (green): governs jointly the national/
  regional affiliate — the "cooperative" kind of entity which deploys and offers the OFN
  software in SaaS access.
- **Community of producers and buyers** (final eaters, chefs, etc.) (yellow): governs
  jointly the local food hub.

"This organization makes it possible to keep the governance of the resource 'in common' at
the appropriate level, thus ensure the sovereignty of the users over the resource they
depend on. It also enable to optimize the management and finance processes necessary for the
preservation and the development of this resource."

**Finding.** This three-level subsidiarity model is a concrete existing structure for a
federated food-system operation: a global commons layer (software), a regional/cooperative
layer (SaaS deployment), and a local food-hub layer (producers and buyers). The concept's
federation question — what does a node join, what does it agree to, what can it disagree
about, what can it leave with — is informed by this: OFN already has a layered federation
with a community pledge connecting instances, a subsidiarity principle for where decisions
sit, and a commons ownership model. The concept is not inventing federation from scratch; it
is synthesizing existing precedents.

### Structural transparency at the consumer level

From the P2P Foundation wiki (Schumilas): "The core defining feature is transparency. For
example, the end consumers can see who grew their food, how their food was grown and how much
the producers at the start of complex 'chains' were paid and how much 'mark up' was taken by
the aggregator/hub/store." And from the Characteristics section: "It is transparent, relying
on peer to peer traceability. Production methods are made transparent to consumers through a
system of labels, and prices are also made transparent. So a consumer can see how much of
their payment went to the producer, and how much covered other costs (e.g. transportation,
packing, administration)."

**Finding.** This is the closest existing statement of sourcing-as-structural-transparency at
the eater level in the scan — an operation where the eater can see who grew the food, how, and
the economics of the chain (producer pay, markup). This is directly relevant to the concept's
sourcing layer and transparency layer, and it's real in OFN, not aspirational. The concept's
distinctiveness is not that OFN lacks this — it's that OFN does this as a platform feature for
community food enterprises, not as a grocery operation with a continuous participatory
information environment and a culture layer.

### The "meritocratic" / "benevolent dictatorship" tension

From the P2P Foundation wiki (Schumilas): "The global OFN community describes its governance
system as 'meritocratic' and following the 'subsidiarity rule'. In this approach those who are
best skilled to do a particular task take the leadership role, and each 'affiliate', service
provider or platform user determines their own decision making process for the decisions made at
the local level, and evolves their own self-governing processes based on the skills assembled."
The US instance setup "might be described as a 'benevolent dictatorship,' where the community
tried to keep hierarchy to a minimum, but temporarily used strong leadership to avoid project
stagnation. Benevolent dictatorships are common in CBPP where participants try to manage the
tensions between hierarchy versus equality, and authority versus autonomy." And: "The OFN
community however, tries to resist the emergence of top down decision making... 'I plan to take
a less proactive role for the time being... when people in the USA are ready to move... I am
happy to offer the experience I have from other OFN countries, but I want to leave it to you to
decide the momentum of this.'"

**Finding.** OFN's governance contains an acknowledged tension between meritocratic/
subsidiarity leadership and the risk of benevolent dictatorship / top-down decision-making — a
real structural-participation tension that the concept's structure layer should be aware of. Any
federated operation has to name how it handles the tension between skilled-leadership
subsidiarity and the risk of unaccountable leadership.

## The Data Food Consortium — the interoperability layer, concretely

- **What it is.** The DFC is "a common and open digital language to develop short circuits" —
  an open data standard for short food supply chains to enable interoperability between farmers,
  marketplaces, and other supply chain actors. (fao.org/agroecology/in-action/detail/data-food-
  consortium...) The FAO page frames it as a "project of interoperability between short supply
  chain platforms in order to allow them to communicate with each other," with the goal "to work
  together to build an open and transparent food system, allowing the emancipation of both
  producers and eaters."
- **Origin.** Started around 2016–2018, hosted by Open Food France. The OFN community thread
  from December 2016 (MyriamBoure) is the clearest early statement of the problem and the vision.
  (community.openfoodnetwork.org/t/data-food-consortium-making-food-platforms-interoperable/772)
- **The problem statement (2016, still current).** Three concrete problems:
  1. A producer managing inventory in one platform who wants to also sell through another (e.g.,
     an OFN shop) has to re-enter all data and update the catalog in each platform every week.
  2. A food hub wanting to display a producer group's products on the hub's OFN shop cannot,
     because the producer group already uses another tool with a dynamic update process and OFN
     could not synchronize the data.
  3. The biggest problem of food hubs is logistics — every hub organizes its own logistics, with
     big economic and environmental cost; some studies show food hubs can be worse than centralized
     food supply in environmental impact because of transport.
  (community.openfoodnetwork.org, MyriamBoure 2016)
- **The vision (2016).** A producer manages its catalog and inventory in one platform (e.g., OFN)
  and can "open up its data to another platform" — read/write authorizations — while remaining the
  owner of its data and controlling them. Standards around describing products and transactions;
  taxonomies; an open-source thesaurus / linked-data platform that all food platforms can refer to.
  Potential for mutualized logistics: "By using open data standards (with a democratic management
  of data and agreement of data owners of course) we could imagine mutualized logistics services
  that would be cheaper for everyone and environmentally friendly." Early participants: Open Food
  France, The Food Assembly, Panier Local, Cagette.net, Ekylibre.
  (community.openfoodnetwork.org, MyriamBoure 2016)
- **Status (ongoing, 2021 framing).** An OFN community post titled "Unveiling our DFC API and
  current developments" says: "The standard is still under active development and open to
  contributors. The implementation is still rough around the edges and in active..."
  (community.openfoodnetwork.org/t/unveiling-our-dfc-api-and-current-developments/3610) — i.e.,
  the interoperability standard exists and is live but is still rough and actively developed.
- **The standard was built because none existed.** Rachel Arnould of DFC, quoted at OFN UK's Food
  Data Collaboration page: "We searched a lot but didn't find any standards that could be applied
  to tools for short food supply chain systems. So, we decided to create our own!"
  (about.openfoodnetwork.org.uk) — the interoperability standards layer is not given by the
  industry; it had to be built by the community.

## DFC current state — now well-grounded and more substantial than the earlier pass suggested

### The DFC Standard has its own site and a major release

From dfc-standard.org:

- "The DFC Standard is a data interoperability standard working in the short food systems space
  that is intentionally designed to support regenerative, agroecological food systems. Our name
  derives from the original (French) project implementing our standard: the Data Food Consortium."
- "The DFC Standard is a standardized way to represent the data needed to supply, sell and deliver
  products through a short supply chain. Requests for and offers of products, customer and purchase
  orders, and data required by logistics providers to ship products, are all examples of data the
  DFC Standard supports. By standardizing the representation of this data, we facilitate the
  exchange of data between platforms and organizations without having to undertake custom development
  for each exchange of data. This simultaneously increases potential market size and reach for good
  food growers and producers, whilst reducing the associated administrative burden of working on a
  platform-by-platform basis."
- "Crucially, there is no one platform to rule them all – the DFC Standard instead fosters diversity
  at scale, ultimately empowering good food systems to achieve greater resilience in an ever-changing
  landscape."
- "The standard ensures the data is represented in specific ways that computers (and software
  engineers) can understand... Work is ongoing in Australia, Canada, France and the UK implementing
  the DFC Standard in a variety of contexts and integrating a diverse range of platforms."

A "DFC v2.0.0 Upgrade Guide" post (Garethe Hughes, February 19, 2026): "We have recently released
our first major release of the DFC Standard. There are a significant number of changes in this major
release version, this guide will give an overview of the changes and cover what's required to comply
with V2.0.0 of the standard."

A "DFC and BECKN protocols – a comparison" post (Garethe Hughes, April 23, 2025): "The DFC Standard
and the Beckn protocol both aim to enable interoperability in digital ecosystems, but they have
different focuses, approaches, and application domains. Both are open-source initiatives that aim to
reduce digital monopolies and create more equitable..."

A "What is the DFC Standard?" post (admin, November 13, 2024).

From the DFC standard documentation intro (docs.dfc-standard.org/dfc-standard-documentation): "This
standard mainly aims at facilitating cooperation and mutualisation within the food distribution system.
But the use of it can be extended, as local buying groups don't only sell food, and it seems that
classical distribution channels might also fit within the business model description of the standard."

**Finding.** The DFC is not "rough around the edges and in active development" in the sense of being
preliminary — it has a v2.0.0 major release (Feb 2026), its own documentation site, an ontology,
taxonomies, a prototype, a connector tool, and named implementations in multiple countries. The
earlier pass's characterization (from the 2021 Discourse thread and the "still rough" quote) was
accurate for 2021 but understates the current state. The concept should treat the DFC Standard as a
real, ongoing, multi-country interoperability standard at v2.0.0, not as a nascent experiment. The
documentation explicitly notes that the standard can extend beyond short food chains to local buying
groups and classical distribution channels.

### The ontology, taxonomy, prototype, and connector tool

From fooddatacollaboration.org.uk/what-does-it-mean-to-implement/:

- The DFC has defined an ontology that provides "a general knowledge framework that maps the concepts
  used within Short Foodchain Systems in a machine readable format." Source of truth: github.com/
  datafoodconsortium/ontology/; published to docs.datafoodconsortium.org/ontology/; a NextOntology
  branch for pre-release/development versions; taxonomies (product type, units, etc.) in a separate
  repository (github.com/datafoodconsortium/taxonomies/); a ShowVOC tool at showvoc.dfc-standard.org.
- The DFC built a prototype that "facilitates product data testing for platforms and is currently the
  primary interface between the systems." "In the longer term it is intended that any member of the
  Data Commons will be able to directly push reads and changes (record create, update and delete
  statements), for the data they own and control, to all other platforms within the Commons, via a
  JSON-LD API that conforms to the DFC ontology." The prototype is published under an MIT licence;
  code at github.com/datafoodconsortium/prototype; current instance at proto.datafoodconsortium.org.
- The DFC developed a Connector Tool (github.com/datafoodconsortium/connector-codegen) using Acceleo
  to generate code libraries to facilitate implementation of the DFC Ontology as a model within
  applications — TypeScript, Ruby, PHP versions; designed to be implemented in other languages;
  "Development costs are anticipated at £1,500-£3,000, and to be borne by the associated project."
- FDC Platforms are currently implementing standard version v1.9.

**Finding.** The DFC is a real technical interoperability stack: ontology, taxonomy, prototype, JSON-
LD API, connector code-generation tool in three languages, with implementations at v1.9 and a recent
v2.0.0 major release. The "data they own and control" language echoes OFN Canada's data-sovereignty
framing and the concept's access-vs-control distinction. The concept's interoperability layer is
informed by a real, specific, existing standard — not a vague aspiration.

### The UK Food Data Collaboration pilots, concretely

From fooddatacollaboration.org.uk/what-does-it-mean-to-implement/:

Three Phase 1 pilots:

1. "The first pilot is with the Hodmedod's trade store, testing our advanced Orders functionality
   (beyond the scope outlined here) between Shopify storefronts... allows us to test our
   interoperability functionality against a single platform and provides value to our pilot users by
   automating updates to the product catalogue (including pricing) and placing of purchase orders/stock
   management."
2. "The second pilot involves linking a small producer using a Shopify store with an Open Food Network
   hub for integrated Ordering."
3. "Our third pilot will link a group of OFN Hubs with the Hodmedod's trade store to allow combined
   bulk orders to reduce costs to hubs whilst automating the complexity of managing group orders
   (including split cases)."

The implementation steps (Phase 1): (1) interface platforms with an Open ID Connect provider (Les
Communs Keycloak server); (2) create and publish a CRUD API for Products data using the DFC JSON-LD
standard; (3) exposure/consumption of Product data from other platforms in the shopfront.

FDC Commons governance requirements for partners: "We require partner organisations to align with the
values outlined in our Vision and Mission" and "We also invite all platform partners to consider
whether they'd be willing (and have capacity) to participate in our Governance circle." And: "FDC
Commons Governance requires all platforms to have the ability to exclude individual users from the
Commons (in cases of extreme or repeated breaches of Commons Rules). The FDC does not seek to control
who can or cannot use individual platforms, however we have a duty to protect the integrity of the
Commons."

**Finding.** The Food Data Collaboration is a funded, multi-pilot interoperability project in action,
with named participants (Hodmedod's, small producers on Shopify, OFN hubs) and a governance layer that
requires value alignment and a Commons Rules enforcement mechanism (the ability to exclude users who
breach Commons Rules) without controlling who uses individual platforms. This is a concrete existing
governance/interoperability model for the concept's structure layer — including the question of how a
federated commons enforces its rules without controlling who uses individual platforms.

## OFN Canada's "open" framing — structure-level definition of open

OFN Canada's "Open Agricultural Technology" page is the clearest existing statement of what "open"
means at the structure level for a food platform, and it maps onto the concept's structure layer
directly.

- **Three categories of open.** (about.openfoodnetwork.ca/project/open-agricultural-technology)
  1. **Open Source** — "Software whose code is freely available to use, improve, and share. It
     encourages transparency, innovation, and collective progress."
  2. **Open Standards** — "Publicly available rules and formats that allow different technologies to
     work together. They prevent silos, encourage interoperability, and make innovation easier."
  3. **Data Sovereignty** — "The principle that farmers should control their own data — deciding how
     it is stored, shared, and applied. It ensures technology serves producers, not outside corporate
     or political interests."
- **The access vs. control distinction.** This is the most important passage for the concept's
  structure layer: "The debate over farm data is not just about access; it's about control. Open data
  improves transparency, but without protections, it risks exposing farmers to exploitation... While
  'open data' increases transparency, it doesn't guarantee fairness or farmer agency. Without strong
  protections, openness risks benefiting the most powerful actors in the supply chain — not the
  producers themselves." Examples of misuse: market manipulation by large buyers using detailed
  production data; unequal value capture by corporations selling insights to insurers, input providers,
  or speculators; loss of autonomy; privacy concerns. (OFN Canada)
- **Data sovereignty framing.** "Data sovereignty goes further than simply making agricultural data
  easy to access — ensuring that farmers themselves govern how their information is used, with clear
  consent and accountability." And: "OFN Canada has demonstrated this commitment by adopting OpenTEAM's
  Ag Data Use Agreements, which provide a framework for responsible, farmer-governed data sharing."
  (OFN Canada)
- **Partners.** OpenTEAM ("a collaborative network advancing climate-smart agriculture and open,
  interoperable tools that keep farmers in control of their data"), D:Food/Web Initiative ("an emerging
  coalition advancing food and data sovereignty through decentralized, community-driven technologies
  and cooperative data governance"), GOAT ("Gathering for Open Agricultural Technology — a global
  network of developers, farmers, and stakeholders advancing open source solutions for transparency,
  secure data sharing, and market access"), DFC Open Standard, and the Québec project ("a collaborative
  project building a shared, open-source web portal that empowers food hubs and producers to federate
  data and coordinate supply chains without sacrificing control of how their data is used, or who has
  access to it"). (OFN Canada)

**Finding for the concept.** This is the strongest existing structural-framing reference in the scan.
OFN Canada's three-category open (Open Source, Open Standards, Data Sovereignty) and its access-vs-
control distinction directly inform the concept's structure layer. The concept's "open structure" and
"open sourcing" layers map onto this: openness is not the same as access; the operation has to be built
so that the people who provide the data (producers, eaters, the community) govern how it is used, with
consent and accountability, and openness does not become a mechanism for the most powerful actors to
extract value. The Ag Data Use Agreements and the Québec federated-data portal are concrete precedents
for the kind of data-governance structure the concept wants.

## What OFN/DFC are not (important for the concept's distinctiveness)

- **Not a grocery operation.** OFN is a software platform that community food enterprises use; it is
  not itself a grocery store, buying group, or distribution operation. It does not embody the concept's
  Action layer in the sense of moving food and capturing/distributing the food dollar.
- **Not a full grocery-scale federation.** OFN is a network of platform instances and community food
  enterprises; it is not a federated grocery operation at the scale the concept imagines. The DFC is an
  interoperability standard for short supply chain platforms, not a governance structure for a federated
  grocery operation.
- **Not a sourcing-transparency-as-structural model.** OFN/DFC work on data about food (products,
  inventory, orders, supply chain relationships), but the sourcing-transparency-as-structural layer the
  concept wants — where sourcing information is surfaced by default and is part of the operation's
  identity to eaters — is not the focus. OFN's transparency is more about supply chain data and data
  sovereignty than about an eater-facing sourcing information environment.
- **Not a continuous participation environment.** OFN has a community (a Discourse forum, global
  gatherings, local affiliates) and the platform supports community food enterprises, but it is not a
  continuous participatory information environment around a grocery operation in the concept's sense.
- **Not a culture layer.** OFN/DFC do not address the meaning/culture dimension of food — Indigenous
  foodways, diaspora traditions, regional food cultures, family and community food practices — as a
  structural layer.

**Finding for the concept.** OFN/DFC are the best available reference points for the concept's Structure
and Interaction questions around federation, interoperability, open source, Commons ownership, and data
sovereignty. They are not the concept. The concept's distinctiveness lies in moving from
platform/interoperability to operational grocery, and from interoperability to continuous participation
and structural transparency and culture. The concept should not claim OFN/DFC as examples of the concept;
it should use them as the closest existing test of pieces of the architecture and learn from what they
built and what they don't yet do.
