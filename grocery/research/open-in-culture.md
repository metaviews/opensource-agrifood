# Open source in culture — first-pass scan

Date: 2026-08-26
Purpose: A first-pass sweep across where the principles, ethics, language, or
modalities of open source have been applied to culture — and what that has
accomplished, complicated, or failed at. This is a surface scan, not a
verification pass. Claims are flagged for follow-up, not settled.

This is a supplement to the grocery concept research, but it is read on its
own terms: the question is not "what does a grocery operation borrow from
open source culture" but "where has open source gone when it left software,
and what happened."

## How this scan was done

Parallel web searches across several cultural domains plus a semantic-history
search on the migration of "open" from software to everything, followed by
extractions of the most outcome-bearing pages. The result is a shape document:
what exists, what the categories look like, what tensions appear repeatedly,
and what questions the scan raises for a second pass.

## 1. The semantic migration — "open" from software to a general adjective

The original referent: open source software, with a specific meaning — source
code is available, modifiable, and redistributable under a license that permits
those acts. The Open Source Definition and the Free Software Definition are
the anchor documents; "free as in freedom, not free as in beer" is the
canonical clarification.

What happened next: the word "open" detached from its software-specific meaning
and began to attach to government, data, science, education, culture, hardware,
design, fashion, journalism, and journalism-adjacent domains. Each adoption
metaphorically extends some part of the open-source idea — availability,
modifiability, sharing, community governance, collaboration — to a domain where
those acts have a different meaning and a different legal and economic shape.

What surfaces in the search:

- "Open government" and "open data" appear as policy movements (Open Government
  Partnership, data.gov, open data indices), often framed in transparency terms
  that are adjacent to but not identical to open source. Open data means freely
  available, reusable, redistributable data under an open license (at most,
  attribution and share-alike), not a governance model.
- "Open science" is defined by UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation as an inclusive
  construct combining open access to publications, open research data, open
  educational resources, open source software and source code and hardware,
  citizen and participatory science, and open evaluation. It is described as a
  "new paradigm" integrating reproducibility, transparency, sharing, and
  collaboration into the "entire research cycle." The recommendation explicitly
  names open source software stacks as a preferred basis for digital
  infrastructures for open science, and names community-driven contribution,
  attribution, and governance as required to enable reuse. It also names the
  FAIR and CARE principles and the tension between them — "open science should
  not only foster enhanced sharing... but also promote inclusion... and
  contribute to reducing inequalities in access to scientific development,
  infrastructures and capabilities."
- "Open access" to research publications (Budapest 2002, Bethesda 2003, Berlin
  2003) is the earlier academic parallel that pre-dates much of this. It is
  about access to the publication, not about the method, and it rapidly became
  entangled with publishing economics.
- "Free culture" and "open culture" emerge as the cultural-domain adoptions,
  explicitly modeled on open source software. Creative Commons is the
  licensing bridge; Lawrence Lessig's Free Culture (2004) is the argument that
  copyright law and technology are shrinking the public domain and that this
  matters for creativity itself.

The pattern that appears in the search more than once: "openwashing" — the term
describes situations where the word "open" is used as marketing or window-
dressing without the substance. It is described as derived from "greenwashing,"
and it is said to extend beyond the private sector to governments and research
institutions. The semantic drift of "open" into a near-synonym for "we are
nice" or "we have a website" is the most conspicuous effect of the migration:
the word's force as a specific claim weakens the more widely it is used.

**Finding for the concept.** The semantic migration of "open" is itself the
story the concept needs to be aware of. A grocery operation that says "open
source grocery" is entering a word that has already been stretched thin.
The concept's own uses of "open" — open sourcing, open information, open
structure, open participation — are specific and layered, but the word outside
the concept has already been diluted. The concept should name its own meaning
of open precisely, and should expect the word to carry weakened associations
from outside. The concept's design principle (not reinforcing what exists,
building something that might not exist without the design) is itself a
response to the dilution: it is a refusal of the "open as a label" move.

## 2. The free culture movement and Creative Commons — the licensing bridge

What exists: The free culture movement, organized around the argument that
culture should be free to build upon — that the default "all rights reserved"
is too restrictive for a creative ecosystem. Creative Commons is the
operational center of this movement: a set of free, public, standardized
copyright licenses that let creators keep their copyright while sharing on
their own terms, plus CC0 for dedications to the public domain.

History: Founded in 2001 by Lawrence Lessig, Hal Abelson, and Eric Eldred,
explicitly as a response to copyright extension (the Sonny Bono / CTEA
legislation, challenged in Eldred v. Ashcroft) and as a translate of the open
source software idea into the cultural domain. The first licenses published
December 16, 2002. By 2015, over a billion works were CC-licensed; by 2019,
2 billion; by 2023, 2.5 billion. Wikipedia switched to CC licenses in 2009,
which was a major structural change in the knowledge commons. ProPublica
launched in 2008 publishing all stories under CC BY-NC-ND. Flickr enabled CC
in 2004. The European Commission adopted CC-BY 4.0 and CC0 for published
documents in 2019. CC became an official UNESCO partner in 2025 and its
licenses were registered as Digital Public Goods in 2024.

What the movement did: It built a licensing infrastructure that made it legally
straightforward to share cultural and knowledge works with varying degrees of
freedom — attribution required, commercial use allowed or not, derivatives
allowed or not, share-alike required or not. It gave institutions a standard
way to say "you can use this" without negotiating. It made "free cultural
works" a legible category (the Open Source Stack Exchange "What is a Creative
Commons Free Cultural Work?" question is a sign that the category has to be
defined against the software referent). It enabled remix culture to be legally
sanctioned in some forms rather than existing in a grey area.

What the movement did not do, and the critiques that surfaced:

- The legal enforcement record is modest. A decade in (by 2012), the licenses
  had been ported to over 70 jurisdictions but upheld in only a handful of
  court cases. The search result on CC history confirms the first court
  decision was in Amsterdam in 2006 (a CC-BY-NC-SA case), and that the
  licenses are now used on tens of billions of works — i.e., the scale is high
  and the court record is sparse.
- Purists object to "some rights reserved." The Free Software Foundation's RMS
  has objected to CC licenses that are not fully free (NC and ND clauses
  especially). This is a recurring internal tension: CC is a spectrum, and the
  spectrum includes licenses that free-culture purists do not regard as free.
- Pragmatic critiques question efficacy and impact: the licenses are often
  embraced as creators' careers are ascending or descending; the licensing
  choice can be aspirational rather than operational; the big commercial
  entertainers (Rolling Stones, Michael Bay) did not adopt CC. The Shareable
  piece (2012) captures this: "Anyone holding their breath for the Rolling
  Stones or Michael Bay to embrace Creative Commons might want invest in
  ventilators."
- The movement's reach is real but uneven. It has strong institutional adoption
  (World Bank open access policy, European Commission, Smithsonian, ProPublica,
  Nature for some genomic research, Wired and Al Jazeera for some photojournalism)
  and a grassroots adoption among individual creators, but the large commercial
  entertainment industries are largely absent.

**Finding for the concept.** Creative Commons is the most successful application
of open-source-style thinking to culture, and its success is primarily as a
licensing infrastructure that lowers the transaction cost of "I will let you
use this under these terms." It is a tool for openness, not a governance
model, and not a participation environment. The concept's grocery operation is
about much more than licensing — it is about sourcing information as a default,
a participation environment, a culture layer, a structure — so CC is a
supporting tool at most, not the architecture. The concept should learn from
the CC model the lesson that a standard, legible, lightweight openness
instrument can be widely adopted in ways that a more demanding model cannot,
and should learn the lesson that "some rights reserved" is the actually-adopted
middle, not the purist binary.

The concept should also learn the critique: CC adoption does not guarantee that
the licensed works actually circulate in an open ecosystem. Licensing and
ecosystem are different things. A CC-licensed piece of footage or a CC-licensed
sourcing record does not by itself create a participatory environment or a
commons that functions.

## 3. Wikipedia — the large case study in open cultural production

What exists: Wikipedia is the largest single example of open, peer-produced,
open-licensed cultural knowledge in existence. It is written and maintained by
a community of volunteers through open collaboration; it runs on open-source
software (MediaWiki); its content is CC BY-SA and public domain. It is the most
concrete demonstration that "anyone can edit" works at enormous scale.

What it accomplished:

- Scale and durability. 25+ years of continuous operation; one of the most
  visited sites on the internet; a knowledge commons that is administered as a
  commons (the Wikimedia Foundation, a nonprofit, steward the infrastructure)
  rather than as a product. The Nature piece (Jemielniak, January 2026,
  Wikipedia's 25th anniversary) is explicit that the academic community
  "systematically failed our greatest knowledge commons" for 25 years — a
  framing that says something about the commons' value and about the failure of
  the surrounding institutions to sustain it.
- Machine-readability and downstream use. Wikipedia's heavy text-based design
  made wiki content machine-readable and useful for the current phase of AI
  development. Wikimedia content has been used for face-recognition training and
  for training most major large language models; the Open Future piece (Tarkowski,
  July 2023) argues that knowledge commons built by the Wikimedia Movement
  packaged into training datasets have proven a valuable resource for AI
  development, and that this creates a need for governance of sourcing such
  datasets sustainably — including a possible Wikidataset and a Wikimedia
  Enterprise voluntary payment model.

What it did not accomplish, and the failures and tensions:

- The participation gap / systemic bias. Wikipedia's editing community is
  demographically narrow. Various studies have estimated only 8.5% to 16.1%
  of editors are women; non-binary editorial participation is "virtually
  non-existent" per one review. As of January 2025, only 18.9% of the content
  in all Wikimedia projects, including biographies, is about women (Project
  Rewrite, Wikimedia Foundation). The content gender gap is multi-dimensional —
  composition of editors, composition of article subjects, structural biases in
  content policies (notability, sourcing), and the way NPOV can reflect rather
  than correct bias in sources. The systemic-bias page is explicit: Wikipedia
  underrepresents the Global South, people without internet access or a
  serviceable computer, people who do not have free time to edit, and topics
  for which reliable sources are not recently published, easily available
  online, and in English; it shows a White Anglo-American perspective due to
  the preponderance of English-speaking editors from Anglophone countries.
  Content reflects the biases in the sources it uses, and NPOV requires
  documenting what the best available sources say rather than correcting
  sources' biases.
- The academic community's failure. The same Nature piece frames the academic
  community's 25-year failure to engage productively with Wikipedia as a major
  problem, and flags that AI systems are feeding on Wikipedia without giving
  back, threatening the commons' survival. This is an effect of the open model:
  what is freely available is also freely extracted, and extraction without
  reciprocity can erode the commons.
- Governance under stress from AI. The Open Future piece argues that
  participatory governance and partnerships need to become principles for
  WikiAI, and that neither is fully realized so far — both are "aspirational."
  Machine learning systems create major challenges (use/exploitation of the
  commons, synthetic content contributed to the commons) and require
  participatory governance mechanisms that the current commons-based peer-
  production model does not yet have. A "Wikimedian assembly on AI" is proposed
  as a way to strengthen governance.

**Finding for the concept.** Wikipedia is the most important existing case of an
open cultural production model at scale, and it is the richest source of
cautions for the concept. The cautions are not that "open fails" — it clearly
does not fail at scale and durability — but that the open model encodes the
demographics and incentives of its participants into the product, that
"anyone can edit" produces a narrow and self-reinforcing contributor base in
practice, that NPOV and sourcing policies can reproduce rather than correct
the biases of the existing source environment, and that the open commons is
vulnerable to extraction without reciprocity, especially by actors (search
engines, AI companies) with far more resources than the commons.

For the concept's grocery operation, these cautions are directly relevant to the
participation layer (who participates, who doesn't, how the environment
shapes its own participants), the culture layer (what gets represented and
how), and the structure layer (how the operation handles extraction, funding,
and the power asymmetry between the commons and the actors who use it). The
concept should not assume that "open participation" automatically produces a
broad, representative, healthy participant base — Wikipedia is the living proof
that it does not, and that a great deal of active work is required to address
the gaps.

The concept should also note the AI-stewardship problem as a forward-looking
caution: if the grocery operation produces openly available information about
food, sourcing, provenance, and community knowledge, that information can be
extracted and used in ways the operation does not control — for AI training,
for competitor intelligence, for models that re-intermediate the eater from
the source. The Wikipedia/Open Future debate about WikiAI and the Wikidataset
is the most concrete existing framing of this question for an open knowledge
commons, and the concept should take it seriously even though food information
is not encyclopedia knowledge.

## 4. Open GLAM — the institutional adoption of open in cultural heritage

What exists: The OpenGLAM (Galleries, Libraries, Archives, Museums) movement
advocates for liberal access to and reuse of public-domain collections — high-
resolution images, collection data, metadata — under open licenses or public-
domain dedications. The OpenGLAM Principles state that cultural institutions
have a fundamental role in supporting the advance of humanity's knowledge, that
the internet presents an unprecedented opportunity to engage global audiences and
make collections more discoverable and connected, and that users should be able
not only to enjoy the collections but also to contribute, participate, and
share. They define "open" by reference to the Open Definition, and list
principles including keeping public-domain works in the public domain (no new
rights added), publishing data with explicit reuse statements, using open
machine-readable file formats, and pursuing novel audience engagement.

Named institutions and events:

- The Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam is the canonical early example: began releasing
  high-resolution digital surrogates in 2011, released 150,000 to the public
  domain in 2015 (now over 600,000). It charges €20 for entry; the ticket-fee
  offset is often cited as what makes open access financially plausible for a
  major museum.
- The Smithsonian launched Smithsonian Open Access, making millions of images
  and data available for download, share, and reuse without asking. The Getty
  Museum, the Victoria and Albert Museum, the British Library, the Walters Art
  Museum, the National Library of New Zealand, and Princeton and Harvard art
  museums are among the institutions that have adopted open-access strategies.
- The Open GLAM Survey (Douglas McCarthy and Andrea Wallace) has surveyed the
  field for six years; it was cited in a 2018 UK Parliament debate on how
  museums balance public access with commercial reuse of digital content. CC's
  2024 post builds on the six years of the survey.
- The Open Heritage Coalition (formerly TAROCH) is a coalition advancing equitable
  access to heritage in the public domain, of which CC is a member.

What institutions report as effects:

- New business opportunities and industry relationships, new methods of
  engagement, knowledge generation, and collections management, and greater
  fulfillment of public missions generally. The PubPub "Barriers to Open Access"
  document says through open access, many GLAMs have seen gains from these.
- New kinds of use and reuse that were not anticipated: people make prints,
  merchandise, datasets, scholarly works, and derivative creative works from
  openly released collections. The Rijksstudio model (user-curated collections
  from the Rijksmuseum) is cited as an example of novel engagement.
- The attention-economy concern: as more GLAMs go open, online traffic and
  consumption increasingly follow the openly-licensed materials; other downsides
  could include decreased brand-licensing revenue, limited public engagement on
  popular websites, or reduced general awareness of collections.

What is thin or contested:

- The business model is genuinely contested. The PubPub piece names the irony
  "when GLAMs fail to level the public domain playing field by locking their
  publics out of its reuse." It names ticket fees as a tradeoff (the Rijksmuseum
  example is often assumed to be representative, but it may not be — many
  institutions do not have a major paid-entry revenue stream). Copyright being
  used as a business model for reproduction sales is named as a "really bad"
  business model in one source.
- Not all collections can or should be opened. The CARE Principles and the
  tension with FAIR appear in the open-science context but are also relevant to
  cultural heritage — some knowledge is sacred, some is not appropriate to
  share, some is held under cultural protocols that "open" would violate. The
  INDIGENOUS FOOD SOVEREIGNTY material from the grocery research (food as sacred,
  "not culturally appropriate to sell") is a parallel that the concept should
  hold in mind: the open-in-cultural-heritage movement has to contend with the
  same question.
- The "open" in OpenGLAM is largely about access to images and data, not about
  participation in the institution's decision-making, governance, or curatorial
  culture. It is open access and open reuse more than open production or open
  governance. This is a limited version of "open" that the concept should
  distinguish from its own multi-layered sense.

**Finding for the concept.** OpenGLAM is the largest institutional application of
open-source-style thinking to culture, and it shows that "open access to the
collection" is a real and achievable thing with measurable effects — new reuse,
new engagement, new knowledge, and a contested but workable business-model
question. It also shows the limits: it is primarily about access and reuse, not
about shared governance or shared production or a culture of participation in
the institution's own decisions; and it runs into the sacred/appropriate-to-share
question that the concept's culture layer shares.

The concept's culture layer should learn from OpenGLAM that open access to
cultural material can be a genuine public good and a genuine institutional
strategy, but the concept's own culture layer is about something different and
more: the meaning of food, the surfacing of cultural substance, the
participation of eaters in a food environment as a cultural environment, not
just a retail environment. The concept should not reduce "open culture" to
"open the collection."

## 5. Open source applied to individual creative practice — film, art, design,
music, fabrication

What exists: A set of individual and small-community practices that apply open
source principles to creative work directly, rather than through an institution.
These are scattered and varied:

- Open Filmmaking: a framework that encourages releasing the "source code of a
  film" — footage under open licenses — and actively uses open source software
  and open multimedia resources. The Opensource.com piece (Subhashish
  Panigrahi, 2023) describes two documentary films ("The Volunteer Archivists"
  and "Nani Ma") made under open licenses, with all footage and supporting
  multimedia released under CC BY-SA 4.0 on the Internet Archive. The framing is
  explicitly ethical as well as practical: marginalized communities have a moral
  ownership over footage, and researchers and others do not always provide
  communities with direct and open access. The piece is candid about the cost:
  "it's tricky, if not impossible, to release a film under an open license if
  the film's copyright owner does not fully own the footage used"; films often
  purchase rights for media produced by others.
- Open source art / Art is Open Source (AOS): an interdisciplinary research
  laboratory founded in Italy in 2004, focused on merging artistic and scientific
  practices. First Monday has published on open source locative art. There is a
  small academic literature (ResearchGate "Open Source and New Media Artists,"
  academia.edu "Open Source Art Again"). This is a niche, not a mainstream
  movement.
- Open source design and Open source fashion: a set of projects and manifestos —
  Openknit (an open source 3D knitting printer), Libre Objet (an open source
  furniture design license), the FASHION OPEN SOURCE MANIFESTO, and a small
  industry discussion about open source libraries of vector templates, technical
  sketches, and patterns. Forrester and TechPacker have published on open source
  design as a community practice. This is currently more an emerging conversation
  and a set of pilots than a functioning ecosystem.
- Open source music: Creative Commons for music is real and widely used (Jamendo,
  freesound.org, CC-licensed music sites). The conversation is mostly about
  licensing choice (which CC variant) and the community/loyalty effect of
  releasing under open terms, less about a music commons that functions as a
  shared production environment. MuseScore's free licensing of scores is a
  concrete example of open sharing enabling third-party reuse that would not
  otherwise be possible.
- Open source hardware: a real and established movement (open source hardware
  association, open source hardware definition), with a clear application to
  fabrication, instruments, and tools. This is adjacent to culture (making,
  craft, DIY) but is more about the object than the cultural meaning.

What the search surfaces as the recurring tension in these practices:

- The uniqueness/ownership problem. The Travidorn piece (2025) is the clearest
  articulation: the art world has traditionally revolved around unique, original
  works whose value comes from scarcity and connection to the creator. Artists
  earn a living by selling unique creations or licensing rights. If a painting
  can be freely copied and distributed, its value to the artist diminishes. Open
  source flips this: share the recipe, not just the cake; the value is in the
  ability to inspect, understand, fix, and contribute. The piece argues for
  opt-in, artist-controlled sharing of formulas for paints, schematics for
  instruments, algorithms behind digital art software, and "source files" of
  digital works — layered files, raw audio tracks, 3D models — and for a remix
  culture that is celebrated and legally recognized rather than in a grey area.
  It is candid that this is currently marginal.
- The economic problem. Open release of creative works does not by itself create
  a sustainable livelihood for the creator; the Travidorn piece notes that
  sharing can build a loyal community and open new monetization paths (premium
  tutorials, workshops), and that an artist's unique process is becoming more
  valuable as AI-generated art becomes a problem, but the economic case is real
  and unresolved.
- The AI problem, inverted. The same piece that treats AI as a threat to the
  finished work also treats the openness of process as something AI cannot
  replicate, and the Open Filmmaking piece treats open release as an ethical
  obligation to communities. The AI moment is splitting the open-in-culture
  conversation in two directions: one defensive (protect the work, opt out of
  training), one offensive (open the process, build the commons, steward the
  commons against extraction).

**Finding for the concept.** Open source applied to individual creative practice
is real but fragmented and mostly small-scale. Its recurring themes are the
tension between uniqueness and sharing, the economic question, and the AI
moment. The most useful lesson for the concept is not a model to copy but a
set of questions: when is sharing the process valuable and when is it not? who
controls the decision to open? what does "source" mean in a domain that is not
software? and how does a creator or community sustain itself when the finished
product is openly available?

For the concept's grocery operation, the culture layer is not a "finished
product" in the art sense, but it is a finished environment — a grocery
operation with a culture, a meaning, a set of surfaced knowledge and practices.
The open-in-creative-practice conversation is useful for thinking about what
"open" means for something that is not a software artifact and not a static
cultural object: an ongoing, participatory, information-rich environment whose
"source" includes sourcing data, community knowledge, recipes, stories,
practices, and the operation's own decision-making.

## 6. Open source journalism and open methods as a cultural/informational
practice

What exists: "Open source journalism" is used on a spectrum: from semi-
participatory online community publications to open-source intelligence (OSINT)
investigations that use publicly available sources and open methods. Nieman
Reports, GIJN, and Reuters Institute have material on open-source and
collaborative journalism. First Draft News was a community for open-source
investigations and verification of eyewitness content.

What surfaces as effects and questions:

- The participatory promise: open-source research offers journalism a
  participatory model that could, in theory, enhance trust in the media. The
  Nieman Reports piece frames this as a promise, not a delivered outcome.
- The verification and trust problem: open-source investigations depend on
  publicly available evidence and on the credibility of methods; the trust
  question is live.
- The collaborative local journalism thread (Reuters Institute, Collaborative
  Journalism) is a parallel: collaboration as a structural response to the
  collapse of local news — interesting because it is about structure and
  sustainability, which is the concept's kind of question.

This domain is less developed in the search than the others and is flagged for
a possible second pass if it proves relevant. The concept's information layer
has some contact with this — an open grocery operation is, in part, an
information environment — but journalism is not the concept's primary
reference.

## 7. The negative case — dilution, openwashing, and the limits of "open" as
a word in culture

What surfaces across the searches as a recurring caution:

- Openwashing: the term describes situations where "open" is used as marketing
  or window-dressing without the substance (derived from greenwashing). Cited in
  connection with open source, open science, open government, open data, and
  open AI models. A 2017 paper on open source projects as incubators of
  innovation and a 2025 paper on open-source models for data and metadata
  standards both reference openwashing explicitly. The most recent and vivid
  example is the meta/LLaMA situation: "Meta is co-opting that culture, draining
  its meaning, and using it as camouflage." The AI moment has produced a live
  debate about what "open source" means for models — a debate that is itself
  evidence that the word's meaning is contested.
- The semantic dilution: the word "open" has been stretched by its migration
  from software to government, data, science, education, culture, hardware,
  design, and fashion. In many of these domains, "open" means "we make this
  available" or "we have a website" or "we have a policy" — a weakened version
  of the original claim. The concept should expect this weakened association
  whenever it uses the word.

- The "free culture" tension between purists and pragmatists: the Free Software
  Foundation objects to "some rights reserved"; the CC model is a spectrum; the
  spectrum includes licenses that do not satisfy the purists. This is a recurring
  internal fracture in the open-in-culture movement: the movement that succeeded
  (CC) is a pragmatic middle, and the purist position is a persistent critique
  rather than the dominant practice.

- The commons-extraction problem, again: Wikipedia's 25-year history of academic
  non-engagement and AI extraction without reciprocity is the clearest existing
  case. The OpenGLAM debate about commercial reuse of open collections is a
  smaller version. The CC history piece notes that the movement "concentrated
  power in the hands of a few and undermined trust in the social contract of the
  commons" — a caution that openness can have unintended distributional effects.

**Finding for the concept.** The negative case is as important as the positive
case. The concept is building something that deliberately uses "open" in a
specific, layered sense, and it should expect and plan for the word to be
weakened by association, diluted by usage, and possibly openwashed by actors
who want the credibility of "open" without the substance. The concept's design
principle (not reinforcing what exists, building something that might not exist
without the design) is a direct answer to this: the concept is not trying to
add "open" as a label to an existing grocery operation; it is proposing an
operation whose openness is constitutive, not decorative.

The concept should also internalize the commons-extraction caution: if it
produces openly available information, it should have a plan for who can use
that information, under what terms, and with what reciprocity — or it should
expect to be extracted by actors with more resources than it has. Wikipedia and
the AI moment make this concrete.

## 8. What the scan does not yet cover

- Deep historical case studies: the free culture movement's own history (Lessig's
  Free Culture book, the Eldred case, the founding of CC) is located but not
  read in depth; the effects of the movement on actual creative practice over
  time are not yet assessed beyond the licensing numbers.
- The concrete effects of Wikipedia's biases on the world: the gender and
  systemic-bias findings are well-documented, but the downstream effects on
  knowledge, decision-making, and representation are not yet traced.
- The user-side effects of OpenGLAM openness: what people actually do with
  open museum collections, in what numbers, with what cultural effect — the
  search surfaces institutional claims and some reuse examples, but not a
  systematic user-side picture.
- Non-Western and Indigenous applications of "open" in culture: this is flagged
  as a likely gap, and it matters because the concept is Canada-centred and
  because Indigenous food sovereignty (already in the grocery research) makes
  the question of what "open" means for culturally held knowledge urgent. The
  CARE Principles / FAIR tension appears in open science but the cultural-heritage
  version is not yet developed in this pass.
- Failure cases in open-in-culture practices: the scan surfaces critiques and
  tensions more than clean failure case studies. Where open-in-culture initiatives
  actually failed, folded, or were absorbed into conventional models, is not yet
  catalogued.
- Open source in cuisine specifically: the scan does not yet address whether open
  source principles have been applied to food/cooking/cuisine as a cultural
  practice — open recipes, open cookbooks, collaborative food knowledge — which
  would be the most directly adjacent domain to the concept.

## What this is for

This scan is a surface pass across the cultural side of open source. It is meant
to give the concept a map of where open source has gone in culture, what it has
done, what it has failed at, and what tensions recur. It is not a synthesis; it
is a map pointing to the most informative cases and the most informative
cautions. The map suggests a second pass that would dig into a few of these:

A note on the grocery connection: the concept is not trying to become an open-
source-culture project. It is a grocery operation that applies the notion of
open on multiple levels. The value of this scan for the concept is the map of
what open-in-culture has accomplished and failed at, so that the concept can
borrow the accomplishments (licensing infrastructure, open-access institutions,
open creative practice, open knowledge at scale) without importing the failures
(dilution of the word, extraction without reciprocity, narrow and self-
reinforcing participation, the reproduction of existing biases) and without
assuming that "open" means the same thing in food as it means in software or
in encyclopedia or in museum collections.
