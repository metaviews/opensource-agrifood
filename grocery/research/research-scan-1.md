# Grocery concept — first-pass environmental scan

Date: 2026-08-26
Purpose: A first-pass sweep across the candidate areas to identify categories,
concepts, named operations, and open questions worth deeper dives. This is a surface
scan, not verification. Sources are search results and landing pages; claims are
flagged for follow-up, not settled.

## How this scan was done

Six parallel web searches across the research-direction categories, plus one emergent
category (food data interoperability) that surfaced during the food-hubs scan. Each
search returned 8 results. This document captures the surface shape — what exists, what
the categories look like, what named things appear, and what questions the scan raises
for deeper dives.

## 1. Cooperative grocery and food co-ops

What exists: A large, mature, North-America-centered food co-op sector. Consumer-owned
grocery cooperatives are widespread in the US and present in Canada. There is an
established support ecology: Food Co-op Initiative (free resources and consulting for
starting retail food co-ops), National Co+op Grocers (NCG, a buying/distribution federation
of co-ops), co-op directories (Food Co-op Finder), and state/regional co-op associations.

Named things that appeared: NCG and its 2024/2025 Food Co-op Impact Report; PCC Community
Markets (Seattle) and its "grocery story" article about how food co-ops transformed an
industry through distribution co-ops; Neighborhood Co-op; Community Food Co-op (Bozeman); Food
Co-op Initiative; co-op association sites (grocery.coop, ncbaclusa.coop, columinate.coop).

What the category looks like: Consumer cooperatives owned by members, democratically
governed, often with a strong focus on local/organic/fair-trade sourcing and community benefit.
Some are retail stores, some are buying clubs. Distribution co-ops exist to increase buying
power and centralize storage/distribution across independent co-ops — a federated structure
already in operation.

Open questions this raises: To what degree are existing food co-ops "open source" in the
concept's sense, versus community-owned retail with a values framing? Where do they already
embody participation, transparency, and community benefit — and where do they fall short of the
concept's internet-era posture? How does NCG's federated buying/distribution model map onto the
concept's federation questions? What does the co-op sector's experience with scaling,
governance, and member participation teach the concept?

## 2. Platform and network models in food

What exists: A platform cooperativism movement (Platform Cooperativism Consortium) that
explicitly applies cooperative ownership to digital platforms. Some food-adjacent platform
cooperatives appear. Federated co-op structures exist in agriculture (e.g., Federated Co-
operatives Ltd. in Canada, a large federated retail/distribution co-op; the USDA/RD paper on
the changing federated relationship between local and regional cooperatives).

Named things that appeared: Platform Cooperativism Consortium (platform.coop); Federated Co-
operatives Ltd. (Canada) selecting Invafresh's fresh retail platform; UCLA Anderson Review piece
on how grocery co-ops fit into the competitive landscape; USDA Rural Development paper on
federated cooperative relationships.

What the category looks like: A mix of true platform cooperatives, conventional platforms with a
co-op label, and federated co-op structures that predate the internet. The "platform" part is
uneven — some are genuinely digital platforms owned by the people they serve, some are digital
tooling for conventional co-ops, and some are federated co-ops using modern software.

Open questions this raises: Which existing food platforms are genuinely platform cooperatives in
the concept's sense, and which are conventional platforms with a different label? How does
Federated Co-operatives Ltd. (a large Canadian federated co-op) relate to the concept of a
federated open source grocery operation — is it a relevant precedent or a different thing? What
does the platform cooperativism movement offer the concept in terms of governance, ownership, and
digital architecture? Where is the "interoperability protocol" idea already alive in food?

## 3. Sourcing transparency and traceability

What exists: A large and active space of blockchain-based and non-blockchain food traceability,
provenance, and transparency projects. IBM Food Trust is a prominent example. Provenance (UK) does
blockchain-based ethical/sustainable sourcing verification with consumer-facing apps. Sourcemap does
supply chain mapping. Academic literature is substantial (systematic reviews of blockchain-based
food traceability frameworks). There is also a research thread on information needs for transparency
in blockchain-enabled food supply chains (Burgess 2024).

Named things that appeared: IBM Food Trust; Provenance; Sourcemap; Silal Fresh (blockchain
traceability case); the PMC systematic review of blockchain-based food traceability frameworks;
the ScienceDirect paper on information needs for transparency in blockchain-enabled supply chains;
TraceX / Tracextech.

What the category looks like: Heavily technology-driven (blockchain prominent, but not only),
often focused on safety, compliance, and premium claims (ethical, sustainable, organic). Consumer-
facing transparency exists (QR codes, apps) but is usually a feature of a conventional supply
chain rather than a structural redesign. The academic thread treats transparency as an information-
need problem.

Open questions this raises: How much of this is structural transparency (the concept's sense)
versus a transparency feature added to a closed supply chain? What is the depth of sourcing
information that actually reaches the eater, and in what form? Do these projects treat sourcing
information as a trade secret that is selectively revealed, or as information that belongs to the
eater? Where does the concept's sourcing layer overlap with this space, and where does it diverge
(e.g., the concept wants community-participatory sourcing knowledge, not just a traceability
ledger)? Is blockchain the right architectural reference, or is it mostly a vendor category?

## 4. Community-governed retail and participatory grocery

What exists: Food co-ops are the dominant example — democratically governed, member-owned, with
cooperative principles. Beyond co-ops, there are community-led grocery projects in food-access
contexts (Detroit People's Food Co-op, a Black-led and community-owned grocery cooperative; Mariposa
Food Cooperative in Philadelphia with a food-justice focus; Mandela Foods in Oakland, a worker-owned
cooperative; Apple Street Market in Cincinnati, a hybrid consumer/worker co-op; Village Market in
Portland; Dorchester Community Food Co-op in development). There is academic work on grocery
cooperatives as governing institutions in neighborhood commercial corridors (Zitcer 2019).
Cooperative principles and democracy-in-cooperatives resources exist (columinate.coop,
communityfood.coop).

Named things that appeared: Detroit People's Food Co-op; Mariposa Food Cooperative; Mandela Foods;
Apple Street Market; Village Market; Dorchester Community Food Co-op; OCGC (One Community Grocery
Co-op); the Zitcer 2019 article on grocery cooperatives as governing institutions; Food buying clubs
& co-ops (County Health Rankings).

What the category looks like: Participation and community governance are real and existing in the
co-op sector, especially at the local node level. The participation is often member governance (board
elections, membership meetings) and sometimes deeper (worker co-ops, hybrid models, community-led
development). There are explicit food-access and food-justice motivations in some projects.

Open questions this raises: How does existing co-op member participation compare to the concept's
internet-era participation layer (interactive, knowledge-producing, community-shaped information
environment)? What forms of participation actually exist, and which are real versus ceremonial?
How do co-ops handle the quality/abuse/manipulation question that the concept raises for
participatory environments? Where does participation shape sourcing, information, and structure in
existing co-ops — and where does it not? Is there a gap between member governance and the kind of
continuous participatory information environment the concept imagines?

## 5. Open-source and commons approaches to food

What exists: A real open-source and commons movement in the biological input side of food —
most prominently open-source seeds. The Open Source Seed Initiative (OSSI, US) creates a pool of
plant varieties under an open source pledge. Open Source Seeds (Europe) operates a seed commons.
There is academic literature on open-source seeds as a novel approach to safeguarding access to a
common good and preventing privatization, and on open-source seeds and the revitalization of local
knowledge. The concept's existing corpus already touches open-source seeds and biological commons.

Named things that appeared: Open Source Seed Initiative (osseeds.org); Open Source Seeds
(opensourceseeds.org, European); the Open Source Seed Licence (PMC article); MDPI article on open
source seeds and revitalization of local knowledge; P2P Foundation piece on seeds as a commons.

What the category looks like: A genuine open-source/commons movement exists in seeds and plant
breeding — the biological commons layer of the food system. This is adjacent to the grocery concept
rather than directly in it, but it is a real precedent for "open" applied to food, and it connects to
the sourcing layer (where food comes from and under what terms).

Open questions this raises: How much of the open-source/commons movement in food is in the inputs
(seeds, breeding) versus in the retail/distribution/consumption layers the concept is about? Does the
seed commons have anything to teach the grocery concept about open licensing, protected commons, and
preventing enclosure? Where do open-source seeds and the grocery concept's sourcing layer meet? Is
there a broader food commons movement that the concept should connect to?

## 6. Counter-concentration and regional/local food systems

What exists: Substantial research and practice around concentration in food retail and
distribution, local and regional food systems, food hubs, farm-to-table, and competition policy.
USDA ERS has comprehensive material on local foods. SARE has a farm-to-table / local-and-regional
food systems resource. FAO has material on the Data Food Consortium and short circuits. There is
academic work on corporate concentration and power in food systems (Clapp 2025). The federal
register has a 2022 notice on competition in food retail and distribution markets. Food hub
definitions and guidance exist (USDA, SARE).

Named things that appeared: Clapp 2025 paper on corporate concentration and power in food systems;
USDA ERS local foods overview; SARE farm-to-table resource; FAO Data Food Consortium material;
federal register notice on competition in food retail (2022); the Cambridge piece on what "local"
means in the grocery store.

What the category looks like: A well-developed research and policy space around concentration,
local/regional food systems, and food hubs as structural alternatives to consolidated supply chains.
Food hubs are a defined thing: centrally located businesses that coordinate aggregation, storage,
processing, distribution, and marketing of local/regional food. Short supply chains and "missing
middle" market structure are themes.

Open questions this raises: How do food hubs relate to the concept's federated node structure — are
they a relevant precedent for the sourcing/logistics layer, or a different thing? What does the
concentration research teach the concept about the structural environment a grocery operation has to
operate in? Where do local/regional food systems embody the concept's community-benefit and counter-
concentration commitments — and where do they fall short of the internet-era posture? Is there an
interoperability or protocol layer already in food hubs, or is it mostly point-to-point relationships?

## 7. Emergent: food data interoperability and open protocols in food

What exists: A real and active food data interoperability space that surfaced during the food-hubs
search and is directly relevant to the concept's federation and interoperability questions. The
Open Food Network (OFN) is a prominent open-source food commerce platform with a community and a data
interoperability project. The Data Food Consortium (DFC) is developing a shared open data standard for
short food supply chains to enable interoperability between farmers, marketplaces, and other supply
chain actors. OpenTEAM is a collaborative network advancing open, interoperable agricultural tools
that keep farmers in control of their data. There is a Québec project building a shared open-source
web portal to let food hubs and producers federate data and coordinate supply chains without
sacrificing data control.

Named things that appeared: Open Food Network (OFN, openfoodnetwork.ca and openfoodnetwork.org.uk);
Data Food Consortium (DFC) open standard; OpenTEAM; the OFN UK "food data collaboration" project; the
OFN community discussion on the DFC making food platforms interoperable.

What the category looks like: This is the closest existing thing to the concept's federation and
interoperability layer — open-source food commerce platforms, shared data standards for short supply
chains, and projects explicitly about interoperability between food platforms without a single central
operator controlling the data. OFN is an open-source platform for food commerce with a community around
it. DFC is a shared open data language for short circuits.

Open questions this raises: How much of this is genuinely open and interoperable versus a platform with
an open-source label? Does OFN embody the concept's architecture in any meaningful way — open-source
platform, community, federation, interoperability — or is it a different thing? What does the DFC open
standard teach the concept about shared protocols for food data? How do these projects handle the
governance, participation, and community-benefit questions? Are these the right precedents to study
deeply, or are they adjacent and incomplete?

## Overall shape of the scan

### What is real and substantial

- A mature food co-op sector with real participation, governance, community benefit, and a
  federated buying/distribution structure (NCG, distribution co-ops).
- A real platform cooperativism movement and some food-adjacent platform cooperatives.
- A large sourcing-transparency/traceability space, heavily technology-driven, often feature-of-a-
  closed-chain rather than structural redesign.
- Real community-governed and participatory grocery projects, especially in food-access and food-
  justice contexts.
- A genuine open-source/commons movement in seeds and plant breeding.
- Substantial research and practice around concentration, local/regional food systems, and food hubs.
- A real food data interoperability space (OFN, DFC, OpenTEAM) that is the closest existing thing to
  the concept's federation/interoperability layer.

### What the scan suggests about the concept's layers

- **Sourcing layer:** real precedents in co-op sourcing, food hubs, traceability projects, and open-
  source seeds; the concept's distinct move is participatory, community-owned sourcing knowledge rather
  than a traceability feature.
- **Information layer:** traceability projects address this but usually as a feature; the concept's
  move is richer, interactive, community-shaped information.
- **Culture layer:** under-addressed in this scan — co-ops and local food movements touch culture but
  rarely as a structural layer; this is a gap worth deeper study.
- **Participation layer:** real in co-op member governance; the concept's move toward continuous,
  knowledge-producing, interactive participation is a step beyond most existing practice.
- **Structure/economics layer:** co-ops embody community ownership and democratic governance;
  federation exists in NCG and Federated Co-operatives Ltd.; platform cooperatives address ownership;
  the concept's internet-era federation and interoperability posture is a distinct move.
- **Interoperability/federation layer:** OFN, DFC, and OpenTEAM are the most relevant existing
  precedents; this is where the concept's internet-era architectural questions are most directly
  testable against the real.

### What the scan raises for deeper dives

1. Food co-ops in depth — how participation, transparency, and community benefit actually work in
   existing co-ops, where they fall short of the concept, and what NCG's federation teaches.
2. Open Food Network and the Data Food Consortium — the most direct existing test of the concept's
   federation and interoperability layer; need to understand what they built, how they govern, and
   where they fall short.
3. Platform cooperatives in food — which are genuine, which are adjacent, what the platform
   cooperativism movement offers.
4. Sourcing transparency projects — distinguishing structural transparency from a transparency feature;
   what depth of sourcing information actually reaches the eater.
5. Community-governed and participatory grocery in food-access contexts — Detroit People's, Mandela,
   Mariposa, Apple Street; what participatory governance looks like on the ground.
6. Culture as a structural layer — this came up as under-addressed in the scan; deeper study needed on
   how food co-ops and local food movements handle food culture, Indigenous foodways, diaspora
   traditions, and cultural meaning.
7. Concentration and counter-concentration — the structural environment the concept has to operate in;
   what the research teaches and what the concept learns.
8. Open-source seeds and biological commons — adjacent but real; what it teaches about open licensing,
   protected commons, and enclosure.

### What the scan does not yet address

- Canadian-specific food co-op and alternative grocery landscape (the existing corpus is Canada-
  centred, so this matters).
- The culture layer in any depth (only surfaced as a gap).
- Concrete economics and viability of alternative grocery models.
- Whether any existing operation combines several of the concept's layers in one — most precedents
  address one or two layers, not the full stack.

## Next step

This scan is a surface pass. It suggests a set of deeper-dive targets, with OFN/DFC and the food co-
op sector as the most directly relevant. The culture layer is flagged as under-addressed and worthy of
a dedicated pass. The scan should be extended with Canadian-specific material and with anything that
combines multiple layers rather than one.
