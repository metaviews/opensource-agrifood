## NCG figures — corroborated and updated

The NCG 2024 Food Co-op Impact Report PDF at ncg.coop/sites/default/files/
documents/NCG_Food_Co-op_Impact_Report_2024_Public.pdf returns "Page not
found." The figures are nevertheless corroborated across multiple public
sources and are stable:

- NCG's own newsroom page "Food Co-ops Credit Community Ownership and Local
  Focus Success" repeats the 2024 framing. (ncg.coop/newsroom/food-co-ops-
  credit-community-ownership-and-local-focus-success)
- grocery.coop has a "Food Co-op Impact Report 2025" page with a downloadable
  PDF, indicating the report series continues.
  (grocery.coop/article/food-co-op-impact-report-2025/)
- A newer NCBA CLUSA blog gives 2025 figures: NCG food co-ops operate 244
  stores and experienced sales growth of 6.2% in 2025, more than double the
  average U.S. grocery store benchmark of 2.9% (RetailStat.com data); 38% of
  combined $2.8B sales from Certified Organic (2025 SPINS); Fair Trade
  Certified 5% of total sales (vs 3% other natural, 0.4% conventional);
  cooperatively produced products 5% (vs 3%, 1%). This updates the 2024
  figures (4.7% growth, 37% organic, 6% Fair Trade, 5% co-op-produced) and
  shows the sector still growing.
  (ncbaclusa.coop/blog/food-co-ops-lead-in-sales-of-local-organic-and-fair-
  trade-products/)
- NCG's About page now says 169 food co-ops operating 241 stores in 40 states
  with combined annual sales over $2.8 billion and serving over 1.3 million
  consumer-owners — slightly up from the 2024 report summary's 165 co-ops,
  240+ stores, 39 states. (ncg.coop/about-us)

**Finding.** The NCG figures are stable and corroborated, not dependent on
reading the PDF. The sector is small (~$2.8B, ~169 co-ops, ~240 stores, 1.3M
members) and growing modestly (4.7% in 2024, 6.2% in 2025, vs ~2.9-3%
benchmark). The grocery.coop 2025 report page confirms the report series is
ongoing. The 2024 PDF being unreachable is a minor access problem, not a data
problem.

The 2024 figures, from the NCBA CLUSA blog (15 April 2025) and MMFC summary
(3 October 2025):

- **Scale.** NCG is owned by 165 member food co-ops operating more than 240
  storefronts in 39 states. Combined annual sales of $2.8 billion. More than
  1.3 million U.S. residents belong to an NCG food co-op.
- **Growth.** Same-store sales growth of 4.7% in 2024, versus the overall U.S.
  retail food market of under 3%.
- **Sourcing.** The average NCG food co-op purchases from 175 local farms and
  producers — more than four times the 41 farms reported on average by similarly
  sized food retailers. 24% of the average food co-op's sales come from local
  products, versus 2% at a conventional grocer. (Citing FMI's "The Food
  Retailing Industry Speaks 2023" for the conventional comparison.)
- **Organic.** 37% of food co-ops' combined $2.8B sales from Certified Organic,
  versus 24% at other natural grocery retailers and 3% at conventional grocers
  (2024 SPINS data).
- **Fair Trade / B Corp / co-op-produced.** Fair Trade Certified 6% of total
  sales (vs 4% other natural, 0.5% conventional). B Corp Certified 9% (vs 6%,
  2%). Cooperatively produced products 5% (vs 3%, 1%).
- **Wages.** Half of NCG's community-owned food co-ops pay all staff a livable
  wage or according to their union contract.
- **Food access.** 77 of NCG's 165 member co-ops are in USDA-designated low-
  access/low-income areas.
- **Advocacy and solidarity funding.** Food co-ops collectively donated more than
  $9 million to local groups; NCG contributed an additional $1 million for
  advocacy. Specific donations: more than $139,000 to North American Traditional
  Indigenous Food Systems (NĀTIFS); support for National Black Food and Justice
  Alliance, Federation of Southern Cooperatives, HEAL Food Alliance, Alianza
  Nacional de Campesinas, Agricultural Justice Project, National Farm to School
  Network.

## How co-op ownership and governance work

- **Ownership.** A food co-op is a grocery store owned by the community members
  who shop there. Members make a one-time investment in a lifetime membership —
  usually a $100–200 purchase of stock — and then elect a board of directors from
  among themselves. (grocery.coop "What Is a Food Co-op?"; NCBA CLUSA blog)
- **One-member-one-vote.** "One of the seven cooperative principles is that every
  member has a vote. Member-owners, not external stakeholders, determine how the
  co-op operates and spends..." (NCBA CLUSA, "Food and Grocery Co-ops"). The
  ISED-ISDE Canada page says: "The 'one member, one vote' principle ensures
  participatory management. Each member actively participates in the annual general
  meeting (AGM), where important decisions are made and the board of directors is
  elected." (ised-isde.canada.ca)
- **Profits.** Members can receive patronage dividends if the business makes a
  profit; members typically receive financial rewards like discounts and coupons.
  (grocery.coop; Food Secure Canada 2024 piece)
- **Values framing.** "Food co-ops don't change their values depending on which
  way the wind blows. They are rooted in their communities and participate in the
  international cooperative business movement, a business model driven by democratic
  ownership." (NCBA CLUSA blog) "Because community ownership of local food systems
  is what co-ops are all about, we support food sovereignty for all communities..."
  (MMFC summary)
- **Distribution co-ops.** PCC's "Grocery story" piece (soundconsumer.com, 2020)
  describes how distribution co-ops were established to increase buying power of
  individual co-ops and centralize food storage and distribution — a federated
  structure that predates the internet.

## The participation gap

The concept wants continuous, knowledge-producing, interactive participation — an
information environment that people contribute to and shape. The existing co-op
sector's participation is real but largely intermittent, formal, and member-only.
The gap is well-documented.

- **Intermittent and formal.** The dominant form of consumer-member participation
  is the AGM and board elections: "Each member actively participates in the annual
  general meeting (AGM), where important decisions are made and the board of
  directors is elected." (ISED-ISDE Canada) Member participation outside the AGM
  is the exception and is a known struggle.
- **Low consumer-member involvement.** Hale 2018 ("Digging into the process of
  cooperation in food and...", Food Systems Journal, cited 37 times) is summarized
  by search as finding "little to no involvement of the consumer members, which are
  the largest member class of the co-op." This is the clearest academic statement
  of the gap.
- **The engagement struggle is normal, not exceptional.** Columinate's "Using the
  Board's Unique Perspective to Increase Owner Engagement and Participation" says:
  "Directors commonly lament their lack of active engagement with owners, who as a
  rule are difficult to entice to annual meetings or other member engagement..."
  (columinate.coop). The Food Co-op Initiative's "Engaging Members with Members"
  piece says organizers of food co-op startups "intuitively understand the need for
  engagement." (archives.grocer.coop). Cooperatives First's "What the heck is member
  engagement?" says co-ops need to plan for member engagement and ensure they are
  providing meaningful opportunities. (cooperativesfirst.com)
- **Treating participation as a duty is counterproductive.** A Facebook group post
  (co-op sector) says: "Treating participation as a member duty can easily lead to
  more conflict and higher level costs. Unfortunately, people who are forced to..."
  (facebook.com/groups/20082993008). This is sector folk knowledge that the concept
  should take seriously: mandating participation produces conflict.
- **The concept's move is a step beyond intermittent formal governance.** The
  Columinate "Democracy in Cooperatives" piece (Healy 2005) frames democracy as "a
  series of actions" and as "the conversation and argument that precedes and follows
  the choice," citing de Tocqueville, Zinn, and Pobihushchy: "Popular elections in
  and of themselves do not a democracy make. Free and open discussion, deliberation,
  and consultation are essential preconditions to elections as democratic elements."
  This is the best existing articulation of what genuine participatory interaction
  could be, and it is still framed around the formal governance machinery (board
  elections, membership meetings) rather than a continuous community information and
  participation environment. The concept's interaction layer wants to extend this
  beyond the formal machinery into the everyday information and decision environment
  of the operation — without collapsing into the duty/forced-participation problem
  the sector already knows about.

## Canada context

- ISED-ISDE's "Understanding co-operatives: How they work, types and contributions"
  is the federal government's plain-language explanation of co-op structure in
  Canada (democratic governance, one-member-one-vote, AGM, board elected by members).
  (ised-isde.canada.ca)
- Food Secure Canada's 2024 piece "Toward fair and sustainable food systems: the
  role of food cooperatives and solidarity grocery stores" frames co-ops and
  solidarity grocery stores as part of fair and sustainable food systems.
  (foodsecurecanada.org, 28 March 2024)
- "Food Cooperatives in Canada" (ResearchGate) lists governance questions including
  "What role does the management have in the governance of your co-operative?" and
  "What role do members play in the..." — i.e., Canadian co-op governance is an
  actual research topic, and member role is an open question there too.
  (researchgate.net)
- The Co-operative Retailing System (CRS) "About" page: "Each Co-op is governed by
  a board of directors elected by and accountable to the Co-op's members. These
  representatives are responsible for setting policies..." (co-op.crs) — a large
  Canadian consumer co-op system with the standard structure.
- Canadian Worker Co-op Federation's "Examples of Consumer Co-operatives" notes
  that "Typically in larger co-ops, these roles are delegated to management through
  the board of directors which is elected by and made up of members."
  (canadianworker.coop) — the delegation problem is visible in Canada too.

**Finding for the concept.** Canadian co-ops follow the same structural pattern as
U.S. co-ops (member ownership, one-member-one-vote, board elected by members,
management delegated in larger co-ops). The participation gap is the same. The
Canada-specific signal is thinner than the U.S. NCG signal — fewer numbers, fewer
named contemporary reports — which is itself a reason to go deeper on Canadian co-ops
separately.

## Viability and competitive landscape

- UCLA Anderson Review (Totty, 24 March 2021) summarizes a paper by Korpeoglu
  (Eindhoven), Körpeoğlu (UCL), Tang (UCLA), and Yu (Fudan): "Entry of Not-for-
  Profit Food Cooperatives and Its Implications on For-Profit Retailers" (SSRN
  3683380; published in MSOM as "Supply Chain Competition: A Market Game Approach").
  The model distinguishes two co-op social missions:
  - **Community co-ops** — higher prices, strive for wider profit margins to support
    local healthy meal programs, cultural events, other community activities. More
    likely to be tolerated by for-profit chains focused on sales volume.
  - **People's co-ops** — aim for higher sales volume at the expense of profit
    margins — creating jobs, sourcing from local producers, supporting the local
    economy. More likely to trigger a tough competitive response from for-profit
    chains, which will slash prices to keep a co-op out.
- The paper notes co-ops remain a tiny part of the U.S. grocery scene: about $2.3
  billion in revenues (NCG figure at the time), ~147 co-ops operating ~200 stores,
  versus Kroger's 2,742 stores and $132.5 billion in 2020 sales. (UCLA Anderson
  Review)
- Reality on price: "surveys suggest co-op prices are higher on some items, such as
  dairy and frozen foods, and lower on produce and bulk goods." The co-op industry
  acknowledges: "There are highly visible products that we may price significantly
  higher than the competition." (archives.grocer.coop, "strategic pricing") Higher-
  priced co-ops succeed in part by attracting socially conscious shoppers less
  sensitive to price; the satisfaction of shopping at a co-op acts as "a kind of
  invisible discount." (UCLA Anderson Review)

**Finding for the concept.** The viability question is real and the academic framing
is useful: a grocery operation optimized for community benefit and participation has
to choose, in part, which mission it foregrounds and what competitive response that
invites. The concept's claim that it is "optimized for participation, transparency,
and community benefit" is not automatically viable — the co-op sector's mixed record
(real growth and community benefit, but tiny share of the market and price
disadvantages on some items) is the evidence. The concept should name this rather than
assume a community-benefiting operation is viable by default.
