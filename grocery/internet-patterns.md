# Internet-era patterns applied to food

A short catalog of the internet-era structural patterns that an open source grocery operation draws on, and what each one means when translated from information to food.

## Open protocols over closed platforms

On the internet: the network scaled because it ran on open protocols that anyone could implement — TCP/IP, DNS, HTTP, SMTP — rather than on a single platform that controlled the rules. You could build your own node and join the network without asking a central operator for permission.

Applied to food: the grocery operation is built on interoperable protocols — for sourcing information, for the information environment, for participation, for economics — that any participating node can implement. A community can form its own node and join the network without asking a central operator for permission. The network grows by nodes joining, not by a central operator adding locations.

What this asks of the concept: what are the protocols? What do nodes share, what do they keep local, how do they interoperate? This is the hardest and most important design question, and it is not yet answered.

## Decentralized production and participation

On the internet: a large number of people can produce and participate without going through a single central gatekeeper. A web page, a blog, a comment, a fork, a new site — production is distributed.

Applied to food: the eater is a producer of knowledge, context, and participation, not just a consumer of choices. Sourcing knowledge, local context, experience, corrections, recommendations — these are produced by the community, not just by the operation. The operation is designed to receive and use that production, not just to present its own curated environment.

What this asks of the concept: how does the operation receive and use community production without collapsing into noise, abuse, or manipulation? How does it distinguish genuine participation from strategic distortion? The participation layer has to carry this question.

## Transparency as structural, not decorative

On the internet: provenance, metadata, and traceability are structural features of the systems that carry information — not after-the-fact transparency reports added to a closed system. You can see where a piece of information came from because the system carries that information by design.

Applied to food: sourcing, economics, and chain information are structural parts of the operation — surfaced by default, not hidden and revealed only on request. The operation does not publish a transparency report; it is built so that the information is there for anyone who wants it.

What this asks of the concept: at what depth is sourcing information available for different kinds of products? How does the operation handle the fact that some sourcing chains are genuinely opaque and some are not fully known even to the operation? The sourcing layer has to carry this question without pretending the answer is simple.

## Forkability and exit

On the internet: if you don't like a platform, you can often take your data and go elsewhere, or build your own instance. The possibility of exit is a structural feature of the network, not an afterthought.

Applied to food: a community can form its own node, carry its own sourcing relationships, and leave without losing its history or relationships. The operation is not a lock-in. The possibility of exit is a structural feature, not an afterthought.

What this asks of the concept: what does a community take with it when it leaves or forms a new node? Its sourcing relationships? Its participation history? Its information? The structure layer has to answer this, and the answer has consequences for how the network is built.

## Interoperability as a scaling mechanism

On the internet: instances interoperate — you can send mail from one provider to another, link from one site to another, move between providers without losing access to the network. The network grows without a single central operator because the pieces can talk to each other.

Applied to food: local nodes carry local sourcing relationships and community preferences, and they interoperate through shared protocols so that the network grows without a single central operator. A comment or a sourcing fact from one node can be useful in another. A community can benefit from the network's shared knowledge without giving up its local autonomy.

What this asks of the concept: what interoperates, at what level, and with what fidelity? Sourcing information, obviously. Information environment, probably. Participation mechanisms, maybe — but participation is also local, and the tension between local participation and network sharing has to be worked out.

## Layered architecture

On the internet: the network is layered — physical, transport, application, content — and each layer has its own rules and development. Layers can be developed, substituted, and combined independently.

Applied to food: the operation is layered — sourcing, information, participation, structure and economics — and each layer has its own rules and development. The layers are distinct but interoperable. A node can develop its sourcing layer without rebuilding its information layer. The network can develop its interoperability protocols without dictating every local decision.

What this asks of the concept: what are the layers, what does each one do, and how do they interoperate? The current sketch has four layers. That may be right, or it may be too many, or too few. The layers are a way of keeping the concept from collapsing into a single undifferentiated "open grocery" idea.

## The internet is not a grocery operation

A caution. The internet is a network for information. A grocery operation is a system for moving food from producers to eaters, with money, logistics, biology, and a concentrated surrounding system in the way. The internet-era patterns are a guide, not a template. They do not solve the problem; they suggest a way of thinking about the structure.

The concept is strongest when it can say what the internet-era pattern actually means in the food context, what it can and cannot do, and what the food-specific tensions are that the pattern does not address.
