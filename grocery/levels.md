# The levels of open in a grocery operation

A working-through of what "open" means when it is applied to a grocery operation as a whole, rather than as a label on a single product or a transparency report on a closed system.

## 1. Open sourcing

The operation can say, for any given product, where it came from — not just the brand and the country of origin, but the farm, the producer, the cooperative, the processor, the transport route, and the conditions under which it was grown, raised, made, or handled.

This is not the same as a traceability badge. It is the principle that sourcing is treated as information that belongs to the eater rather than as a proprietary chain that the operation protects. The depth of sourcing information varies by product and by context, but the direction is consistent: more transparency, not less, and no hidden layer that the operation treats as its competitive secret.

This level also includes the economics of sourcing: what the operation paid, what that means in relation to the broader food-dollar distribution the existing record documents, and what the operation's purchasing does to the people at the other end. Open sourcing without open economics is a partial gesture.

## 2. Open information

The information environment around a product is richer than a nutrition label and an ingredient list. It includes:

- Producer and sourcing context (the open sourcing layer, surfaced as information)
- News relevant to the product or its category — what is happening with the crop, the region, the regulatory environment, the price structure
- Comments and discussion from other eaters — questions, experiences, corrections, local knowledge
- Alternatives and comparisons — other products, other sources, other ways of meeting the same need
- The ability to ask questions and get answers that are not pre-scripted marketing copy

This level draws directly on the food-side frames in the existing record. The biochemical-reductionist model (nutrition labels, percent daily intake, ingredient lists) is not wrong, but it is incomplete: it misreads what actually drives food choice, which encodes identity, class, memory, culture, and social position. An open information environment takes that seriously. It does not replace the biochemical frame; it surrounds it with the social and material context that the biochemical frame omits.

This level also means the information is interactive, not broadcast. The operation is not a television channel presenting a curated set of choices with limited context. It is a media environment where information can be added to, questioned, and developed by the people who use it.

## 3. Culture

Food is not only a material substance with a sourcing chain and an information environment. It is also a cultural substance — it carries identity, memory, tradition, community, ritual, and belonging. The culture level is the recognition that a grocery operation has to be legible as food, not only as a set of commodities with transparency labels attached.

The culture level includes:

- The cultural contexts in which food is produced — Indigenous foodways, diaspora food traditions, regional food cultures, family and community food practices
- The cultural meanings that food carries for the people who eat it — what a food means in a community, a family, a tradition, a ritual
- The information and participation environment that surrounds food with cultural meaning — context that is cultural rather than purely material
- The recognition that food cultures are lived, specific, and often sensitive, and that a grocery operation has to handle food cultures that are not its own without flattening or appropriating them

This level is closely related to the open information level, but it is distinct: information can be factual and material without being cultural, and a grocery operation can have rich sourcing and news information while still treating food as a commodity. The culture level is what keeps the operation from doing that.

In a federated structure, the culture level is local by default — each community's food culture is its own — but it is also the level through which communities can share and learn from each other's food cultures, where it is welcome and appropriate to do so. The culture level is the level the operation has to be most careful with, because culture is not a uniform thing that can be abstracted and shared without loss.

## 4. Open participation

The eater is not a passive recipient. They can contribute to the information environment: questions, corrections, local knowledge, experience-based ratings, recommendations grounded in actual use, and the kind of community knowledge that a broadcast model treats as noise.

This is not the same as a review system grafted onto a conventional retail operation. A conventional review system is usually a thin layer of star ratings and short text, governed by the platform's rules and optimized for the platform's purposes. Open participation means the information environment is genuinely shaped by the people who use it — including the capacity to surface information that the operation did not anticipate and would not have produced on its own.

This level also includes participation in the operation's decisions where that is appropriate: what it carries, what it highlights, what it changes, what it stops carrying, how it handles a supply problem, how it responds to a price shock. The degree and form of participation will vary — this is not a claim that every eater governs every decision — but the direction is toward a more participatory structure than the conventional broadcast model allows.

## 4. Open structure

The operation itself is not a black box. Its rules, its economics, its decision-making, and its relationship to the broader food system are things that can be understood and, where appropriate, shaped by the people who participate in it.

This is the level at which the concept meets the structural-omissions frame most directly. The existing record documents a food system in which five grocery chains control 80% of retail, two firms control 95% of beef processing, and farmers receive roughly 11% of the food dollar. Concentration is not a neutral starting point; it is a structural condition that shapes every choice inside the system. An open source grocery operation is, among other things, a proposition about what a grocery operation looks like when it is not structured as a consolidated player in a concentrated system.

Open structure does not mean the operation has no structure, no ownership, no decisions, no economics. It means those things are not hidden. It means the operation's relationship to the broader food system — its suppliers, its workers, its customers, its community, its competitors, its funders, its own incentives — is informable and, in some respects, shapeable by the people involved.

## How the levels relate

The concept is strongest when the levels reinforce each other.

Open sourcing makes open information meaningful: if you can see where a product came from, the information environment around it has something real to work with. Open information invites open participation: if the information is rich and interactive, people have something to contribute to. Open participation, sustained over time, shapes open structure: a participating community is not just a user base, and a media environment that people contribute to is not just a broadcast channel.

The concept is weakest when the levels are decoupled. A transparency label on a closed operation is open sourcing without open structure. A review system grafted onto a conventional retailer is open participation without open information or open sourcing. A sourcing page with no community and no participation is open information without open participation. These are partial gestures, and the concept is interested in the stronger combination.

## What this is not

This is not a claim that every grocery operation should be a platform. It is not a claim that open source grocery is a software problem. It is not a claim that transparency alone solves the structural problems of the food system. It is not a claim that the internet-era model is automatically better than the broadcast model in every respect — only that it is a different model, and that the differences are worth thinking through on their own terms.

The concept is strongest when it can name what it is actually proposing, what it is actually changing, and what remains difficult or unresolved.
