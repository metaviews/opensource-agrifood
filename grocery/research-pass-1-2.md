## Target 1 — Food co-ops and the participation gap

### 1a. The NCG model in numbers (US, contemporary)

The most concrete recent data comes from National Co+op Grocers' 2024 Food Co-op Impact Report and its public summary (NCBA CLUSA blog, 15 April 2025; MMFC summary, 3 October 2025).

- **Scale.** NCG is owned by 165 member food co-ops operating more than 240 storefronts in 39 states. Combined annual sales of $2.8 billion. More than 1.3 million U.S. residents belong to an NCG food co-op. (NCBA CLUSA blog)
- **Growth.** NCG member co-ops achieved same-store sales growth of 4.7 percent in 2024, versus the overall U.S. retail food market of under 3 percent. (NCBA CLUSA blog)
- **Sourcing.** The average NCG food co-op purchases from 175 local farms and producers — more than four times the 41 farms reported on average by similarly sized food retailers. Twenty-four percent of the average food co-op's sales come from local products, versus 2 percent at a conventional grocer. (NCBA CLUSA blog, citing FMI's "The Food Retailing Industry Speaks 2023" for the conventional comparison)
- **Organic.** Thirty-seven percent of food co-ops' combined $2.8 billion sales come from Certified Organic products, versus 24 percent at other natural grocery retailers and 3 percent at conventional grocers (2024 SPINS data). (NCBA CLUSA blog)
- **Fair Trade / B Corp / co-op-produced.** Fair Trade Certified products made up 6 percent of total sales (vs 4 percent other natural, 0.5 percent conventional). B Corp Certified products made up 9 percent (vs 6 percent, 2 percent). Cooperatively produced products made up 5 percent (vs 3 percent, 1 percent). (NCBA CLUSA blog)
- **Wages.** Half of NCG's community-owned food co-ops pay all staff a livable wage or according to their union contract. (MMFC summary)
- **Food access.** Seventy-seven of NCG's 165 member co-ops are located in parts of the country designated as having low access to fresh foods and a low median household income (USDA designation). (MMFC summary)
- **Advocacy and solidarity funding.** Food co-ops collectively donated more than $9 million to local groups in the reported year; NCG contributed an additional $1 million to organizations advancing co-op advocacy priorities. Specific donations highlighted: more than $139,000 to North American Traditional Indigenous Food Systems (NĀTIFS) based on Field Day brand sales in November; support for National Black Food and Justice Alliance, Federation of Southern Cooperatives, HEAL Food Alliance, Alianza Nacional de Campesinas, Agricultural Justice Project, National Farm to School Network. (MMFC summary)

### 1b. How co-op ownership and governance work (the structural baseline)

- **Ownership.** A food co-op is a grocery store owned by the community members who shop there. Members make a one-time investment in a lifetime membership — usually a $100–200 purchase of stock — and then elect a board of directors from among themselves. (grocery.coop "What Is a Food Co-op?"; NCBA CLUSA blog)
- **One-member-one-vote.** "One of the seven cooperative principles is that every member has a vote. Member-owners, not external stakeholders, determine how the co-op operates and spends..." (NCBA CLUSA, "Food and Grocery Co-ops"). The ISED-ISDE Canada page on co-ops says: "The 'one member, one vote' principle ensures participatory management. Each member actively participates in the annual general meeting (AGM), where important decisions are made and the board of directors is elected." (ised-isde.canada.ca)
- **Profits.** Members can receive patronage dividends if the business makes a profit; members typically receive financial rewards like discounts and coupons. (grocery.coop; Food Secure Canada 2024 piece)
- **Values framing.** "Food co-ops don't change their values depending on which way the wind blows. They are rooted in their communities and participate in the international cooperative business movement, a business model driven by democratic ownership." (NCBA CLUSA blog) "Because community ownership of local food systems is what co-ops are all about, we support food sovereignty for all communities..." (MMFC summary)
- **Distribution co-ops.** PCC's "Grocery story" piece (soundconsumer.com, 2020) describes how distribution co-ops were established to increase buying power of individual co-ops and centralize food storage and distribution — a federated structure that predates the internet. This is corroborated by the general federation pattern.

### 1c. The participation gap — what the concept's "interaction" layer is a step beyond

The concept wants continuous, knowledge-producing, interactive participation — an information environment that people contribute to and shape. The existing co-op sector's participation is real but largely intermittent, formal, and member-only. The gap is well-documented.

- **Intermittent and formal.** The dominant form of consumer-member participation is the annual general meeting and board elections: "Each member actively participates in the annual general meeting (AGM), where important decisions are made and the board of directors is elected." (ISED-ISDE Canada) Member participation outside the AGM is the exception and is a known struggle.
- **Low consumer-member involvement.** Hale 2018 ("Digging into the process of cooperation in food and...", Food Systems Journal, cited 37 times) is summarized by search as finding "little to no involvement of the consumer members, which are the largest member class of the co-op." This is the clearest academic statement of the gap.
- **The engagement struggle is normal, not exceptional.** Columinate's "Using the Board's Unique Perspective to Increase Owner Engagement and Participation" says: "Directors commonly lament their lack of active engagement with owners, who as a rule are difficult to entice to annual meetings or other member engagement..." (columinate.coop). The Food Co-op Initiative's "Engaging Members with Members" piece says organizers of food co-op startups "intuitively understand the need for engagement." (archives.grocer.coop). Cooperatives First's "What the heck is member engagement?" says co-ops need to plan for member engagement and ensure they are providing meaningful opportunities. (cooperativesfirst.com)
- **Treating participation as a duty is counterproductive.** A Facebook group post (co-op sector) says: "Treating participation as a member duty can easily lead to more conflict and higher level costs. Unfortunately, people who are forced to..." (facebook.com/groups/20082993008). This is sector folk knowledge that the concept should take seriously: mandating participation produces conflict.
- **The concept's move is a step beyond intermittent formal governance.** The Columinate "Democracy in Cooperatives" piece (Healy 2005) frames democracy as "a series of actions" and as "the conversation and argument that precedes and follows the choice," citing de Tocqueville, Zinn, and Pobihushchy: "Popular elections in and of themselves do not a democracy make. Free and open discussion, deliberation, and consultation are essential preconditions to elections as democratic elements." This is the best existing articulation of what genuine participatory interaction could be, and it is still framed around the formal governance machinery (board elections, membership meetings) rather than a continuous community information and participation environment. The concept's interaction layer wants to extend this beyond the formal machinery into the everyday information and decision environment of the operation — without collapsing into the duty/forced-participation problem the sector already knows about.

### 1d. Canada context (why it matters for the corpus)

- ISED-ISDE's "Understanding co-operatives: How they work, types and contributions" is the federal government's plain-language explanation of co-op structure in Canada (democratic governance, one-member-one-vote, AGM, board elected by members). (ised-isde.canada.ca)
- Food Secure Canada's 2024 piece "Toward fair and sustainable food systems: the role of food cooperatives and solidarity grocery stores" frames co-ops and solidarity grocery stores as part of fair and sustainable food systems. (foodsecurecanada.org, 28 March 2024)
- "Food Cooperatives in Canada" (ResearchGate) is a research piece that lists governance questions including "What role does the management have in the governance of your co-operative?" and "What role do members play in the..." — i.e., Canadian co-op governance is an actual research topic, and member role is an open question there too. (researchgate.net)
- The Co-operative Retailing System (CRS) "About" page: "Each Co-op is governed by a board of directors elected by and accountable to the Co-op's members. These representatives are responsible for setting policies..." (co-op.crs) — a large Canadian consumer co-op system with the standard structure.
- Canadian Worker Co-op Federation's "Examples of Consumer Co-operatives" notes that "Typically in larger co-ops, these roles are delegated to management through the board of directors which is elected by and made up of members." (canadianworker.coop) — the delegation problem is visible in Canada too.

**Finding for the concept.** Canadian co-ops follow the same structural pattern as U.S. co-ops (member ownership, one-member-one-vote, board elected by members, management delegated in larger co-ops). The participation gap is the same. The Canada-specific signal is thinner than the U.S. NCG signal — fewer numbers, fewer named contemporary reports — which is itself a reason to go deeper on Canadian co-ops separately.

### 1e. Viability and competitive landscape (what the co-op sector teaches about whether an open, community-benefiting operation can survive)

- UCLA Anderson Review (Totty, 24 March 2021) summarizes a working paper by Korpeoglu (Eindhoven), Körpeoğlu (UCL), Tang (UCLA), and Yu (Fudan): "Entry of Not-for-Profit Food Cooperatives and Its Implications on For-Profit Retailers" (SSRN 3683380). The model distinguishes two co-op social missions:
  - **Community co-ops** — higher prices, strive for wider profit margins to support local healthy meal programs, cultural events, other community activities. More likely to be tolerated by for-profit chains focused on sales volume.
  - **People's co-ops** — aim for higher sales volume at the expense of profit margins — creating jobs, sourcing from local producers, supporting the local economy. More likely to trigger a tough competitive response from for-profit chains, which will slash prices to keep a co-op out.
- The paper notes co-ops remain a tiny part of the U.S. grocery scene: about $2.3 billion in revenues (NCG figure at the time), ~147 co-ops operating ~200 stores, versus Kroger's 2,742 stores and $132.5 billion in 2020 sales. (UCLA Anderson Review)
- Reality on price: "surveys suggest co-op prices are higher on some items, such as dairy and frozen foods, and lower on produce and bulk goods." The co-op industry acknowledges: "There are highly visible products that we may price significantly higher than the competition." (archives.grocer.coop, "strategic pricing") Higher-priced co-ops succeed in part by attracting socially conscious shoppers less sensitive to price; the satisfaction of shopping at a co-op acts as "a kind of invisible discount." (UCLA Anderson Review)

**Finding for the concept.** The viability question is real and the academic framing is useful: a grocery operation optimized for community benefit and participation has to choose, in part, which mission it foregrounds and what competitive response that invites. The concept's claim that it is "optimized for participation, transparency, and community benefit" is not automatically viable — the co-op sector's mixed record (real growth and community benefit, but tiny share of the market and price disadvantages on some items) is the evidence. The concept should name this rather than assume a community-benefiting operation is viable by default.

## Target 2 — Open Food Network and the Data Food Consortium

### 2a. Open Food Network — what it is, concretely

- **Identity.** OFN is "an international not-for-profit focused on creating the tools and resources needed to build food systems that are fair, local, and transparent." Its flagship project is "an open source software platform that makes it easy to create innovative, independent, community food enterprises." (openfoodnetwork.org/about-us)
- **Open source and Commons.** "Open Food Network operates on an open and transparent governance model. This is why we create open source solutions, communicate with transparency, and hold everything we create as part of the Commons — it doesn't belong to a single entity, but instead to a community." (openfoodnetwork.org/about-us)
- **Global and locally-led.** "Open Food Network was founded in 2012 in Australia but is now an international community that is locally-led across the world." (openfoodnetwork.org/about-us) OFN Canada launched in 2017 and is most active in Ontario. (openfoodnetwork.org/find-your-local-open-food-network)
- **Scale (one snapshot).** Openfoodnetwork.net shows a running count: 1,039 food producers, 363 food shops, 7,693 food shoppers, 115,728 food orders; described as "a free and open source software platform." This is a live public dashboard figure — useful as a scale indicator, but it is a snapshot and should not be treated as a verified financial or governance statement.
- **Code.** OFN is on GitHub at github.com/openfoodfoundation/openfoodnetwork. The README says: "Supported by the Open Food Foundation and a network of global affiliates, we are proudly open source and not-for-profit - we're trying to seriously disrupt the concentration of power in global agri-food systems..." (github.com/openfoodfoundation/openfoodnetwork) This is a direct statement of the concentration-disruption intent.
- **Business model.** "Our code is Open Source and freely available to anyone to install, adapt and add to, but it does cost money to pay for servers and to compensate developers." (about.openfoodnetwork.net, OFNUSA pricing page) The LocallyGrown comparison piece: "If open source governance, community ownership, and platform independence are core values that outweigh the extra work required, OFN aligns with your mission." (locallygrown.net/why-not-open-source) — explicit statement that the open-source + community-ownership + platform-independence set of values is optional and costly.
- **What the platform does.** OFN's software platform page describes enabling "new, ethical supply chains." The platform is an ecommerce/order-management tool for community food enterprises — food hubs, online farmers' markets, co-ops, independent producers. (openfoodnetwork.org/software-platform)

**Finding for the concept.** OFN is the closest existing thing to the concept's stack in one body: an open-source platform for community food commerce, with a Commons ownership model, an explicit concentration-disruption intent, a global but locally-led community, and real scale. It is not, however, a grocery operation in the concept's sense — it is a platform that community food enterprises use. The concept wants to move from platform to operation, and from platform interoperability to operational federation. OFN is the best available reference point for the platform and community parts; it does not yet embody the sourcing-transparency-as-structural, participation-as-continuous-environment, or culture layers in the way the concept imagines.

### 2b. The Data Food Consortium — the interoperability layer, concretely

- **What it is.** The DFC is "a common and open digital language to develop short circuits" — an open data standard for short food supply chains to enable interoperability between farmers, marketplaces, and other supply chain actors. (fao.org/agroecology/in-action/detail/data-food-consortium...) The FAO page frames it as a "project of interoperability between short supply chain platforms in order to allow them to communicate with each other," with the goal "to work together to build an open and transparent food system, allowing the emancipation of both producers and eaters."
- **Origin.** Started around 2016–2018, hosted by Open Food France. The OFN community thread from December 2016 (MyriamBoure) is the clearest early statement of the problem and the vision. (community.openfoodnetwork.org/t/data-food-consortium-making-food-platforms-interoperable/772)
- **The problem statement (2016, still current).** Three concrete problems:
  1. A producer managing inventory in one platform who wants to also sell through another (e.g., an OFN shop) has to re-enter all data and update the catalog in each platform every week.
  2. A food hub wanting to display a producer group's products on the hub's OFN shop cannot, because the producer group already uses another tool with a dynamic update process and OFN could not synchronize the data.
  3. The biggest problem of food hubs is logistics — every hub organizes its own logistics, with big economic and environmental cost; some studies show food hubs can be worse than centralized food supply in environmental impact because of transport.
  (community.openfoodnetwork.org, MyriamBoure 2016)
- **The vision (2016).** A producer manages its catalog and inventory in one platform (e.g., OFN) and can "open up its data to another platform" — read/write authorizations — while remaining the owner of its data and controlling them. Standards around describing products and transactions; taxonomies; an open-source thesaurus / linked-data platform that all food platforms can refer to. Potential for mutualized logistics: "By using open data standards (with a democratic management of data and agreement of data owners of course) we could imagine mutualized logistics services that would be cheaper for everyone and environmentally friendly." Early participants: Open Food France, The Food Assembly, Panier Local, Cagette.net, Ekylibre. (community.openfoodnetwork.org, MyriamBoure 2016)
- **Status (ongoing).** An OFN community post titled "Unveiling our DFC API and current developments" says: "The standard is still under active development and open to contributors. The implementation is still rough around the edges and in active..." (community.openfoodnetwork.org/t/unveiling-our-dfc-api-and-current-developments/3610) — i.e., the interoperability standard exists and is live but is still rough and actively developed. Another post references "the new DFC (Data Food Consortium) API to fetch the data for this app so that it can be re-used with different data sources" in the context of OFN as data source for SOLID apps. (community.openfoodnetwork.org/t/ofn-as-data-source-for-solid-apps/2872)
- **The standard was built because none existed.** Rachel Arnould of DFC, quoted at OFN UK's Food Data Collaboration page: "We searched a lot but didn't find any standards that could be applied to tools for short food supply chain systems. So, we decided to create our own!" (about.openfoodnetwork.org.uk) — the interoperability standards layer is not given by the industry; it had to be built by the community.

### 2c. The Food Data Collaboration (UK, 2022) — a concrete funded instance

- A two-year project funded by The National Lottery Community Fund (just under £500,000), starting March 2022, led by OFN UK. Publicly announced at the Oxford Real Farming Conference 2022 in the "Linking Short Supply Chains with Data" session (chaired by Kath Dalmeny of Sustain; featured Rachel Arnould of DFC, Duncan Catchpole of Cambridge Food Hub, Lynne Davis and Sophie Paterson of OFN UK). (about.openfoodnetwork.org.uk/introducing-the-food-data-collaboration)
- **Concrete problem quotes.**
  - Duncan Catchpole, Cambridge Food Hub: "We primarily use two different systems: the veg box scheme is managed by Box Master and we use The Open Food Network for the food hub side of things. There's no link between the two, so they both work completely independently of each other. The product data that appears on one has to be re-entered on the other." (OFN UK)
  - Duncan Catchpole on collaboration friction: "It can be a barrier. When we started working with Hodmedod's, they were already selling their products on several different platforms. It took a bit of persuading for them to come on board and start using another platform on top of those." (OFN UK)
- **What interoperability enables (framed by OFN UK).** Simpler coordination of deliveries across trusted parties; distributors can collaborate with lower overhead; circular logistics costs can be coordinated and shared; simpler API access to a wide array of producers and distribution models meaning lower barriers to entry for community food businesses; producers spend less time coordinating produce across outlets; a single integration can enable social and environmental monitoring data so that producers and distributors without resources can easily contribute their data. (OFN UK)
- **Governance framing.** "Governance — processes around rules for data sharing are managed and agreed; Operations — business operations of platforms, distributors and producers; API and standards — an agreed framework and ontology for data sharing." The first stage was to bring together a trusted group of actors to develop the governance and processes. Partners included BigBarn, Box Master, Ooooby, Better Food Traders, the Landworkers' Alliance, Sustain, and the DFC. (OFN UK)
- **The commons framing.** Lynne Davis of OFN UK: "It's about creating an open-source commons that benefits the sector as a whole, rather than any individual corporate business — with the potential for huge benefits to wider collaboration as well." (OFN UK)

**Finding for the concept.** The Food Data Collaboration is the most concrete funded instance of the concept's interoperability layer in action: an open data standard, a community-governed data-sharing process, a commons framing, and real platforms and food hubs involved. It is evidence that the interoperability problems the concept names are real and solved-in-progress by this community. The concept's distinct move is to take this interoperability layer and embed it inside a grocery operation that also has structural transparency, continuous participation, and a culture layer — which the FDC/OFN instance does not yet do.

### 2d. OFN Canada's "open" framing — structure-level definition of open

OFN Canada's "Open Agricultural Technology" page is the clearest existing statement of what "open" means at the structure level for a food platform, and it maps onto the concept's structure layer directly.

- **Three categories of open.** (about.openfoodnetwork.ca/project/open-agricultural-technology)
  1. **Open Source** — "Software whose code is freely available to use, improve, and share. It encourages transparency, innovation, and collective progress."
  2. **Open Standards** — "Publicly available rules and formats that allow different technologies to work together. They prevent silos, encourage interoperability, and make innovation easier."
  3. **Data Sovereignty** — "The principle that farmers should control their own data — deciding how it is stored, shared, and applied. It ensures technology serves producers, not outside corporate or political interests."
- **The access vs. control distinction.** This is the most important passage for the concept's structure layer: "The debate over farm data is not just about access; it's about control. Open data improves transparency, but without protections, it risks exposing farmers to exploitation... While 'open data' increases transparency, it doesn't guarantee fairness or farmer agency. Without strong protections, openness risks benefiting the most powerful actors in the supply chain — not the producers themselves." Examples of misuse: market manipulation by large buyers using detailed production data; unequal value capture by corporations selling insights to insurers, input providers, or speculators; loss of autonomy; privacy concerns. (OFN Canada)
- **Data sovereignty framing.** "Data sovereignty goes further than simply making agricultural data easy to access — ensuring that farmers themselves govern how their information is used, with clear consent and accountability." And: "OFN Canada has demonstrated this commitment by adopting OpenTEAM's Ag Data Use Agreements, which provide a framework for responsible, farmer-governed data sharing." (OFN Canada)
- **Partners.** OpenTEAM ("a collaborative network advancing climate-smart agriculture and open, interoperable tools that keep farmers in control of their data"), D:Food/Web Initiative ("an emerging coalition advancing food and data sovereignty through decentralized, community-driven technologies and cooperative data governance"), GOAT ("Gathering for Open Agricultural Technology — a global network of developers, farmers, and stakeholders advancing open source solutions for transparency, secure data sharing, and market access"), DFC Open Standard, and the Québec project ("a collaborative project building a shared, open-source web portal that empowers food hubs and producers to federate data and coordinate supply chains without sacrificing control of how their data is used, or who has access to it"). (OFN Canada)

**Finding for the concept.** This is the strongest existing structural-framing reference in the scan. OFN Canada's three-category open (Open Source, Open Standards, Data Sovereignty) and its access-vs-control distinction directly inform the concept's structure layer. The concept's "open structure" and "open sourcing" layers map onto this: openness is not the same as access; the operation has to be built so that the people who provide the data (producers, eaters, the community) govern how it is used, with consent and accountability, and openness does not become a mechanism for the most powerful actors to extract value. The Ag Data Use Agreements and the Québec federated-data portal are concrete precedents for the kind of data-governance structure the concept wants.

### 2e. What OFN/DFC are not (important for the concept's distinctiveness)

- **Not a grocery operation.** OFN is a software platform that community food enterprises use; it is not itself a grocery store, buying group, or distribution operation. It does not embody the concept's Action layer in the sense of moving food and capturing/d distributing the food dollar.
- **Not a full grocery-scale federation.** OFN is a network of platform instances and community food enterprises; it is not a federated grocery operation at the scale the concept imagines. The DFC is an interoperability standard for short supply chain platforms, not a governance structure for a federated grocery operation.
- **Not a sourcing-transparency-as-structural model.** OFN/DFC work on data about food (products, inventory, orders, supply chain relationships), but the sourcing-transparency-as-structural layer the concept wants — where sourcing information is surfaced by default and is part of the operation's identity to eaters — is not the focus. OFN's transparency is more about supply chain data and data sovereignty than about an eater-facing sourcing information environment.
- **Not a continuous participation environment.** OFN has a community (a Discourse forum, global gatherings, local affiliates) and the platform supports community food enterprises, but it is not a continuous participatory information environment around a grocery operation in the concept's sense.
- **Not a culture layer.** OFN/DFC do not address the meaning/culture dimension of food — Indigenous foodways, diaspora traditions, regional food cultures, family and community food practices — as a structural layer.

**Finding for the concept.** OFN/DFC are the best available reference points for the concept's Structure and Interaction questions around federation, interoperability, open source, Commons ownership, and data sovereignty. They are not the concept. The concept's distinctiveness lies in moving from platform/interoperability to operational grocery, and from interoperability to continuous participation and structural transparency and culture. The concept should not claim OFN/DFC as examples of the concept; it should use them as the closest existing test of pieces of the architecture and learn from what they built and what they don't yet do.

## Cross-cutting findings from targets 1 and 2

### On participation

The co-op sector has real but intermittent formal participation (AGM, board elections, member ownership, one-member-one-vote) and a well-documented engagement gap (Hale 2018 "little to no involvement of consumer members"; Columinate on the AGM-enticement struggle; the sector folk knowledge that treating participation as a duty backfires). The concept's interaction layer wants to go beyond intermittent formal governance into a continuous, knowledge-producing, interactive environment — but the concept should take seriously the sector's existing knowledge that mandated participation produces conflict and that "the great work of society" in co-ops is the conversation and argument, not the vote. The concept's hardest interaction question is not whether participation is good but what form of continuous participatory environment avoids the duty/conflict trap and the noise/abuse/manipulation trap while remaining genuinely participatory.

### On federation and interoperability

NCG is a real federated grocery structure at scale (165 co-ops, $2.8B, 1.3M members) but it is a pre-internet federation — federated buying/distribution, not internet-era interoperability. OFN/DFC are an internet-era interoperability project at the platform level, with a standard still under active development and "rough around the edges," and a funded UK instance (Food Data Collaboration, £500K, 2 years) that turns the interoperability problems into concrete, quotable operational pain. The concept's internet-era federation is a synthesis that doesn't yet exist: NCG-scale federation plus OFN/DFC-style interoperability plus a grocery operation rather than a platform. The concept's structure question — what does a node join, what does it agree to, what can it disagree about, what can it leave with — is unresolved and the existing precedents don't answer it.

### On open and data sovereignty

OFN Canada's three-category framing (Open Source, Open Standards, Data Sovereignty) and its access-vs-control distinction are the best existing structure-level framing of "open" in food. The concept's structure layer should adopt the access-vs-control distinction explicitly: openness is not the same as access, and an open grocery operation has to be built so that the people who provide information (producers, eaters, the community) govern how it is used, with consent and accountability, rather than becoming a transparency surface that benefits the most powerful actors. The Ag Data Use Agreements and the Québec federated-data portal are concrete precedents.

### On viability

The co-op sector's mixed record is the evidence on viability: real growth (4.7 percent same-store sales growth, $2.8B, 1.3M members) and real community benefit (local sourcing, community donations, food-access locations, solidarity funding), but tiny share of the market (versus Kroger's $132.5B) and price disadvantages on some items. The UCLA Anderson working paper's community-co-op vs people's-co-op framing is a useful way to think about which mission an operation foregrounds and what competitive response that invites. The concept should name its viability question rather than assume a community-benefiting operation is viable by default.

### On meaning/culture

Neither the co-op sector nor OFN/DFC address the meaning/culture dimension as a structural layer. NCG has meaningful commitments (food sovereignty, Indigenous foodways via NĀTIFS, Black food justice, farmworker justice) and OFN Canada has a food-sovereignty framing, but these are values commitments and partnerships, not a structural layer designed to carry and surface cultural meaning. This remains the thinnest category in the scan and is flagged again for a dedicated pass.
