# Framework Agreement on First Nation Land Management (land-code regime)

- Status: `curated`
- Region / reach: Canada (224 signatory First Nations; growing)
- Project: https://labrc.com/
- Field-guide context: new discovery via the land sectoral scan (G-OSA-35, sub-cell 4); verified 2026-09-04. **This is a governance/jurisdiction record, not an open-data record** — the corpus records it because land control in Canada runs through this jurisdictional layer, and the analytical point is jurisdiction operating through community-defined law.

## Problem addressed

Indian Act land administration — 44 land-related sections administered by the federal Crown — leaves First Nations governing their own reserve lands under someone else's law. The Framework Agreement (signed 1996-02-12 by 13 First Nations and Canada) provides the exit: signatory nations ratify community land codes, resume full law-making authority over their reserve lands and environment, and govern outside the Indian Act land regime and outside the federal First Nations Land Register.

## Open layer

Governance documents, deliberately nation-controlled. The Land Codes Repository (122 files) is published by the Resource Centre **"for resource and knowledge sharing purposes only, please contact the First Nation directly to confirm their active land code"** — no open licence; each land code is community-authored law attributed to its nation, and reuse requires that nation's permission. This is consistent with the corpus's refusals pattern: jurisdiction over land includes jurisdiction over the visibility of land law.

## Governance and control

Verified structure and process: a nation develops (1) its own **Land Code** — the community's basic land law (lands governed, use/occupation rules, accountability for land revenues, law-making powers, dispute resolution), requiring **no ministerial or federal approval**; (2) an **Individual Agreement** with Canada transferring administration (Canada's grantor rights/obligations transfer on the code coming into force, per s.8 of the 2022 Act); (3) a **Community Ratification Process** — a membership vote on- and off-reserve, monitored by an independent Verifier. Governance bodies: the **Lands Advisory Board** (political; elected, 10 members + chair across three regions) and the **First Nations Land Management Resource Centre** (technical arm).

## Statutory basis

Framework Agreement on First Nation Land Management Act, S.C. 2022, c. 19 (in force 2022-12-15), which repealed and replaced the First Nations Land Management Act (SC 1999, c. 24) and gives the Framework Agreement itself the force of law. Lands remain within s.91(24) of the Constitution Act 1867; the Agreement is not a treaty and does not affect treaty or constitutional rights. Budget 2023 committed $35.3M (2023-2026) toward co-developing a First Nation-owned land registry to replace the ISC-operated one for operational nations — the registry layer is itself in transition to First Nation control.

## Evidence of use

Counts (labrc.com, as of 2026-08-06): **224 total signatories; 128 with operational land codes; 46 developmental-active; 4 in full self-governance** — i.e., **132 nations governing under land codes** (internally consistent: labrc.com reported Mitaanjigamiing First Nation as "the 132nd signatory to regain land governance" on 2026-06-01). Economic evidence exists but is commissioned rather than independent: the KPMG socio-economic benefits studies (2009/2014) and LAB parliamentary briefs report stronger development outcomes on land-code lands; no independent peer-reviewed study cleanly isolates land-code vs Indian Act outcomes — recorded with that caveat.

## Why it belongs

The land cell's largest jurisdictional development, and the correct frame for the corpus's Indigenous-governance finding: 132 nations (and growing) govern reserve land through their own community-defined law, replacing a federal register with their own records while a First Nation-owned registry is co-developed. The record documents jurisdiction as the substantive open-layer question in land — what is governable, by whom, under whose law — rather than assuming openness is the terminal value. It pairs with the fisheries cell's Netukulimk finding: Indigenous land governance in Canada operates through community-defined rules and controlled visibility, by design.

## What it does not demonstrate

Open data (the land codes are knowledge-sharing-only, by instruction); uniform outcomes (economic evidence is commissioned, not independent); coverage (224 signatories is a minority of Canada's 600+ First Nations; on-reserve agricultural capability constraints remain, per the scan's Saskatchewan analysis).
