# ROMI — Robotics for Microfarms

- Status: `curated`
- Region / reach: Europe; field sites near Paris and Barcelona
- Project: https://romi-project.eu/
- Documentation: https://docs.romi-project.eu/
- Field-guide context: new discovery lead; not transferred from the Agrifood AI Field Guide

## Problem addressed

ROMI develops robotic tools to assist organic, diversified, human-scale vegetable farms with physically demanding or repetitive work such as weeding, crop monitoring, and plant phenotyping.

## Open layer

Open hardware designs, software, research, data, and shared documentation.

## What is actually open

The project documents a Plant Imager, Farmer’s Dashboard/Cablebot, Rover, PlantDB, viewers, and algorithms, with public source repositories. The ROMI Rover design repository identifies a Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 4.0 licence. Individual hardware, software, data, and documentation components require separate licence checks.

## Governance and control

ROMI describes co-development with professional farmers and a focus on farming communities. Its field work took place at two core sites across four seasons. The project’s governance beyond the funded research consortium, and farmer control of generated data, remain unclear.

## Evidence of use

The project reports field testing with professional farmers near Paris and Barcelona. Public repositories and documentation provide evidence of technical development. Several repositories show their most recent visible activity in 2022–2023, so current maintenance should not be assumed.

## Maintenance and funding

ROMI was a four-year Europe-funded research project. The central maintenance question is what happens to the tools, designs, and documentation after the funded project period.

## Why it belongs

ROMI adds open hardware and robotics to a collection otherwise dominated by software, data, standards, and governance. It makes repairability, manufacturing access, labour, affordability, and practical autonomy visible.

## What it does not demonstrate

Open designs and project field trials do not establish affordable manufacture, broad adoption, continued maintenance, or improved farmer livelihoods. Open hardware may still require specialised supply chains and expertise.
