# Flying Labs Network (WeRobotics)

- Status: `curated`
- Region / reach: close to 40 countries across Africa, Latin America, the Caribbean, and Asia-Pacific
- Project: https://flyinglabs.org/ ; network page: https://werobotics.org/our-work/networks-of-local-experts/the-flying-labs-network
- Field-guide context: new discovery from the Global South open-hardware cycle, 2026-08-02

## Problem addressed

Professional drone, data, and AI capacity is concentrated in the global North. The Flying Labs Network builds local expertise in the global South through independent local knowledge hubs, with agriculture among its named application sectors.

## Open layer

Network and capacity infrastructure: a social-franchise ("Glocalization") model in which local hubs deliver drone, data, and AI services using open-technology tooling and open practices. The network is the largest located global South drone/data/AI ecosystem with documented agriculture applications.

## What is actually open

- The network's model is openness-adjacent rather than a hardware commons: local hubs are independent, locally led, demand-driven, and self-financed, and the network works with open-source drone, data, and AI tools.
- Specific open tools used per deployment (e.g., open autopilots such as ArduPilot, open photogrammetry such as OpenDroneMap) were not verified per project in this pass; hubs may also use commercial tools. The openness claim therefore applies to the network model and its open-tech orientation, not to every deployment.

## Governance and control

WeRobotics (a nonprofit) co-created the network; each Flying Labs is an independent local knowledge hub with full agency over its activities and sectors, tied to one or several Sustainable Development Goals. Hubs are fully self-financed, providing services to communities, governments, non-profits, research institutions, and international organisations. The "localization" claim is structural (independent local hubs) rather than farmer-level (farmers are clients, not owners).

## Evidence of use

- Network-reported figures: 38 Flying Labs at the end of 2025; 669 locally led projects and trainings; a 10-year impact report (2025).
- Agriculture applications documented: CGIAR and Tanzania Flying Labs "Drones for Agriculture" training (multi-copter and fixed-wing autonomous missions); Nepal Flying Labs agriculture monitoring, damage assessment, and property-rights mapping; a dedicated "Drones, Data and AI for Agriculture" news category on flyinglabs.org.
- Independent coverage: ESRI (network spanning 41 countries); Tech Matters interview with WeRobotics on "decolonizing drones".

## Maintenance and funding

Each hub is self-financed; the network is sustained by WeRobotics (nonprofit) and hub service revenue. The 10-year impact report indicates network durability. Impact figures are network self-reported and were not independently audited.

## What this case demonstrates

Flying Labs is the strongest located instance of global South drone/data/AI capacity infrastructure with documented agricultural use: a localization model that keeps capability in-country and is durable (10+ years). It fills the "drone ecosystems" sub-cell of the Global South hardware gap with a network-level, not farm-level, record.

## What it does not demonstrate

- It does not establish farmer control; farmers are typically clients of hubs.
- Impact figures (38 labs, 669 projects) are network self-reported.
- Per-deployment open-tool usage is unverified.
- It is capacity infrastructure, not an open-hardware commons or open design set.
