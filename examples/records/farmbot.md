# FarmBot

- Status: `curated`
- Region / reach: global; company based in San Luis Obispo, California, US
- Project: https://farm.bot/
- Open-source page: https://farm.bot/pages/open-source
- Field-guide context: new discovery from the open-hardware/robotics/sensing cycle, 2026-08-02

## Problem addressed

FarmBot is a CNC (Cartesian coordinate) precision-farming machine for automated small-scale food production: sowing, watering, and mechanical weed control at the scale of a garden, greenhouse, or small plot. It addresses labour and precision in small-scale food growing rather than commercial field agriculture.

## Open layer

Open hardware designs, open software, and open documentation, with company governance and commercial kit sales.

## What is actually open

- Software: verified MIT licence on public repositories (farmbot-web-app; farmbot-arduino-firmware) as of 2026-08-02.
- Hardware: company policy commits to releasing hardware designs per the Open Source Hardware Association (OSHWA) definition of open source hardware, with CAD models, bill of materials, schematics, and assembly instructions published.
- Documentation: company policy commits to licences conforming to the Open Knowledge Foundation's definition of open.
- Upstream open-data layer: the OpenFarm crop database (OpenFarmCC/OpenFarm, MIT) is a free and open farming-knowledge database associated with the project.
- The "100% open source" framing is a company claim; a per-asset audit of every hardware part and document was not performed for this record.

## Governance and control

FarmBot Inc. is a California Benefit Corporation. Its bylaws encode benefit purposes including: openly sharing products (per the definitions above), openly sharing business information (sales, financials, salaries), defaulting to transparency, contributing 1% of annual net revenue to the community, and "be good people". The company controls the core product; a community forum exists for users and DIY builders.

A notable boundary: the company states it cannot dedicate resources to troubleshooting DIY builds — technical support is only offered for full kits purchased from its store, with DIYers directed to the community forum. Openness of the design therefore does not extend to company-backed support for self-builders.

## Evidence of use

- The company reports more than 1,000 kits shipped to customers in over 65 countries (a second company page claims "over 90 countries" — the two figures are inconsistent and both are company claims, not audited counts).
- Hackaday Prize finalist (2015); substantial press coverage from Wired, Smithsonian, and Arduino blog.
- Community forum activity exists but was not quantified for this record.

## Maintenance and funding

Funded through commercial kit sales and services; originally seeded by a Shuttleworth Foundation grant (2014). The benefit-corporation structure is the main stated commitment to maintaining the open commons. The DIY support boundary means community maintenance of self-builds depends on the forum, not the company.

## What this case demonstrates

FarmBot is the clearest located example of company-backed open hardware in agrifood: openness encoded in corporate bylaws, sustained by commercial revenue, and bounded by explicit support limits. It tests the project's question of whether "open" and commercial value capture can coexist, and where the boundary of the company's responsibility sits.

## What it does not demonstrate

- It does not establish that every hardware part, document, and asset is open — that requires a per-asset audit.
- It does not establish commercial-scale agricultural deployment; its scope is small-scale/backyard production.
- The kit-shipment and country figures are unverified company claims with internal inconsistency.
- It does not demonstrate farmer governance; control sits with the company.
