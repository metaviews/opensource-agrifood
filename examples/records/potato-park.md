# Potato Park (Papa Ayllu)

- Status: `curated`
- Region / reach: Cusco, Peru (altitude 3,400-4,600m); five Quechua communities
- Project context: Asociación ANDES; https://localbiodiversityoutlooks.net/the-story-of-the-potato-park/
- Field-guide context: new discovery from the biological-commons cycle, 2026-08-02

## Problem addressed

The Potato Park is an Indigenous Biocultural Territory conserving agrobiodiversity — about 600 native potato varieties plus other Andean crops — through Quechua collective governance, in-situ conservation, and biocultural protocols, as an alternative to ex-situ gene-bank and IPR-based approaches.

## Open layer

Indigenous-governed biological commons: collective land title, inter-community benefit-sharing agreement based on customary law, and a Biocultural Heritage Protocol. The governance is the commons mechanism — this is not an open-licence system, and translating it into open-licensing terms would misrepresent it.

## What is actually open

- Since 1998, Asociación ANDES has worked with five Quechua communities (over 6,000 people) to establish and govern the Park through a general council of elected community leaders.
- Governance instruments: an Inter-Community Agreement for Benefit Sharing / Biocultural Heritage Protocol; collective governance registered as the Association of Communities of the Potato Park.
- Repatriation: a five-year agreement with the International Potato Center (CIP) returned 410 native potato cultivars from the gene bank to the communities for in-situ conservation, with community knowledge registers protecting rights and preventing IPR capture of varieties and ancestral knowledge.
- An in-situ genetic reserve for wild potato relatives was launched with Crop Trust and the government of Peru (2017), with management plans built on Quechua normative principles.

## Governance and control

Community-governed: authority shared between the villages, each electing a chairperson to coordinate the Association; governance by Indigenous communities rather than governments or international organisations (per the Nature-based Solutions case study).

## Evidence of use

- Peer-reviewed and institutional documentation: Springer chapter on the Park's seed governance; the Nature-based Solutions Initiative case study; IIED documentation of the biocultural protocols; the Satoyama Initiative case study of the Ayllu system.
- The CIP repatriation agreement (2016 paper) documents outcomes for community rights understanding.
- ~600 native potato varieties and the 410-cultivar repatriation are the scale anchors.

## Maintenance and funding

Community-governed with NGO (ANDES) and institutional partnerships (Crop Trust, CIP). Long-term funding and the balance of external partnership vs community authority are the open questions.

## What this case demonstrates

Potato Park is the strongest located example of Indigenous-governed biological commons in agrifood: collective, customary-law-based governance of genetic resources with repatriation and in-situ conservation — the governance alternative to both open licensing and corporate IP. It is the field case for the project's Sovereign Seeds caution: commons governance should not be flattened into open-licensing terms.

## What it does not demonstrate

- It is not an open-licence system; access rules are community-governed, not open-to-all.
- External partnership dynamics (ANDES, Crop Trust, CIP) vs community authority are not fully documented in the sources read.
- Scale of seed circulation outside the Park communities is unverified.
- The repatriation agreement's ongoing implementation was not verified beyond 2016-2017 documentation.
