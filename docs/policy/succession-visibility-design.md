# Seeing the transition: open farmland-ownership analysis and the non-family succession gap

Date: 2026-11-18
Status: design document, in the genre of `community-processing-regulatory-design.md`. It
contains proposals rather than findings, and keeps the two visibly apart.

## How to read this document

This document answers the question *what should be changed* about how Canada sees and
supports its farm-transfer wave. It is a successor-document in two senses: it follows the
community-processing design document's method, and it concerns succession.

- **Documented** — a claim with a named source, checkable by anyone. All documented
  claims here were verified in the succession research program's cycle passes
  (`succession/research/`) on 2026-11-18.
- **Proposed** — this project's own design. Not evidence, not a finding, and not a claim
  that anyone has agreed to it.
- **Unresolved** — a question the proposal raises that this document cannot answer.

Three disciplines carry over from the sibling document:

1. **No unattributed consensus.** Where this document says something is agreed, it names
   by whom.
2. **No weakening of outcomes.** Every proposal either adds visibility, adds a lawful
   adaptation right, or extends an existing shelter to a population that lacks one. None
   reduces anyone's existing protection, and each proposal names how it could go wrong.
3. **No drafting.** These are design proposals, not statutory text. Nothing here is legal
   advice, and none of it has been costed.

## 1. What is documented about the present situation

### 1.1 The transfer wave is the largest in Canadian agricultural history — and policy is family-shaped

Statistics Canada's 2021 Census of Agriculture puts the average operator at 56.0 years,
60.5% aged 55+, only 8.6% under 35, and just 12.0% of farms with a written succession
plan (`research/2026-09-land-sectoral-scan.md`). Industry projections differ in
methodology but agree in direction (FCC: >$50B in farm assets changing hands over a
decade; CBA: 88% of farms with no written plan; FMC: "75% farms will change hands, yet
only 8% of farmers have a written transition plan" — three figures from three
methodologically distinct sources, recorded as disagreement in
`succession/research/2026-11-c1-succession-knowledge-pass.md`).

The federal instruments that answer this — the Lifetime Capital Gains Exemption
($1.25 million for qualified farm or fishing property, Budget 2024), the tax-deferred
rollover, and the five-year reserve — are built for **parent-to-child transfer**
(AAFC Question Period Note AAFC-2025-QP-00152, published on open.canada.ca, 2025-12-11).
A farmer without a child who wants to farm — the majority case the demographic
arithmetic implies — has no equivalent shelter when transferring to a beginning farmer,
an employee cohort, a co-operative, or a community vehicle. **This is documented as the
architecture's shape, not asserted as its intent.**

### 1.2 The public cannot see the wave

The land scan's finding stands two years on
(`succession/research/2026-11-c6-visibility-policy-pass.md`): no federal public
beneficial-ownership registry operates (ISED's live consultation asks whether to
establish one); BC's Land Owner Transparency Registry remains Canada's only public
land registry of its kind; no province publishes open transaction-level price data (the
HM Land Registry Price Paid Data model exists and is cited by the corpus as the
reference); Ontario's parcel data is commercially locked (Teranet).

### 1.3 The analysis method works — where the data allows it

The Johnson Shoyama Graduate School of Public Policy's brief "Who Owns Saskatchewan's
Farmland?" (Magnan, Qualman, Duncan, Desmarais, 2026-03-30) turned **public land-titles
records** — complete ISC data for all Saskatchewan rural municipalities, December 2023 —
into named findings: 84 large-scale private owners holding 1.44 million acres (2.4% of
Saskatchewan farmland, +140% in five years); investor ownership stabilized then
declined, with CPPIB fully divested by 2023; and "no evidence of non-Canadian ownership"
— an evidence-based correction to the foreign-ownership frame that dominated the
province's Fall 2025 legislature debates. The brief is free-to-read and unlicensed; the
method is not packaged for reuse anywhere else. Ontario — the province where the
transfer stakes are most concentrated in dollar terms — cannot replicate it, because its
parcel ownership data is commercial.

### 1.4 The knowledge layer exists, is free to read, and is closed — and its default provider is a lender

Every province publishes succession guides (OMAFRA Publication 70 carries "© Queen's
Printer for Ontario, 2020"; Alberta's guide states it was adapted from Manitoba's
"with permission" — adaptation is normal practice, done by private permission);
the farm sector's model legal documents exist free-to-read but no Canadian actor grants
adapt-and-redistribute rights over any of them; the only genuinely open-licensed
succession asset found anywhere is a US workbook published under CC BY-SA 4.0
(Land For Good, verified in-document). Meanwhile the federal government's own
question-period answer to "how does the Government support intergenerational farm
transfer?" routes farmers to "Farm Credit Canada offers free learning programs regarding
succession planning and targeted loan products" (AAFC QP note, cited above) — and FCC's
terms of use forbid derivative works on its worksheets and checklists (verified
2026-11-18). **The country's default succession knowledge is lender-authored,
lender-funded, and legally unforkable — documented at both the market level and as
stated federal policy.**

## 2. Diagnosis: four faults

1. **Invisibility.** The largest private wealth transfer in the sector's history
   proceeds without public transaction-level data, with a beneficial-ownership regime
   one province deep, and with an analysis method that works but is neither repeated
   nor licensed.
2. **Family-shape.** The tax architecture, the provincial guides, and the sector's
   institutional imagination assume a successor who is a child. The demographic record
   implies most transfers will not be.
3. **Enclosure without a lock.** The knowledge and documents exist; what is missing is a
   licence. Free-to-read without derivative rights sends every real adaptation back to a
   billable hour — reproducing the professional-services bottleneck the free material
   appears to bypass.
4. **Lender mediation.** The default knowledge provider for the country's most
   consequential family and business decision is its largest farm lender, by policy and
   by terms of use.

## 3. Design principles

- **Open by licence, not by landing page.** Free to read is not open. Anything public
  money funds in the succession space should ship with an adapt-and-redistribute licence
  (share-alike preferred).
- **Lender-independent knowledge.** Succession knowledge is a public good with a
  structural conflict of interest when provided by a lender.
- **Evidence before narrative.** The Saskatchewan brief shows what happens when titles
  data answers a moral-panic question: the panic ("foreign ownership") was not
  supported, and the real pattern (mega-farm concentration) was invisible without the
  data.
- **Design for the non-family majority.** Every instrument named here should work for a
  transfer to a beginning farmer, employee cohort, co-operative, or community vehicle,
  not only to a child.
- **Interface, never design-around, Indigenous jurisdiction.** Per the land scan's
  sub-cell 4 finding, any ownership-visibility design operates on lands where title and
  jurisdiction questions are live; OCAP governs data involving First Nations. The
  proposals below are scoped to private-title farmland and must not become instruments
  of pressure on Indigenous land questions.

## 4. Proposals

### P1 — A Farmland Transition Observatory

A repeatable, **open-licensed** farmland-ownership analysis program: the Saskatchewan
method (titles records → named owners → investor/mega-farm concentration), generalized
across provinces, its methodology and code published under CC BY-SA so any province,
university, or newsroom can run and fork it. Start where the data allows
(Saskatchewan's ISC; BC's ParcelMap open subset), and name the blocker where it does
not (Ontario's Teranet lock-up) as the policy ask it is.

- **Precedent:** the JSGS brief itself; HM Land Registry's open data as the reference
  regime.
- **Failure mode:** a dashboard nobody audits. Mitigation: publish the raw extraction
  alongside the analysis, under the same licence, so findings are checkable — the brief's
  credibility came from its method note, and an observatory without a method note is a
  press release.
- **Unresolved:** cost; whether ISC/other registries will license bulk research access;
  how investor identification (Corporate Registry + internet searches, in the brief)
  scales honestly.

### P2 — Transaction-level visibility: price-paid data and beneficial ownership

Adopt, province by province, the two missing layers the reference regime already proves:
open **price-paid data** (HM Land Registry's model — transaction-level, open-licensed)
and **beneficial ownership of land** beyond BC (federal: respond to ISED's live
consultation by establishing the public registry Budget 2022 committed to; provincial:
extend the LOTR model). For the succession question specifically, price-paid data at
parcel level is what converts "farmland values rose 9.3%" (FCC aggregate, lender
conflict undisclosed) into a public record of who paid what to whom.

- **Precedent:** HM Land Registry Price Paid Data (open, since 2014, data to 1995);
  BC LOTR (world-first, 2021).
- **Failure mode:** privacy harm to individual farmers. Mitigation: follow the LOTR's
  graduated-disclosure design (search-not-bulk initially, research access tiered), and
  scope price-paid publication to agricultural parcels with a delay, as the UK does
  historically. The UK experience suggests both are manageable.
- **Unresolved:** provincial political economy; interaction with farm-status and
  family-corporation privacy expectations.

*Refined 2026-11-18 after cycle C2 (farm-link anatomy):* C2's verified finding is that
no examined farm-link operator publishes matching criteria and no evaluation study
identifies criteria opacity as a failure mode — the documented failure modes are
trust, readiness, external conditions, and follow-through. Published criteria and
outcome data are therefore framed here as **accountability infrastructure** (making a
publicly funded service inspectable and its match-count claims comparable), not as a
mechanism to raise match rates.

### P3 — A Canadian open succession-knowledge commons

Fork what is legally forkable and rebuild what is not. The Land For Good workbook is
**CC BY-SA 4.0 inside the document** — a Canadian adaptation (jurisdictional
tax/rollover content, Canadian case material, French version) is lawful today and would
give Canada its first open-licensed succession instrument. Around it: commission
Canadian adaptations of the model legal documents that exist free-to-read (the Agrarian
Commons equitable lease and bylaws, the Schumacher CLT instruments — noting their own
licences forbid adaptation, so these are rebuilds informed by the models, not copies),
published under CC BY-SA. House it somewhere lender-independent: a university extension,
a co-op federation, or an inter-provincial farm-organization consortium.

- **Precedent:** Alberta adapting Manitoba's guide "with permission" — the behaviour
  exists; the licence generalizes it. SELC's site-wide CC BY-SA practice proves a legal
  organization can publish its output share-alike.
- **Failure mode:** an abandoned fork — the corpus's maintenance-funding profiles exist
  precisely because open assets die without them. Mitigation: fund maintenance, not just
  creation, from the start (the processing document's P5 logic).
- **Unresolved:** who hosts it; French-language equity; whether provincial guides can be
  integrated given Crown copyright (ask: their reuse terms are unstated — this document
  proposes making the ask formally).

### P4 — Publish the legal specification when public money pays for it

Where governments or public funders commission succession-related legal work (guide
updates, template development, transition-program toolkits), the engagement terms should
require **open licensing of the output** (CC BY-SA recommended). The parallel is exact:
the community-processing document proposed that publicly funded specifications be
published rather than subsidized into secrecy. OMAFRA's Publication 70 is the standing
example — publicly produced, Crown-copyrighted, unadaptable.

- **Failure mode:** ministries resist on advice-risk grounds. Mitigation: the
  educational-disclaimer model already used by every toolkit this program examined;
  open licensing of the *text* does not create liability the disclaimer does not
  already address.
- **Unresolved:** Crown-copyright modernization (some provinces run Open Government
  Licences; the succession documents simply don't use them).

### P5 — A non-family transfer shelter

Extend the logic of the intergenerational rollover and the LCGE to transfers **to
beginning farmers, employee cohorts, farm co-operatives, and community land vehicles** —
a statutory shelter for the transfers the demographic record implies will dominate, with
anti-avoidance guardrails (maximum acreage or value per transfer, entrant-occupation
requirements, clawback on resale within a defined period — the guardrail pattern the
tax system already uses for the family rollover).

- **Precedent in principle:** the family rollover itself; the LCGE's 2024 expansion
  shows the architecture can move. The Young Agrarians BC Transition Toolkit's entire
  reason to exist is the non-family case; OFA/CFA advocacy already demonstrated that
  farm-transfer sensitivity shapes capital-gains policy (the inclusion-rate episode) —
  this proposal redirects that demonstrated sensitivity toward entrant access rather
  than only incumbent protection.
- **Failure mode:** converting a succession measure into a tax-planning industry;
  asset-shuffling into "community vehicles" to shelter gains. Mitigation: the
  guardrails above, plus the honest note that the Saskatchewan evidence (concentration
  driven by large operators, not investors) means any shelter must also be capped
  against mega-farm accumulation, not only investor capture.
- **Unresolved:** cost; interaction with the AMT changes; provincial land-ownership
  rules (the land scan's NFU compilation) that cap some of these vehicles' eligibility.

### P6 — A successor-neutral transition information service

Public extension should be the default first stop for transition knowledge, with the
lender as one advisor among several rather than the state's designated answer. The AAFC
QP note shows the current assignment; P3 provides the content; this proposal is the
channel: provincial extension services (which already publish the guides) carrying an
explicitly non-lender transition curriculum, in both official languages.

- **Failure mode:** reproducing the guide problem — free-to-read, closed-licensed,
  unread. Mitigation: P3's licensing discipline and P7's measurement.

### P7 — Farm-link platforms publish criteria and outcomes

The land scan verified that no open farm-link platform exists anywhere and Canada has
none at all; the platforms that do exist (nonprofit land-matching services) publish
neither their matching criteria nor their outcome rates. This proposal asks less than an
open platform and more than the status quo: any farm-link service taking public funding
publishes its matching criteria and annual outcome data under an open licence. If
matching is a trust-and-volume problem openness cannot solve (the honest alternative
finding recorded in `succession/research/research-mapping.md` C2), the published
outcomes will say so — that is the point.

- **Failure mode:** metrics that game the match definition. Mitigation: require the
  outcome definition (what counts as a "completed match") to be published with the
  numbers.

## 5. What could be done without changing a statute

- P1 (observatory), where registry access permits — Saskatchewan first.
- P3 (the CC BY-SA fork of the Land For Good workbook, and Canadian adaptations of the
  rebuild-able model documents), with existing program funding.
- P4 (open-licensing conditions on newly commissioned work) — administrative, funder by
  funder.
- P6 (extension as the default channel) — administrative.
- P7 (funding conditions on farm-link services) — administrative.

Requiring statute or regulation: P2 (registries and price-paid data), P5 (Income Tax
Act), and the Ontario piece of P1 (registry access).

## 6. Channels that already exist

- ISED's live beneficial-ownership consultation — the direct vehicle for P2's federal
  layer.
- The Next Policy Framework consultation cycle — the NFU's June 2026 submission already
  names land access; P5 and P3 belong in that frame.
- OFA/CFA's demonstrated capacity to move capital-gains policy (the inclusion-rate
  cancellation) — the advocacy channel for P5.
- The community-processing design document's own circulation question — if the project
  circulates design documents, this one joins that posture decision.

## 7. What this document does not claim

- It does not claim any proposal has support beyond this project. Each is a design
  grounded in documented findings, not a coalition position.
- It does not cost anything.
- It does not resolve the Indigenous-jurisdiction interface; it scopes itself to
  private-title farmland and defers that interface to the program's standing constraint.
- It does not claim openness solves the capital wall. It claims the transfer's
  visibility, knowledge, legal instruments, and non-family pathways are all currently
  closed by licence or by design, and proposes opening them.
