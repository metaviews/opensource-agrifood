# On-site and community-based processing: a regulatory design proposal

Date: 2026-09-11
Status: design document. This one is different from everything else in this repository, and it
says so.

## How to read this document

Every other file in this project answers the question *what is true*. This one answers the
question *what should be changed*, which means it contains proposals rather than findings. The
two are kept visibly apart:

- **Documented** — a claim with a named source, checkable by anyone.
- **Proposed** — this project's own design. Not evidence, not a finding, and not a claim that
  anyone has agreed to it.
- **Unresolved** — a question the proposal raises that this document cannot answer.

Three disciplines follow, and they are the price of being taken seriously:

1. **No unattributed consensus.** Where this document says something is widely agreed, it says
   by whom, and cites them. "There is growing consensus" is an assertion until it names its
   authors.
2. **No weakening of outcomes.** Every proposal here redistributes *fixed costs* or moves
   verification to where the risk actually is. None of them reduces the obligation to produce
   safe food, and the document names, for each proposal, how it could go wrong.
3. **No drafting.** These are design proposals, not statutory text. Nothing here is legal
   advice, and none of it has been costed.

The proposals arise from the upcycling concept's research tree (`upcycling/`), because that
case produced the most concrete evidence about where the framework bites. They are written to
apply to on-site and community-based processing generally, which is the framing both industry
associations and governments already use.

## 1. What is documented about the present framework

### 1.1 The processing infrastructure has been concentrating for decades

Ontario's count of provincially licensed abattoirs fell from **229 in 1999 to 115 by October
2020**, according to the province's own figures as reported by TVO. An academic account puts
the decline at **138 closures since 1998 — a reduction of just over 50% in sixteen years**.
The Ontario Federation of Agriculture states that the decline in small processors has left
**too few processors to place competitive bids on livestock**, and that abattoirs are "in need
of funding."

This is the concentration story the parent corpus documents in the abstract, with the numbers
attached. What closed was not capacity in aggregate; it was *local* capacity, and the closure
of the nearest kill floor or mill is what removes a farm's option to process at all.

### 1.2 The federal Standing Committee has already recommended most of what follows

*Room to Grow: Strengthening Food Processing Capacity in Canada for Food Security and
Exports* — Standing Committee on Agriculture and Agri-Food, 4th report, 43rd Parliament, 2nd
session (chair Pat Finnigan, April 2021). It heard **57 witnesses over twelve meetings between
November 2020 and March 2021** and made **18 recommendations**. Among them, verbatim:

- **Recommendation 2** — "modernize… regulations and implement targeted programs, in
  collaboration with the provinces and territories, to encourage the development of local
  processing businesses and regional small-scale abattoirs."
- **Recommendation 3** — "identify strategic funding opportunities to address regional
  processing capacity."
- **Recommendation 4** — "increase funding to the Local Food Infrastructure Fund and dedicate
  a specific stream open to a broad group of regional agri-food businesses to promote
  processing capacity across the country."
- **Recommendation 6** — "conduct an external review of its regulations in order to modernize
  them, streamline approvals and remove barriers to bringing new solutions to market, without
  compromising food safety."
- **Recommendation 7** — "seek further harmony between federal and provincial processing
  standards… and encourage innovation through a focus on **outcomes rather than prescriptive
  measures**."
- **Recommendation 17** — encourage banks and institutional investors to establish funds
  providing capital to fast-growing small and medium-sized agri-food firms.

So the argument that the framework obstructs local processing is not this project's invention,
and it is not only a critique from outside government. **The committee's own sixth
recommendation asks for exactly the external regulatory review this document is a draft
contribution to.** That is the single most useful fact in this section: there is a legitimate,
already-legislated-for place to put proposals like these.

### 1.3 The sector's own account, with numbers

The Small Scale Food Processor Association's policy brief, *Small-scale Food Processing*
(24 March 2022), was developed with Dr. Irena Knezevic and based on a scoping review plus
**80 interviews with 83 stakeholders** across British Columbia, Alberta, Ontario and Nova
Scotia. Its findings, quoted:

- "nearly universal frustration with the regulatory frameworks."
- Processors are "subject to **multi-layered regulation, typically designed for larger
  operations and insensitive to unique operating circumstances of small business**."
- "Small-scale processors are often lost in **the patchwork of regulations and agencies**…
  From local health units and zoning departments, to federal grant programs, small enterprises
  are required to interface with all levels of government, and find it difficult and sometimes
  impossible to find the right information, right office, or the right person to assist them."
- "**Permits and licences can take months to process.**"
- The amount of red tape "is in effect, if not in intent, **tailored for larger businesses that
  can afford administrative staffing dedicated to interfacing with various agencies**,
  excluding, even if inadvertently, small businesses from pathways to growth."
- Interprovincial trade barriers remain, and meat processing "is greatly impeded by lack of
  harmonized food safety standards across provinces."
- Two quotes from named participant categories carry the argument better than prose: a
  farmer-microprocessor in Ontario — "**Some of the rules are not about food safety, but about
  aligning the regulations with what the large slaughterhouses have to do for safety.**" A
  cheesemaker in Ontario — "There is no education for processors to prepare them for making
  safe food… We need more apprenticeships for artisanal food making."

Scale context from the same brief (AAFC 2021): the food and beverage processing industry was
worth **$117.8 billion** in 2019, about **2% of GDP**, with roughly **290,000 employees** and
**about 7,800 companies — 91% of them small-scale (under 100 employees)**, against 1% with more
than 500 employees. The sector that regulation is least shaped for is the overwhelming majority
of the sector by firm count.

**The honest qualification:** this is one association's brief, reporting its own commissioned
research, on a self-selected interview sample. It is credible and it is not neutral, and it
should be read as *the sector's account* — which is precisely what a policy design needs to
engage, and not the same thing as a measured finding. The committee record (1.2) is the
weightier evidence because it is a parliamentary process with dissenting members.

### 1.4 What this project's own research adds

From `upcycling/research/` (all documents read 2026-09-11):

- **The licence boundary is a cliff, not a ramp.** Under the Safe Food for Canadians
  Regulations, manufacturing or preserving food for sale within a province requires no federal
  licence; trading it across a provincial border requires one, plus a written preventive
  control plan. A plant does not gradually acquire obligations as it grows; it steps off a ledge
  the first time it sells to a customer in Manitoba.
- **Risk classification does not match the food.** A pH of 4.6 divides high-acid from low-acid
  and is the correct safety line. But Ontario's low-risk exemption list for home-based food
  businesses is bakery-shaped — breads, baked goods without custard, chocolate, granola, nuts,
  cookies — and the health-unit guidance routes the question "are canned preserves low-risk?"
  to canning-specific material instead of answering it. Jams, pickles and preserves, which are
  among the safest shelf-stable products in existence, are left in a default category that the
  regulation treats as potentially hazardous.
- **Validation is a private fixed cost with no commons.** Ontario expects that validating a
  thermal process "must be done by an appropriate laboratory or technology centre with a
  thermal process authority." That validated process is established for a specific product,
  container type and size. There is no public, adoptable, liability-bearing library of validated
  processes for commercial producers — so every micro plant pays the same fixed cost alone and
  rationally keeps the result secret. (The US publishes tested processes for *household* canning
  through the National Center for Home Food Preservation; that public-good precedent exists one
  scale down and not at commercial scale.)
- **Approval attaches to a place, not a process.** Premises approval follows the site, so a
  modular or container facility, or a shared kitchen hosting several producers, meets
  re-approval at each site and each user rather than carrying an approval with it.
- **The compliance interface is fragmented across four authorities.** The health unit governs
  the premises; the municipality governs the building, the grease trap's size and location,
  zoning, business licensing, and change of use; the province governs the framework; and the
  federal regulator appears the moment a product crosses a border. The regulator's own
  published material contains no cost figures at all — a costing exercise the operator must
  undertake alone.
- **The category's defining standards are paywalled.** Upcycled Certified is a fee schedule;
  CSA K100:26, the first Canadian food-loss-and-waste terminology and measurement standard, is a
  purchased document. The vocabulary of the thing being regulated is a product.
- **The cause of the waste stream is entirely private.** Retailer and buyer cosmetic grading
  specifications manufacture much of the surplus that community processing would use, and they
  are uniformly trade secrets. Regulation addresses the symptom downstream and never the
  document upstream.

### 1.5 On the claim that the status quo is broken

Stated precisely, because it matters: the **documented** position is that a parliamentary
committee, after taking testimony from 57 witnesses, recommended a regulatory review to remove
barriers to local processing without compromising food safety (Rec 6); that the association
representing small processors reports near-universal frustration, a patchwork of agencies,
months-long permitting, and rules participants describe as modelled on large plants; that
provincial licensed abattoir capacity in Ontario fell by roughly half in two decades; and that
this project's own document research independently found the cliff, the misclassification, the
privatised validation cost, and the site-bound approval.

What is **not** documented here: that the framework is a net obstacle to an efficient agrifood
system in aggregate, that the consensus extends beyond the associations and committees cited,
or that any specific rule is unnecessary. Those are propositions this document proposes to
test, not findings it reports. Anyone reading this should be able to see exactly which parts
are established and which are ours.

## 2. Diagnosis: five faults

**F1 — Risk is classified by the wrong axis.** The framework regulates by premises category and
product type, with a high-acid/low-acid line that is scientifically correct but applied through
exemption lists that are shaped by the historical businesses they were written for. Safe
products sit in cautious default categories; genuinely risky ones sit next to them.

**F2 — Approval attaches to place, not process.** Everything built for mobility — shared
kitchens, modular facilities, seasonal units that follow the harvest — fights the framework's
assumption that production happens where it is licensed.

**F3 — Fixed costs are privatised and duplicated.** Validation, documentation, hazard analysis
and label compliance are largely volume-independent, they are mandatory, and there is no
mechanism for sharing them. The rational response for each micro operator is to pay in full and
publish nothing.

**F4 — The interface is designed for firms with administrators.** Four authorities, no shared
file, no single entry point, no published cost picture. The committee's Recommendation 6 and the
SSFPA's "patchwork" finding are the same observation from different directions.

**F5 — The information that governs the system is private, and the standards that name it are
sold.** Grading specifications upstream decide what becomes surplus; measurement standards
downstream are paywalled. The regulated party cannot see the rules that decide its inputs, and
must buy the vocabulary used to measure its outputs.

## 3. Design principles

1. **Proportionate to risk.** Obligation should scale with hazard and with the actual
   consequence of failure, not with floor area, headcount, or the historical category a product
   resembles.
2. **Verify the process; register the place.** A validated process and a type-approved facility
   should be portable, so that approval attaches to what was demonstrated rather than to an
   address.
3. **Pay once for what everyone needs.** Where a fixed cost is identical across thousands of
   operators, the public interest is in paying it once and publishing it.
4. **One front door.** A small operator should have one application, one file, and one named
   contact, with the authorities coordinating behind that interface rather than in front of it.
5. **Fund compliance, not just capital.** Grants currently buy equipment. The binding
   constraint is documentation, validation and permitting — which no program funds.
6. **Openness is a compliance asset.** Published intake specifications, batch records and
   processes make the regulator's information problem smaller and the operator's evidence base
   larger. Ontario already publishes food premises inspection results, so the principle has a
   precedent inside the current system.
7. **Refuse where governance requires it.** Community and Indigenous food processing is not
   automatically a transparency problem to be solved; where a community's protocols govern its
   food, the framework should recognise community control rather than override it to a default
   of publication.

## 4. Proposals

Each proposal states what it is, the precedent that makes it plausible, what it fixes, how it
could fail, and what would test it. None is costed; none is drafted; none has been discussed
with any authority.

### P1 — A defined community-processor tier

**Proposed.** Create a third regulatory class between cottage food and full commercial
licensing, defined by hazard class and throughput ceiling rather than by product name, with a
proportionate control set: documented standard operating procedures, calibrated thermometer and
pH records, one-step-back/one-step-forward traceability, food-handler certification, and
mandatory notification to the health unit. Expressly excludes the hazard classes that belong
elsewhere (low-acid canned goods, meat, dairy, and anything requiring refrigeration for
shelf-stability are handled under their own regimes).

**Precedent.** The EU hygiene package's flexibility provisions — Regulation (EC) 852/2004
Article 13 permits derogations from the annexes "in particular to facilitate the implementation
of Article 5 for small businesses, taking into account the relevant risk factors, provided that
such derogations do not affect the achievement of the objectives of this Regulation" — and its
recital position that direct supply of small quantities by the producer to the consumer or a
local retailer is appropriately protected through national law. The US cottage food law
movement is the same idea arrived at state by state.

**Fixes.** F1, F4. It replaces the bakery-shaped exemption list with a hazard-defined class, and
gives a small operator one coherent rulebook instead of a default designed for factories.

**How it could fail.** A tier can become a ceiling: operators stay small to stay in the tier, or
grow and face the same cliff. It can also fail by capture — a tier so permissive that the public
health outcome is undermined, which would poison the reform for a decade. The literature on the
EU's own flexibilities is a caution worth reading before drafting: flexibility on paper is not
proportionality in practice, because the discretion lands on front-line inspectors with no
training in exercising it.

**Test.** Does a defined tier actually reduce the number of operators who would otherwise be
unlicensed, and does it hold its food-safety record against a comparable licensed population?

### P2 — A public process-validation commons

**Proposed.** Fund the validation of common shelf-stable processes — high-acid water-bath
products and fermented vegetable products first — by a competent authority, and publish the
validated processes as adoptable, liability-bearing documents with an adopting-registration
mechanism. An operator who adopts a published process, follows it exactly, and logs the record
has discharged the validation obligation. Variants, new container sizes and new product
formulations still require their own validation.

**Precedent.** The US National Center for Home Food Preservation publishes tested processes at
household scale, and USDA cooperative extension has distributed them for a century; Canada
already has the community-cannery tradition the corpus curated. What does not exist anywhere is
the same public-good move at commercial scale, which is the gap.

**Fixes.** F3 directly, and F5 in part. This is the single change that does most to reduce the
fixed cost of entering community processing, because it converts a cost every operator pays
alone into a cost paid once.

**How it could fail.** Liability: whoever publishes a process owns its adequacy, which is
exactly why no one has published one. It fails if the library is stale, if adoption is
unverifiable in practice, or if the published processes don't match what producers actually
make. It also fails the opposite way — if it crowds out private validation capacity that
genuinely novel products need.

**Test.** Take the two or three highest-volume high-acid products in a jurisdiction, validate
them publicly, and measure adoption and incidents over three years.

### P3 — Approval that travels: type-approved facilities and shared premises

**Proposed.** Two changes. (a) **Type-approval for modular and container facilities:** a
facility design approved once against the building, plumbing, electrical and food-premises
requirements, so that units of that type can be sited with a notification and a site check
rather than a fresh full approval. (b) **A shared-premises licence:** the host holds the
premises approval and is accountable for the building, systems, sanitation and monitoring; each
user registers, holds food-handler certification and insurance, and is accountable for its own
process and product. Both are recorded in a public register.

**Precedent.** Modular construction already has an engineering basis (AgriKit's units are
described as engineered to CSA A277 standards), and Ontario already type-approves mobile food
service equipment through TSSA field approval with a travel-able label and an annual
inspection. The shared-kitchen arrangement already operates in practice — Manning Canning
rents five certified kitchens individually in Toronto — without an explicit regulatory category
for it.

**Fixes.** F2, and much of F4. It legitimises what the market is already doing, and it makes a
relocatable facility — including a unit that follows the harvest — a normal thing rather than an
anomaly.

**How it could fail.** Shared premises can diffuse responsibility: when several producers use
one kitchen, a contamination event's source may be untraceable, and the host's incentive is
occupancy, not enforcement. Type-approval can calcify a design and disadvantage better ones.
Both require the register to actually be maintained, which costs money no one has budgeted.

**Test.** Run a shared-premises pilot with a public register and trace every incident to a user;
compare incident rates against single-occupancy premises.

### P4 — One front door for small operators

**Proposed.** A single application and a single file for small processors, with the health unit,
municipality and (where applicable) the federal regulator working from the same submission, one
named contact for the applicant, published service standards for each decision point, and a
published cost schedule covering every fee the operator will encounter.

**Precedent.** The committee's Recommendation 6 (streamlined approvals); the SSFPA's
"patchwork" finding; and ordinary one-stop business-service practice, which most jurisdictions
already run for business licensing.

**Fixes.** F4. It does not change a single substantive requirement. It changes how many
conversations an operator has to have to comply with them.

**How it could fail.** A single front door that still hides four decision-makers behind it adds
a layer rather than removing one. It fails if the coordination is voluntary rather than
mandated, and it fails if the service standards are unenforceable.

**Test.** Count the elapsed days and the number of distinct contacts required for a small
preserving operation to open, before and after.

### P5 — Fund compliance, not just capital

**Proposed.** Add an eligible cost category for **compliance** to community food infrastructure
programming — process validation, hazard analysis and plan preparation, label and standards
compliance, and the first year of permitting — alongside the equipment it already funds.

**Precedent and the gap, stated precisely.** The Local Food Infrastructure Fund's Small Scale
Projects stream already funds, in its own words, "infrastructure and equipment needed to
establish a community kitchen or food skills program where you take food you've grown to make
soups, pickles, etc." It requires a food-production element, sets a minimum $25,000 in eligible
costs, caps motorized equipment at $50,000 per item, excludes consumables, food and staff
salaries, and requires project completion by a fixed date. **Nothing in that structure pays for
validation, documentation or permitting** — the costs this project's research identifies as the
binding constraint. The committee's Recommendation 4 asked to expand this fund for processing
capacity; this proposal is a specific, small amendment in the same direction.

**Fixes.** F3, in the one place where a funder can fix it cheaply and immediately. It also
aligns incentives: a funded plant that cannot be legally operated is a wasted grant.

**How it could fail.** It becomes a consultant subsidy, with validation providers pricing to the
grant. Guardrails: fixed-fee schedules, published deliverables, and preference for shared rather
than individual work — the point is to pay for the commons, not to buy each operator a private
consultant.

**Test.** Track how many funded projects reach licensed operation within twelve months, with and
without the compliance line.

### P6 — Extend liability protection from donation to processing

**Proposed.** Amend provincial food-donation liability provisions so that protection also covers
a processor that takes diverted surplus under defined handling protocols — particularly where
the output is partly directed back to food-insecure households. The protection should not extend
to negligence in processing, and the conditions should be stated plainly rather than buried.

**Precedent.** Ontario's Donation of Food Act, 1994 provides that a person who donates food, or
distributes donated food, is not liable for damages resulting from injuries or death, subject to
conditions in the Act. The US analogue goes further in exactly the direction proposed here: the
federal Good Samaritan protection extends to donors of food that doesn't meet all quality and
labelling standards **if** the donor informs the recipient, the recipient agrees to recondition
it, and the recipient is competent to do so. That is the "donate it to someone who will process
it" case, already legislated elsewhere.

**Fixes.** A live deterrent in the upcycling case: the concept's own tension analysis found the
legitimacy question is real, and the legal question — who is liable when a processor takes food
a donor could not sell — is part of what makes operators avoid the stream.

**How it could fail.** Liability shields can be read as permission to be careless, and a
well-publicised incident under a shield would set the reform back badly. The conditions must be
specific and the protocols enforceable.

**Test.** Measure whether protection changes sourcing behaviour among processors who currently
decline surplus, and whether incident rates change.

### P7 — Publish the specification, or stop subsidising its secrecy

**Proposed.** Where public money funds the disposal, donation or diversion of food — or where a
public program's targets depend on redirecting it — require that the food be offered to
registered processors against a **published specification** (grade, quantity window, handling
class, and price or free). This does not compel a private retailer to reveal its standards
generally; it attaches a disclosure condition to the public benefit.

**Precedent.** Ontario's mandatory public disclosure of food premises inspection results under
the Food Safety Protocol, 2019 — the principle that information generated in the public
interest is published. The committee's Rec 3 (strategic funding for regional processing
capacity) is the funding half.

**Fixes.** F5. The corpus's upcycling scan found that the single most consequential open
artifact would be the cosmetic grading specifications that manufacture the surplus in the first
place, and that they are uniformly private. This proposal does not open them; it stops the
public purse from subsidising their opacity.

**How it could fail.** Retailers may decline the public program rather than publish, which could
reduce diversion overall — the opposite of the intent. It only works if the public benefit is
large enough to be worth the disclosure.

**Test.** Pilot on one public diversion or donation program and measure both intake by
processors and any fall in participating retailers.

### P8 — Free the measurement standards

**Proposed.** Where a measurement or terminology standard is developed with public money, used
in public reporting, or required for compliance with a public program, publish it freely.
Applies directly to food loss and waste measurement.

**Precedent and the gap.** CSA K100:26 is described by the CSA Group as the first Canadian
standard for defining, measuring and monitoring food loss and waste — a public-interest
measurement standard sold as a purchased document, consistent with the corpus's wider finding
that standards bodies capture value where practices scale. Compare the pattern in data
standards, where a royalty-free standard layer exists and functions.

**Fixes.** F5 in part. A regulator cannot ask an industry to measure something and then charge
it for the definition.

**How it could fail.** Standards bodies fund development through sales; removing that revenue
needs an alternative funding model, and voluntary standards may simply not be produced.

**Test.** Track adoption of the standard in public reporting where free versus paid access has
applied.

### P9 — Community-controlled processing for country and traditional foods

**Proposed.** Establish, negotiated with communities rather than designed for them, a
recognition pathway in which community-controlled processing of country and traditional foods
operates under community protocols with regulatory recognition — including data and record
governance on the community's terms, not a default of publication. The pathway should carry
capital support (a processing unit is a capital item) and the compliance funding proposed in P5.

**Precedent.** The parent corpus's northern/remote findings (G-OSA-39) established that
community ownership, offline-first operation and community-controlled data are already how the
north runs, on Global-South tooling. The Federal Court-recognised OCAP principles and Inuit data
governance set the terms. And the hardware exists: modular container processing units are
already marketed in Canada for processing wild game, fish and seasonal harvests in remote
communities.

**Fixes.** The most consequential gap in the current framework's imagination: it treats a
community's food processing as a premises to be inspected, rather than as a food system with
its own governance, and it treats publication as the default.

**How it could fail.** Recognition can become a two-tier system in which community processing is
held to a lower safety standard as a form of disrespect, which is the opposite of the intent.
The design test is that the safety outcome is equivalent, the governance is the community's, and
neither is traded for the other.

**Test.** Negotiate one pathway, in one region, and publish the terms both parties agreed to.

### P10 — A community processing sandbox with published results

**Proposed.** Time-limited, monitored exemptions for a defined cohort of community processors,
with mandatory reporting, independent evaluation, and the results published whether or not they
flatter the proposal. Sunset unless the evidence justifies continuation.

**Precedent.** Regulatory sandboxes are ordinary practice in other sectors, and the committee's
Rec 6 asks for the review; a sandbox is how a review generates domestic evidence rather than
importing conclusions.

**Fixes.** All five faults, in the only way that is honest: it generates the evidence this
document does not have.

**How it could fail.** Sandboxes can become permanent exemptions by inaction, and can attract
only operators who would have complied anyway — producing a flattering sample. Guardian: a
published evaluation with pre-registered measures and a hard sunset.

**Test.** It is the test.

## 5. What could be done without changing a statute

Worth stating, because it is the cheapest path and the one least likely to be derailed:

- **P2** requires funding and a competent authority, not legislation, if the adopting-registration
  mechanism is voluntary and the liability question is settled by the authority that publishes.
- **P4** is administrative coordination between authorities that already exist.
- **P5** is a program design change, not a regulation.
- **P8** is a procurement and funding condition.
- **P10** is how a regulator tests a change it is not yet willing to make.

P1, P3 and P6 require statutory or regulatory amendment. P7 requires a funding condition plus
the will to attach it. P9 requires negotiation before drafting, and should not be attempted
without it.

## 6. What this document does not claim

- It does not claim consensus beyond the sources named. It claims that a parliamentary committee
  recommended a regulatory review, that the processors' association reports what it reports, and
  that this project's document research independently found five specific faults.
- It does not claim the current framework is unsafe. The claim is about cost distribution,
  proportionality and access, not about food safety outcomes, which this document has not
  measured and could not.
- It does not claim any proposal works. None has been piloted, costed, or tested against an
  administration's constraints.
- It does not claim to speak for small processors, Indigenous communities, or any authority. P9
  in particular is written as an agenda for negotiation, not a position.
- It does not address labour (the corpus's C2 workstream), land, or finance beyond Rec 17, all of
  which bear on whether community processing can survive even under a better framework.
- It is not legal advice, and nothing here should be relied on as a statement of what the law
  currently requires. For that, read the sources in §7 and, where it matters, ask the regulator.
