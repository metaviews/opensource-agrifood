# Open Source Agrifood event — design brainstorm

Status: brainstorm, working draft. Not a brief. Not a commitment.

Date: 2026-08-05
Hosts: future-herd/ salon programme
Project: opensource-agrifood/ (research clearing house / observatory to be built)
Format: online, Discord, four sessions, salon-style, situationist
Outputs: Future Herd podcast episodes + open agrifood clearing house / observatory

## What is already settled (do not relitigate)

- Platform: Discord, designed as curated participatory theatre (Aug 1 venue scan). Use that document's spatial grammar: foyer, main stage, backstage/green room, audience discussion, breakout spaces, roles, text-as-marginalia, bots/webhooks as cues, scheduled events as time framing, persistent commons afterward.
- The research base exists: 58 verified records, 13 scan docs, 21 gaps, 4 contested claims. The headline finding — openness is layered, the layers decouple, and farmer agency is the scarcest layer — is load-bearing.
- Salon precedent: the 2026-08-25 "Feed the People!" salon is a usable rehearsal. It establishes: 90-min rhythm, two-presence model (salon participants on audio/video, public viewers on stream), RSVP-by-email workflow, manual moderation, publisher link not Future Herd position, no fixed agenda promised.
- Situationist tendencies: SAM manifesto frames the broader practice. The salon is one mode — there is no obligation to import the whole SAM tactical repertoire.

## Your four sessions, annotated

Your framing — agents/applications, research, open source, ethics/policy — is the canonical technology-policy quartet. It is clean, defensible, and program-friendly. The risk is that it becomes a sequence of four panels where smart people explain things.

A salon needs friction, surprise, and a reason to be in the room together. So the question is not whether the four topics are right, but whether they should be sessions, lenses, rooms, or something else. Some options:

### Option A — four topical sessions, one topic each (your current frame)
- Session 1: Agents and applications
- Session 2: Research
- Session 3: Open source (the central, perhaps longer one)
- Session 4: Ethics and policy
- Pro: clean, citable, podcast-friendly.
- Con: each one is a genre that agrifood already has conferences for. Risk of being "yet another panel."

### Option B — three topical sessions + one situation
- Sessions 1–3 as above.
- Session 4: a "situation" rather than a panel — a constructed scene (a provocation, a mock hearing, a trial, an absent-everyone debate, a ghost audience of farmers who could not attend whose voices are present in recordings, etc.). This is where the situationist tendency pays off. Outputs from sessions 1–3 become material the situation performs against.
- Pro: the situation becomes the memorable thing. Podcast episode 4 has a shape no one else has.
- Con: requires more production risk; harder to sponsor or sell.

### Option C — four sessions, each oriented around a current from the landscape
Use the seven currents (farmer-maker, state DPI, open-core + paid services, commons governance without licences, donor-funded farmer-centric paradigm, Global South trust-and-transfer, regulation as access not control) as the spine. Each session picks a current and runs a salon that lets practitioners from competing positions in that current talk to each other.
- Pro: this surfaces the actual contested terrain in agrifood, not the abstract topics. A panel of "JoinData vs. farmer co-op vs. CEADS vs. data-privacy researcher" is more alive than "open data policy."
- Con: less intuitive for press; harder to promote by topic.

### Option D — four rooms in one server, four slow sessions
Run all four lenses as ongoing rooms in the Discord over the run of the programme. Each "session" is a 90-minute salon inside one room. Between sessions the other three rooms stay open for asynchronous discussion and material accumulation.
- Pro: this is exactly Discord's spatial grammar. The server is the programme, not a sequence of events.
- Con: more moderation labor; less podcast-crisp (you have to edit across rooms).

My instinct is to blend A and B or A and C. Keep your four lenses as the *spine*, but make at least one session a constructed situation, and orient the panel construction around the contested currents rather than around topics.

## What you are missing

Things that do not fit neatly inside the four-lens frame, in rough order of importance:

### 1. The farmers, explicitly
The headline finding of your own research is "farmer agency is the scarcest layer." A four-session programme about open source in agrifood that does not have farmers in the room, on mic, on equal terms with researchers and technologists, will reproduce the structural problem your research documents. The salon format already helps — salons are flatter than keynotes — but the question of *who is invited and who is paid to be there* is decisive. Travel stipends, time-zone-friendly scheduling, low-bandwidth and audio-only participation paths, language interpretation, compensation norms for non-institutional participants. Without this, the situationist framing becomes a style applied to a gathering that is still the usual gathering.

### 2. Language, voice, and low-bandwidth access as a first-class design constraint
Your landscape synthesis shows the language column is the strongest deployment evidence in the open-AI stack — and that it is all managed, not farmer-governed. Common Voice itself is research/pilot only. The salon should not be a video-only or English-only room. At minimum: text chat as a fully featured participation path (not a back-channel), transcripts as canonical artifacts, language interpretation for at least one non-English session, voice-only and low-bandwidth testing. This is also the cheapest way to make the event actually global instead of North American + invited internationals.

### 3. The clearing house / observatory as a *salon output*, not a separate workstream
The clearing house is more credible if it is built during the event, not after. Concrete options: every dossier entry that gets referenced in a session becomes a curated record on the spot; the contested-claims ledger is updated live; the gap register gains entries that the salon surfaces; a session produces a structured artifact (a candidates list, a research agenda, an open letter, a forked taxonomy) that the post-event commons continues to work on. The salon is then a research instrument, not a performance. This also makes the podcast episodes less like coverage and more like "voices that contributed to v3 of the synthesis."

### 4. Time and pacing
Three or four sessions over what span? A single week compresses the afterlives; a month spreads the moderation. Three sessions at fortnightly cadence is a reasonable default. The salons should not be too long (90 minutes, matching the Feed the People! template) and should not promise a fixed agenda. Two-presence model (salon participants on audio/video, public viewers on stream) lets the situation breathe.

### 5. Naming and invitations
Salon culture lives or dies on its title and its first-page invitation. "Open Source Agrifood Salon #1: Agents" is a programme. "Feeding the Algorithm" or "The Sealed Source" or "Who Owns the Yield" is a situation. The title should signal that something is going to be examined, not merely discussed. Working titles worth trying out: *The Sealed Source*, *Feeding the Algorithm*, *Commoning the Commons*, *Who Counts the Yield*. Pick a title and let it carry.

### 6. The Discord server itself as a curated environment
This is the bet from your prior venue scan. The server is the artwork, not the platform. Decisions to make early:
- Channel structure: small, legible, plain-language labels.
- Roles: audience, salon participant, speaker, facilitator, observer-from-the-field (e.g. farmers, co-op staff), moderator, archivist. Each role has a visible purpose.
- Onboarding: a single short text-channel walkthrough that takes a first-time participant from "I just clicked the link" to "I understand where the salon happens and where I can talk."
- Marginalia: a parallel text channel functioning as live notes and citations. Cite as you speak. The transcripts become primary documents.
- The post-event commons: a #commons channel that survives the salons. The observatory is born here.
- Avoid: bots, gamification, badges, default notification noise, gaming-era jargon.

### 7. Production infrastructure
The salons need: someone running the stage channel, someone in the green room briefing speakers, someone writing live notes, someone watching the chat for safety and for serendipity, someone handling the public stream. Two to three production humans per session is a realistic minimum. The transcripts feed the podcast; the live notes feed the research base; the chat logs feed the observatory. Without this triad, the event is reduced to a stream and a podcast.

### 8. The podcast as an output, not the event
The Future Herd podcast is one artifact of the salon. Other artifacts: a published transcript per session, a curated dossier per session, a research-question update, a post-event public letter or situation report, an updated landscape synthesis v3. If the podcast is the only output, the salon becomes a content farm. If the salon is treated as a research instrument, the podcast becomes one of several honest records.

### 9. Ethics, safety, and the consent architecture
Especially with farmer participants and Global South interlocutors: recording consent, attribution norms, the right to be quoted vs. the right to be heard, the right to speak off-record in the green room, the difference between public text and salon speech. This is a structural design decision, not a footnote.

### 10. Money
Travel stipends for non-institutional participants, speaker honoraria or fair-use alternatives, transcription and translation, podcast production, Discord boost (if needed for stage-video capacity), a small contingency for accessibility. The event's openness claim depends on whether people who are not already paid to be in these conversations can show up.

## Questions to decide before we go further

1. Four sessions as topics, or four rooms / four currents / four situations? My recommendation: keep four salons as your spine, but reframe at least one as a constructed situation and orient panels around contested currents rather than abstract topics.
2. Cadence: one week, fortnight, month?
3. Who is the invited audience? Researchers, technologists, farmers, co-op staff, public-interest journalists, policy people, civil-society groups — and in what proportions? The proportions are the politics.
4. Are we paying farmer participants and Global South interlocutors? If yes, the budget determines who can actually come.
5. Language: English-only plus transcripts, or one bilingual session, or interpretation throughout?
6. What is the clearing house in concrete terms by the end of the four salons? A dossier library? An updated landscape synthesis? A federated observatory? A podcast-and-corpus bundle?
7. What is the *one* thing we want a participant to remember in 12 months?
