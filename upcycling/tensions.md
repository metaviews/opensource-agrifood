# Tensions the concept has to carry

These are not problems to be solved before starting. They are contradictions the concept
holds open, because resolving them prematurely would settle questions the concept is not
entitled to settle yet.

## The surplus does not obviously belong to the processor — but it is not scarce

*Revised 2026-09-11. The first version of this section asserted that a for-profit processor
enters the surplus hierarchy late and as a competitor for a stream charity receives free. The
availability half of that was wrong, and it was wrong because it reasoned from policy
convention rather than from practice.*

The stream is ordered conventionally: food banks and rescue organizations first, animal-feed
diversion second (Loop Resource moves grocery surplus to farms as feed; FoodMesh handles the
donation channel for much of the same retail surplus), composting and landfill last. A
processing operation that buys surplus for a commercial product is outside that hierarchy
entirely, and there is no way to argue that its claim ranks above a food bank's.

But the ordering describes a claim convention, not necessarily the flow. Whether a great deal
of food continues to be wasted, and whether an upcycling marketplace layer is emerging that
sells surplus to processors at low cost, are empirical questions this concept has not
answered — they are research target 4, and the direction for asking came from experience that
cannot be independently confirmed. If it holds, the processor is not fighting charity over a
fixed and nearly exhausted pool. It is a buyer in a forming market, alongside other non-charity
buyers.

The tension that remains is therefore reputational and ethical rather than material:

- A business making margin from food that a charity would have taken for free still has to
  justify the position. The available justification — that it processes what donation cannot
  place in time, at the volumes and grades donation cannot absorb — is a real one, but it is
  a claim about the operation's actual intake, and it is falsifiable. If the plant's intake
  turns out to be the same grade and volume a food bank wanted, the justification is gone and
  the concept should say so plainly rather than reach for the "too ripe, too much" defence.
- The reverse risk now matters more than the original one: if low-cost commercial surplus is
  abundant, an operation could quietly become a buyer in a market that exists precisely
  because the fresh market's grading rules discard food that is fine. Sourcing from that
  market is cheap, legal, and does nothing about the cause. The concept should not confuse
  participating in a waste market with reducing waste.

## Openness versus copying

Publishing a formulation is not like publishing software. A competitor can take a recipe and
out-manufacture it: their plant is bigger, their jars are cheaper, their distribution is
wider, and nothing about the recipe licence prevents them from eating the market the open
operation built. The licence can require attribution; it cannot require fairness.

This is why the ladder has formulations at stage 3 and not stage 1. But the tension does not
vanish at stage 3: a federation of five small plants sharing recipes is still five small
plants competing with larger closed manufacturers who can read the commons for free.

Counter-consideration: the same was said about open-source software for decades, and the
answer there was that the commons changes what is worth competing on. Whether that transfers
to a physical product with a jar, a shelf life, and a freight cost is exactly the kind of
question that should be answered with evidence, not analogy.

## Validation is a fixed cost in a sector of tiny operators

Getting a shelf-stable process right — validated, documented, defensible to an inspector —
costs roughly the same whether you run 500 jars or 50,000. That is the structural reason the
sector is opaque: every small operator pays it alone and rationally keeps the result private,
so the cost is paid over and over and nothing accumulates.

This is the strongest argument for the concept's second rung (open food-safety
documentation), and it is also the hardest to make legally comfortable, because sharing a
process plan means sharing responsibility for its adequacy. The corpus already holds the
warning case: the only open HACCP document it found (Underground Meats, CC0) is dead-hosted,
its host gone. Opening a liability document and then abandoning it is worse than leaving it
closed.

## Seasonality versus shelf-stable ambition

The operation is built to convert gluts into year-round product, which means capacity must
be sized for the peak and then sit idle. That produces the defining cost problem: fixed
assets and seasonal labour, against a cost base that does not scale down in the off-months.

The corollary is a labour problem: the peak processing weeks are the same weeks the farm
season peaks, so the plant competes for exactly the labour that is scarcest and most
expensive during exactly the weeks it needs it most. A plant that only works three months a
year cannot offer year-round employment, and a plant that cannot offer year-round employment
cannot attract the people who would get good at the work.

Possible answers (all unverified): contract processing for other producers in the off-season;
retort or frozen input to smooth the year; a product mix that includes steady raw input;
multi-plant federation where plants peak at different times. None is obviously sufficient.

## Certification is both credibility and capture

The category's credibility layer is a paid product — Upcycled Certified runs on application
and per-product fees, CSA K100:26 is a purchased standard. Both are exactly the
value-capture pattern the corpus keeps finding, and both are also the things that make an
"upcycled" claim legible to a buyer who has been burned by greenwashing.

So the operation faces a choice it does not like: pay into a closed standard for
credibility, or publish its own evidence and compete against the standard's recognizability.
The concept's instinct — that stage 1's open intake spec and batch record are *better*
evidence than a certification mark, because they are specific and checkable — is plausible
but unproven. Buyers often want the logo.

## The upstream cause stays closed

The scan's sharpest finding: the grading and cosmetic specifications that create the waste
stream are trade secrets, so openness downstream of them never touches the cause. An open
processor can publish everything it does with the surplus and still leave the mechanism that
declares the surplus unsellable entirely private.

The concept can carry this honestly in two ways, and probably both: publish the intake
specification as a visible counter-artifact (what this plant will take, and what it pays),
and state plainly that the operation is a downstream response to a problem it does not
address. Claiming more would be the same openness-as-identity move the scan documented in
food rescue.

## Viability may argue against openness

This is the tension the concept most needs to hold without flinching. If a specific rung of
the ladder — say, open formulations — measurably reduces the chance the pilot survives, then
a concept that is genuinely about viability has to say so and skip it, even though the
project's subject is open source.

The honest formulation is a conditional: the operation commits to the rungs that are net
positive on both cost and mission, publishes which rungs it has not taken and why, and does
not describe itself as open source in a way that implies the rungs it skipped. Gradual
openness is only an improvement over opacity if the gradient is real and the current
position on it is stated.

## What these tensions add up to

Several of them resolve the same way: toward a smaller, more specific, more honest operation
than the concept's ambition would prefer. That is probably correct — but it is worth naming
as a pattern, because it can also be a way of talking a concept out of its own point.
