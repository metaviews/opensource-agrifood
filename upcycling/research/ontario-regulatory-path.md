# Ontario regulatory path for a micro preserving plant

Research date: 2026-09-11
Method: document gathering for `upcycling/research-direction.md` target 1, plus the practice
question attached to each document. Ontario was chosen as the pilot jurisdiction on
2026-09-11. Everything here is a document read from a government or health-unit source;

The direction for asking came partly from experience that cannot be independently confirmed,
and is not used as evidence — see the anticonflation rule and the evidence classes in
`../concept.md` and `../research-direction.md`.

Register context: sibling to the A1 processing workstream and its upcycling sub-cell

## The overall shape, verified

Ontario does not have a single provincial "food processor licence" for this kind of product.
What exists is a layered regime:

| Layer | Instrument | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| Federal | Safe Food for Canadians Regulations | An SFC licence is required to manufacture/process/preserve food for interprovincial trade or export. No licence for food manufactured and sold within the province. Even without a licence, SFCR preventive controls, PCP, traceability, packaging, and labelling requirements may apply. |
| Federal | SFCR Part 4 Div. 6 | A written preventive control plan is generally required if you hold a licence — **but** the CFIA notes certain businesses may not be required to have one, "for example, if their annual gross food sales are $100,000 or less." |
| Provincial | Health Protection and Promotion Act, s. 16 | Before opening, the operator must **give notice of intent to the public health unit** and provide information about food preparation, storage, and sale on request. |
| Provincial | O. Reg. 493/17 (Food Premises) | Sets out the requirements for any premises where food is manufactured, processed, prepared, stored, handled, distributed, transported, sold or offered for sale — including a home kitchen used for food business. At least one **Certified Food Handler must be on site at all times** (s. 32). Some requirements are relaxed or exempted for low-risk food. |
| Provincial | Food Safety Protocol, 2019 | Requires public health units to publicly disclose inspection findings — premises type, name, address, inspection date. Ontario already publishes food premises inspection outcomes. |
| Municipal | Building Code, zoning, business licensing | Municipalities control location, water and energy use, and waste disposal; business licensing, building approval, zoning and fire code are municipal. |
| Other provincial | Toxics Reduction Act, environmental and labour law | Ontario's guide states these are triggered by the technology, process, or products used, independently of food safety licensing. |

## The low-risk / home-based carve-out does not clearly cover preserves

This is the finding that most changes the concept's assumptions, and it contradicts what the
sector's folklore (and at least one aggregator site) says.

York Region Public Health's "Home-Based Food Business" guidance (read 2026-09-11) lists
examples of **low-risk** foods that are exempt from some Food Premises Regulation
requirements: breads and buns without meat or cream fillings, most baked goods with no
custard, chocolate, coffee, tea leaves, granola, nuts, brownies, muffins, cookies. Preserves,
pickles, and jams are **not** on that list.

The same document defines **potentially hazardous foods** as those supporting bacterial
growth, including "moist foods with a pH above 4.5," and answers the direct question
"Are canned preserves considered low-risk foods?" by stating that the risk depends on the
type of food being canned and the processing method used, referring operators to a
"Safe Canning for Operators" guidance document.

Reading, stated as a reading: **the home-kitchen lane is not established for canned
preserves in Ontario.** The exemption list is bakery-shaped, and the preserves question is
routed to canning-specific guidance rather than answered with an exemption. For a micro plant
this points to an approved commercial premises with a certified food handler on site, not a
domestic kitchen — which is a materially different capital requirement.

Note also that a home-based business preparing potentially hazardous food still needs a
Certified Food Handler on site, a designated hand-washing sink, a 2- or 3-compartment sink or
commercial dishwasher, and a posted proof-of-inspection sign.

The counter-claim on record: an aggregator site (PickYourOwn.org) states that Ontario "allows
producers to sell some lower-risk homemade items… Pickles, jams and preserves." It is a
secondary source with no citation to the regulation, and it conflicts with the health-unit
guidance above. Recorded here only because it shows how the folklore travels; not relied on.

## Fees: verified where a source states them

- **Health unit notice of intent: no fee.** Timiskaming Health Unit's "Opening or Renovating
  a Food Premises" guide states, in capitals, "THERE IS NO FEE FOR THIS APPLICATION, NOR FOR
  ITS PROCESSING." Multiple health units describe a Notice of Intent to Operate a Food
  Premises form as the required step. Recorded as verified for that health unit; whether every
  Ontario health unit operates identically is **not** established here.
- **Municipal business licence fee: not established.** Municipalities license businesses
  separately from the health unit; no Ontario municipality's fee schedule for this activity
  was read for this pass. Unverified.
- **Certified food handler training: cost not established.** The requirement is verified
  (O. Reg. 493/17 s. 32; required at all times the premises operates). Course prices were not
  read for this pass.
- **Federal SFC licence: fee not read.** Not established in this pass.

## What the documents say about the facility itself

From O. Reg. 493/17 and York Region's simplified restatement, the physical requirements that
actually shape a plant budget are: a designated hand-washing sink with hot and cold running
water, soap and towels; a two- or three-compartment sink or a commercial dishwasher for
utensils; smooth, non-absorbent, cleanable surfaces; adequate ventilation, lighting, garbage
disposal, and storage; accurate working thermometers in every temperature-control unit;
potable water, and a small drinking water system compliance question if water is not
municipal (O. Reg. 319/08). Commercial dishwashing equipment is not required for a home-based
low-risk business, but the multi-compartment sink or dishwasher requirement applies to
potentially hazardous food preparation.

## What this file does not demonstrate

- It does not state what any Ontario health unit actually inspects, how often, or how it
  treats a small preserving operation. The documents set the envelope; the practice question
  is open and is not answered from experience here.
- It does not price the facility. Municipal licence fees, certified-handler course costs, the
  cost of fitting out a compliant small space, and insurance are all unverified.
- It does not establish whether a specific Ontario product (e.g. a fruit jam at pH 3.4)
  qualifies as low-risk; the health-unit guidance points to canning-specific material instead
  of answering, and the safer reading is that it does not.
- It is not a substitute for the health unit of the municipality where the plant would sit.
  Ontario has 36 local boards of health and the operator-facing material is written per unit.
- It does not address the US market, which brings an entirely separate regime (see
  `process-validation-and-canning.md`).
