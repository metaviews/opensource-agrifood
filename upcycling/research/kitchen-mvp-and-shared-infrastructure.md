# The minimum viable kitchen, and shared/ghost kitchen infrastructure

Research date: 2026-09-11
Method: document and quote gathering, per the evidence classes in `../research-direction.md`.
Published rates, published equipment lists, and government requirements only. Prices that
exist only "on application" are recorded as such rather than estimated. This file answers two
questions: what is the least expensive compliant kitchen a micro preserving plant could
actually run in, and what state is the rentable shared/ghost kitchen sector in as a source of
infrastructure.

## The MVP question, reframed

The first pass on facility cost asked "what does a compliant kitchen cost." The better
question is "what is the cheapest compliant kitchen that could actually run," and the answer
turns out to be a sequencing question rather than a construction question. Four routes, in
ascending order of capital commitment:

| Route | Character | Capital | Evidence in this file |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Rent certified kitchen hours | Pay by the hour in someone else's inspected premises | ~$0 | Verified Ontario rates, named facilities, published equipment lists |
| B. Second-generation food space | Lease a space that was already a kitchen (hood, drains, sinks, grease trap in place) | Low lease + make-good | No Ontario price obtained — open |
| C. Modular/container processing unit | Buy a factory-built certified facility, delivered | Capital purchase, grant-fundable | Named Canadian provider with published specification and timeline; **no published price** |
| D. Build out from a shell | Lease a bare space and construct a food premises | Highest | Cost drivers verified from regulation; no prices |

The finding that matters: **for a seasonal operation, Route A is not merely the cheapest way
to start — it is structurally cheaper than owning a kitchen at the volumes this concept
assumes** (arithmetic below). The pilot should not build anything until the hourly route has
been exhausted, and the concept should treat "own a kitchen" as a later and separate decision
from "process food."

## What a compliant Ontario kitchen must actually have

Verified from Halton Region Health Department's construction and renovation guidance for food
premises operators (read 2026-09-11), which restates O. Reg. 493/17 requirements:

- **Sinks:** a separate hand sink in each food preparation area, supplied with paper towels
  and liquid soap in dispensers; a 3-compartment sink **or** an approved commercial dishwasher
  for multi-use utensils; if a dishwasher is used, a 2-compartment sink large enough for the
  largest pot must also be provided; a separate mop sink to prevent contaminating food and
  dishwashing areas.
- **Dishwasher performance:** all commercial dishwashers need accurate temperature gauges;
  high-temperature machines must reach 82°C (180°F) in the final rinse; low-temperature
  machines must use approved sanitizing chemicals.
- **Floors, walls, ceilings:** tight, smooth and non-absorbent floor coverings in kitchen,
  storage and washrooms — the guidance names vinyl and ceramic tile as examples; walls and
  ceilings with an easily cleanable finish; acoustic tile not acceptable in the food
  preparation area.
- **Ventilation:** all cooking equipment, dishwashing equipment and washrooms require
  mechanical ventilation vented to the outside; ventilation over cooking equipment must have
  an exhaust fan, canopy and filters. The health unit directs the operator to the municipal
  building department and the fire department for details.
- **Grease trap:** the health unit directs the operator to the Building and Licensing
  Department for **location and size** requirements. It is the municipality, not the health
  unit, that decides this.
- **Lighting:** sufficient for cleaning in kitchen, preparation and storage areas; food
  preparation lighting must meet Ontario Building Code requirements enforced by the local
  building department.
- **Temperature control:** refrigerators at 4°C or less with accurate readable thermometers;
  freezers at −18°C; hot holding at 60°C or above.
- **Storage, garbage, washrooms, potable water:** garbage containers durable, cleanable and
  rodent-proof inside and out; washrooms must not open directly into food processing areas;
  a private sewage disposal system may need a permit for a change of use; well water must
  test at zero coliform and zero E. coli.
- **Noise in the guidance worth noting:** no cost figures at all. The regulator specifies
  performance, never price.

**The strategic reading:** the expensive parts of a compliant kitchen are the *building*
systems — drainage and grease interception, mechanical ventilation over cooking equipment,
flooring, wall finishes, lighting to Building Code — not the cooking equipment. That is why
Route A and Route B are dramatically cheaper than Route D: both inherit systems somebody else
already paid for and already had inspected.

## Route A: certified kitchen hours (verified Ontario rates and facilities)

### Manning Canning Kitchens (Leaside, Toronto) — the closest precedent in the province

The single most relevant facility found in this pass. Manning Canning is a Toronto preserves
company operating a facility at 105 Vanderhoof Ave, Unit 8, that has been **divided into five
kitchens, each rented individually**. Listed on The Kitchen Door (read 2026-09-11):

- **Published rate: "$22.00 – $38.50/hr"**, on a sliding scale based on how the space is used.
- **Published equipment list** — and it is, almost exactly, a preserves equipment list: two
  **30-gallon steam kettles**, a **piston filler**, a Robot Coupe (×2), immersion blender,
  combi oven, two convection ovens, a 10-burner stove with two ovens, grill, four induction
  burners, 10-quart Hobart mixer, table-top mixer, **industrial dishwasher**, and small wares.
- Storage: dry, refrigerated, and limited frozen. 24/7 access, free parking, a loading door,
  and a kitchen manager who will accept deliveries Monday–Friday with notice.
- Requirements: a copy of your food handler's certificate, and the kitchen must be added to
  your general liability insurance as an additional insured party with **$2 million minimum
  coverage**.
- Accepted business stages explicitly include **"Brand New (concept phase, no sales)"**, and
  accepted types include **Packaged Goods (CPG)** and Farmer — this is a facility that will
  rent to a pre-revenue preserving business.
- Lease types offered: month-to-month, 6-month, annual, 2-year, more than 2-year.
- The operator also sells **consulting for consumer packaged goods companies** "to help you
  kick start your path to retail, or to help you manage growth as you scale up."
- Listing states "many of Toronto's most successful start-ups have started here."

The concept should take three things from this. First, a preserves-capable certified kitchen
already exists in Ontario and can be rented by the month with no capital. Second, the
equipment that a small preserves operation most needs to avoid buying — the steam kettles and
the filler — is exactly what such a kitchen has. Third, the arrangement is *also* how a
preserves company diversifies its own revenue: the incumbent rents capacity to the next
entrant, which is a business model the concept should register rather than assume away.

### Other verified Ontario rates

- **Syzl's GTA list (published March 2026, secondary/interested source)** names five shared
  kitchens: Newmarket $22/hr (gas range, deep fryer, full hot prep, curbside pickup, loading
  area, free parking); Ajax $25/hr (six gas stoves, flat top, deep fryer, cold and dry
  storage); a nut-free kitchen in the Stockyards at $28/hr; a downtown "Cathedral Kitchen" at
  $30/hr described as functional and bare-bones; a St. Clair West bakery/prep kitchen at
  $30/hr with 148 bookable slots a week. The same source states certified GTA shared kitchens
  "start at $22/hour" and that most starting food entrepreneurs spend **$200–400/month for
  8–16 hours** of production time.
- The same page states the entry requirements to rent: a food handler's certificate (Toronto
  Public Health requires it; about four hours online, roughly $35, per that source), general
  liability insurance with a $2M minimum at **$50–150/month** depending on revenue, and an
  Ontario business number.

### The arithmetic that settles the sequencing question

Using only the published figures above: renting at Manning Canning's $22–$38.50/hr against a
published Toronto **ghost kitchen suite at $3,500/month, all included** (The Kitchen Door),
break-even is **≈91 hours/month at $38.50, or ≈159 hours/month at $22**. Below roughly
90–160 production hours a month, paying by the hour is cheaper than holding a dedicated
suite; above it, the dedicated suite starts to win.

For a seasonal operation that processes gluts in concentrated windows and is idle otherwise,
that threshold is the whole argument. A pilot running two or three harvest weeks plus
intermittent top-up production is very unlikely to clear 91 hours a month year-round, which
means the capital question can be deferred until the operation has a reason to be in a kitchen
most weeks. **The concept's facility problem is therefore a scheduling problem before it is a
capital problem.**

Caveat on the comparison: the two figures come from different sources and describe different
things — hourly access to a shared preserves-equipped kitchen versus an all-in dedicated suite
whose equipment may be delivery-shaped rather than processing-shaped (see the ghost kitchen
section below). The arithmetic compares published prices; it does not compare like facilities.

## Route B: second-generation food space

Leasing a space that was previously a food premises — a failed restaurant, a closed bakery, a
caterer's unit — inherits the expensive systems: the hood and make-up air, the floor drains
and grease interception, the sinks, the washable floors and walls, the utility capacity. It is
the route the sector talks about most and, in this pass, the one with **no Ontario price
obtained**. Nothing in this file should be read as pricing it.

What the documents do establish is what determines whether a given space can be converted:
the municipality decides grease trap location and size (health unit defers to the building
department); mechanical ventilation over cooking equipment must be vented outside and the
health unit directs the operator to the building and fire departments; floors and walls must
be non-absorbent and cleanable; lighting must meet Building Code; and a private sewage system
may need a permit for a change of use. Zoning and business licensing are municipal, and the
Ontario manufacturing guide adds that environmental and labour obligations are triggered by
the process and equipment used, independently of food safety licensing.

Named ask: a commercial realtor or a lease listing search for former food premises in the
pilot municipality, with the equipment list and the municipal zoning designation attached.

## Route C: modular and container-based processing facilities — AgriKit

Investigated at the user's direction, 2026-09-11. **AgriKit** (agrikit.ca) designs, builds and
delivers modular food processing and cold storage facilities inside 20-foot and 40-foot
shipping containers, turnkey, across Canada. Verified from the company's own site:

- **Engineering basis:** units are described as weather-resistant shipping containers
  "engineered to meet CSA A277 standards" and outfitted with commercial-grade equipment.
- **Three product families:** an Agricultural unit (post-harvest wash, cool, pack, store at the
  farm gate); a Hunting & Fishing unit (wild game and seafood processing, curing and storage,
  built for remote environments); and an Off-Grid unit (solar power, water cisterns, automated
  backup generators).
- **Published customization options** relevant to a preserving operation: commercial-grade
  flooring, spray foam insulation in walls/floor/ceiling, commercial kitchen grade wall
  panelling, a 100 A 240 V panel, plumbing with sink rough-ins and connection for an external
  water source, air conditioning and heating, a walk-in cooler, "multiple sinks, washing
  stations, packing tables and other post harvest/cleaning equipment or specialized
  equipment," custom shelving and dry storage, and an attached greenhouse option.
- **Process and timeline:** discovery and consultation with a preliminary quote; custom design
  delivering detailed specifications, equipment lists, layout drawings, 2D and 3D renders and a
  comprehensive final quote; fabrication 3–4 months with the remaining balance due before
  delivery; delivery 1–2 weeks; **typically 4–6 months from contract to operation**. The
  company states it handles design, fabrication and delivery with a one-day site installation.
- **Grant support:** AgriKit offers free grant-application writing support for non-profits,
  charities, and Indigenous communities seeking funding to buy a unit, and states it will help
  identify funding opportunities.
- **Stated applications** include non-profits and charities (community kitchens, food banks,
  meal programs, food rescue operations), Indigenous communities processing traditional foods,
  seasonal operations, remote communities, farms adding value-added products, and food hubs.
- **Company:** founded by Chris Hildreth (previously founded TOPSOIL, described as a 20,000
  sq ft urban farm supplying 30+ restaurants) and Mike Paterson (CPA, co-founder/CFO).
- **Price: not published.** The site directs all pricing to a quote after consultation.

**Why this is significant for the concept, stated carefully:** AgriKit is a Canadian supplier
of exactly the artifact the concept's facility question needs — a certified, relocatable,
turnkey processing space — and it addresses the concept's central structural problem
(seasonality) directly, because a portable unit can be sited where the glut is and moved as
the season moves. Grant support for non-profits and Indigenous communities also connects to
the concept's culture and structure layers and to the corpus's northern/remote findings
(G-OSA-39): a containerised processing facility for country food is already a productised
thing in this country.

**Open and unresolved:**

- No price. "Comprehensive final quote" after consultation. **The single most useful number
  this concept could obtain is a quote for a preserving-equipped 20-foot unit.**
- Whether a container unit satisfies Ontario's requirements as a food premises — municipal
  building permit, change-of-use, health unit premises approval, plumbing and grease
  interception, and electrical (the site mentions a 100 A panel and an accredited electrical
  approval for mobile equipment above 30 VAC exists via TSSA/ESA rules for mobile food service
  equipment, but that regime is written for fuel-fired cooking in a mobile unit, not
  necessarily for a stationary container processing facility). **Unverified and important.**
- Whether the units are relocatable in practice under municipal zoning once sited — the site
  says easy relocation, which is a claim about hardware, not about permits.
- CSA A277 is a standard for pre-fabricated/modular buildings; the site describes the units as
  meeting it. The standard's text was not read in this pass.
- AgriKit is a closed commercial vendor with no open-source component identified. It is
  recorded here as infrastructure the concept could **buy or rent**, and as a lead for the
  parent corpus's A1 processing workstream — **not** as an open artifact, and not curated into
  `examples/`.

## Ghost kitchens and shared kitchens as rentable infrastructure

### The market state, verified

The delivery-oriented ghost kitchen asset class built in 2020–22 has largely failed, and the
failures are documented rather than inferred:

- **Kitchen United**, the segment's pioneer, announced in November 2023 that it would **sell or
  close all its physical locations and pivot to software**, having already shut all eight of
  its Kroger units (44% of an 18-unit footprint). It had raised $100 million the previous year.
- **REEF Technology** lost its Wendy's deal in May 2023, which at one point contemplated 700
  Reef kitchens, and has closed hundreds of locations.
- **CloudKitchens** laid off staff in September 2023; one account of the segment reports its
  facilities running at **50% occupancy in early 2023** and **41 of 71 restaurants closed
  across five locations**.
- **C3** closed at least one food hall; **Butler Hospitality** dissolved; **Wonder** abandoned
  its van-based model.
- A market-analysis source states **no major US ghost kitchen company has raised funding since
  2021** and that IBISWorld reports the US ghost kitchen industry **contracting at −2.2%**.
- This all belongs to the segment's *dedicated delivery-facility* model. It is not evidence
  that shared kitchens generally failed; the commissary and hourly-rental sector (Manning
  Canning, the Syzl listings, The Kitchen Door's network) is a different and older business.

### What is verifiable in Ontario

- **CloudKitchens operates a Toronto location** (a public location page exists). No Toronto
  rate was obtained in this pass.
- **A published Toronto ghost kitchen price:** a Kitchen Door listing titled "Ghost Kitchen for
  Rent – College & Bathurst – **$3,500 All Included**" at 74 Lippincott St, Toronto. This is
  the source used in the break-even arithmetic above. What "all included" covers, whether
  production (as opposed to delivery-only assembly) is permitted under the premises approval,
  and whether the landlord allows jarred shelf-stable products are all **unverified**.
- The Kitchen Door's Toronto directory also lists MCK Inc./Manning Canning, Le Jasmin bakery,
  Boutique Production Kitchen, Growth Kitchen, Eat Drink Laugh, and the Sheridan Student Union
  Mississauga campus — i.e. a working directory of Ontario certified production kitchens
  exists and is publicly searchable, which is itself infrastructure the concept can use.

### The assessment

For this concept, the ghost kitchen boom matters less as a source of *suitable* kitchens than
as a source of **cheap, equipped, under-used space whose owners want it filled**, and as a
cautionary case. Two readings, both held:

1. **The cautionary reading.** The delivery-suite model failed because the suites were built
   for order-by-order cooking in dense delivery catchments: small footprints, no loading dock,
   no dry storage, no bulk processing capacity, no room for a pallet of jars. A preserves
   operation needs almost the opposite profile — bulk input, bulk storage, case output, truck
   access, and long dwell times. A ghost kitchen suite is a bad shape for this work, and the
   $3,500/month all-in price is worth comparing only against a genuinely processing-capable
   space.
2. **The opportunity reading.** The failures put real stainless, hoods, drainage and utility
   infrastructure on the market in the same cities where the food is, with landlords motivated
   to fill it. Some of these facilities — particularly the containerised or flexible
   commissary formats and the multi-tenanted commissaries rather than the delivery suites — are
   plausibly convertible, and the hourly-rental sector already demonstrates the model (Manning
   Canning runs five kitchens).

**The unresolved question that decides between the two readings:** what the health unit
actually approves a premises to do. Premises approval in Ontario attaches to a food premises
and its activities, not to a marketing category like "ghost kitchen." Whether a given ghost
kitchen site can hold an approval to *manufacture* shelf-stable preserves (as opposed to
prepare and serve food for delivery) is a per-site, per-municipality question that must be
asked of the health unit and the landlord, and it is not answerable from documents. This is the
named ask.

## What this file does not demonstrate

- It does not price Routes B or D at all. No lease rate, build-out cost, plumbing,
  ventilation, flooring or electrical cost was obtained.
- It does not price AgriKit. The most valuable single number available — a quote for a
  preserving-equipped container unit — has not been requested.
- It does not establish whether any shared kitchen, ghost kitchen, or container unit actually
  satisfies Ontario's requirements as a food premises for manufacturing shelf-stable preserves.
  That is a premises-approval question and it needs the health unit.
- It does not establish insurance costs in Canada. The $50–150/month figure is a booking
  platform's claim about general liability in the GTA, not a broker quote; product liability
  and recall cover are separate lines and no Canadian figure exists in this tree.
- The break-even arithmetic compares two published prices from different sources describing
  different facility types. It is arithmetic, not analysis, and it should not be quoted as a
  recommendation.
- The Syzl figures are from a booking platform that benefits from the rentals it describes.
  Recorded as published claims about Ontario, not as verified transaction prices.
- It does not establish the occupancy, pricing or condition of Toronto's ghost kitchen stock.
  One listing price and one operator's national footprint were all that could be verified.
