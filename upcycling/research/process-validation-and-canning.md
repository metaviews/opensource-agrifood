# Process validation and canning requirements

Research date: 2026-09-11
Method: document gathering for `upcycling/research-direction.md` target 2 — what a small
Ontario producer must actually do to have a defensible shelf-stable process. This is the
target that decides whether the concept's strongest cost argument (open, shared food-safety
documentation) is coherent, so it is the most consequential file here.

## Why the question matters to the concept

The openness ladder in `../concept.md` puts shared food-safety documentation at stage 2 and
calls it the highest-leverage rung, on the argument that validation is a fixed cost that no
micro plant can amortize alone. If validation is genuinely shareable — a template, a
protocol, a lab result that another plant can adopt — the argument holds. If validation is
necessarily site- and product-specific, the rung is much weaker and the honesty clause
applies.

## What was found in Canadian and Ontario documents

### Ontario expects a thermal process authority (verified)

Ontario's own Guide to Food and Beverage Manufacturing states, on thermal processing:

> Validating a thermal process for your product must be done by an appropriate laboratory or
> technology centre with a thermal process authority.

The same page establishes the familiar frame: a pH of 4.6 divides high-acid from low-acid
foods; low-acid foods (pH above 4.6) such as meat and vegetables need retort sterilization
(at least 121°C under pressure) to destroy *Clostridium botulinum* spores; spores "can be
inhibited at pH values of less than 4.6"; and acidification (vinegar, or fermentation
producing lactic acid — the guide names olives and sauerkraut) is what moves a naturally
low-acid food into the high-acid class. Shelf-life determinations are to be made by "qualified
resource" via quality and safety shelf-life studies.

Source: `ontario.ca/document/guide-food-and-beverage-manufacturing-ontario/food-preservation-and-processing`,
updated 2026-04-09.

**What this establishes:** the process-authority concept — which the sector conversation
usually attributes to the US — appears in Ontario's own guidance for thermal processing.
Validation is not a self-certification exercise here.

**What it does not establish:** whether a process authority is *required* for a high-acid
water-bath product (a jam at pH 3.4) as opposed to a low-acid or acidified product destined
for a hermetically sealed container. The guide's statement sits in a section on thermal
processing generally and does not itself draw the requirement line by product class. **That
line is the single most important open question in this concept** and it was not resolved by
documents in this pass.

### Canada does not appear to mirror the US statutory model

The United States regime is explicit and is what most sector writing quotes: commercial
processors of shelf-stable acidified foods and low-acid canned foods in hermetically sealed
containers must register each establishment and file scheduled processes with the FDA for
each product, product style, container size and type, and processing method (21 CFR 108), with
process authority validation and Food Canning Establishment (FCE) registration and scheduled
process (SID) filings. Acidified food = finished equilibrium pH of 4.6 or below with water
activity above 0.85; low-acid canned food = pH above 4.6 with water activity above 0.85.
Naturally acid foods (pH below 4.6) are exempt from the US canning regulations.

Nothing equivalent to the FDCA §108/113/114 registration-and-filing scheme was located in
Canadian federal documents in this pass. What Canada has is the SFCR preventive-controls and
PCP regime, which is outcome- and hazard-analysis-based rather than a named-product filed
process. Ontario's older Processed Products Regulations (C.R.C., c. 291) appear on the
Justice Laws site in archived form, consistent with the SFCR having absorbed the regime.

**Stated as a finding about this pass, not about Canada:** no Canadian equivalent to the US
establishment-registration and scheduled-process filing requirement was found in the documents
read. Absence of evidence in a single pass is not evidence of absence, and this is exactly the
kind of claim that should be settled by asking the regulator rather than by searching more.

### Training: the certification path is US-shaped

The Better Process Control School (BPCS) is the standard training attached to the US
acidified/low-acid regime. Published prices, read 2026-09-11 from a university provider
(Michigan State University, self-paced online): acidified course $315; retort course $485;
aseptic course $485; any two courses $570; all three $635; with group discounts. Other
providers (SilganU, IEH Academy, Zosi Learning) advertise the same certification, with a
Zosi acidified certification listed at $500.

Whether a Canadian producer is *expected* or *required* to hold BPCS, or whether Canadian
practice relies on equivalent competence demonstrated through the PCP, was not established by
documents in this pass. The courses are available to Canadians but are not a documented
Canadian requirement. **This is a named research question, not a finding.**

### What a validated process actually is, in the documents' terms

Synthesising only what the sources state: a validated process is one where a competent
authority has established the acidification and heat process by accepted scientific methods
for a specific product, container type and size, taking account of microbial load, storage
temperature, water activity and composition. The FAO/WHO Code of Hygienic Practice for Low
and Acidified Low-Acid Foods (CAC/RCP 23-1979) states the requirement in the strongest terms —
scheduled processes "must be established only by competent persons having expert knowledge of
thermal processing and having adequate facilities for making such determinations" — and notes
that improperly processed or sealed acidified canned foods have supported mould growth that
raised product pH above 4.6 and allowed *C. botulinum* growth.

That last sentence is the technical argument for why the validation layer exists, and why it
is the layer the sector keeps private.

## The consequence for the openness ladder

The concept's stage 2 (shared food-safety documentation) splits cleanly into two parts, and
the documents support the split:

- **Shareable without much legal discomfort:** the structure of a PCP for a defined facility
  type; hazard analyses for a product class; monitoring, corrective-action and verification
  procedures; record-keeping templates; the Ontario/CFIA guidance itself, which is already
  public. This is document architecture, and duplicating it privately plant by plant is
  waste.
- **Not obviously shareable:** the validated process for a specific product in a specific
  container, because by the sources' own terms it is established for that product, style,
  container and method, by a competent authority. A second plant adopting it is making its own
  validation claim, or relying on someone else's, and the liability question is real.

So stage 2 survives as an argument, but in a narrower form than the first draft implied: the
commons is the **documentation architecture and the hazard analysis**, while the **validated
process** remains product-and-site specific and may have to be re-established per plant or
per product family. The concept should say that rather than claiming validation is shareable
across a federation.

**Same-day revision (2026-09-11, second pass): the validated process is much cheaper than this
file first implied, and the argument has to be reweighted accordingly.** Published fee
schedules from US land-grant university process authorities (see §"Published fee schedules"
below) show process classification and process letters costing **$75–$195 per product/recipe**,
not thousands. Validation is therefore not, in absolute terms, the barrier to entry this
concept has been treating it as. What is genuinely difficult is narrower and more specific:

- **Availability, not price.** These services are the extension system's, and Canada appears to
  publish no equivalent service or fee schedule. A Canadian operator cannot buy the $75
  evaluation; that is the gap, and it is a gap in public infrastructure rather than in
  affordability.
- **The cost scales with variation, not volume.** Each recipe variation and each container size
  is charged separately — WSU's own example prices three salsa heats as three recipes, and three
  jar sizes as three more. A plant with two products in two jar sizes pays four times, at any
  volume. That is still a fixed-cost problem rather than a marginal one, but it is a few hundred
  dollars, not a capital barrier.
- **The record-keeping regime is the real weight.** The mandatory records for acidified foods
  (raw material examination, pH and acidity controls, fill and process temperatures, container
  closure inspections, thermometer calibrations, process deviations and corrective actions,
  initial distribution by code, supplier guarantees, recall plan — retained three years) are a
  continuing operational obligation that does not scale down for a small plant. This, not the
  validation fee, is what a proportionate small-operator tier would most usefully address.
- **The public capacity is being withdrawn.** WSU announced that from 1 July 2025 it continues
  process authority services but **no longer offers its discounted laboratory testing for
  acidified foods**, requiring producers to use a certified commercial lab instead, and stated
  the reason: "federal and state funding for extension services has consistently reduced over
  the years… we have reached a point where we are not able to sustain this service." The
  cheap-validation model is not a stable feature of the landscape; it is a declining public
  service.

The consequence for the concept: **the openness ladder's stage 2 argument should be reweighted
from cost-sharing to infrastructure-provision.** A shared food-safety documentation commons is
still worth building, but not primarily because validation is unaffordable — it is worth
building because the service does not exist in Canada, because records rather than fees are the
binding obligation, and because the public capacity that used to provide it is contracting.

functioning extension service, is on the order of a few hundred dollars plus the cost of
laboratory work.** The mandatory record-keeping attached to it (see the "consequence" section)
is a larger and more permanent obligation than the fee.

## Correction: what jams and jellies are, and where ferments sit

Second-pass correction to this file's framing, and it changes the concept's picture materially.

**Jams and jellies are not the hard case — they are the easy one.** WSU states that "jams and
jellies which meet the published standards of identity are exempt since their water activities
are 0.85 or less," and Alaska's guidance is consistent. The exemption here runs on **water
activity**, not simply on acidity, and it means the concept's primary committed product class
sits outside the acidified-foods regime in the US model. Earlier in this file the product class
was described as "high-acid water-bath," which is the food-science label; the regulatory label
matters more, and for preserves it is more favourable than assumed.

**Fermented products are excluded, and are also excluded from the service.** Alaska's guidance
states plainly that "acidified foods do not include fermented foods such as sauerkraut, kimchee,
water kefir, etc." WSU states it "does not provide process authority evaluations for any
fermented products or products which have not been thermally processed." So the concept's *named
ferment* — the second half of its committed product class — sits outside both the process
authority route and the acidified-foods filing regime. That may mean less paperwork; it also
means less available expertise, no process letter to obtain from at least one public provider,
and a less-defined regulatory position rather than a clearly easier one.

**What this does to the concept's product-class logic:** the two halves of the committed class
are in genuinely different regulatory situations — preserves via a water-activity exemption,
ferments via exclusion — and neither is the "high-acid water-bath validation" case this file
originally treated them as. The class commitment still holds, but the reason and the paperwork
differ per half, and the concept should say so rather than treating the pair as one regulatory
problem.

## What this file does not demonstrate

- It does not determine whether a high-acid water-bath product in Ontario requires a process
  authority, a lab validation, or neither. This is the priority question and it was not
  answerable from documents in this pass. It needs a named authority or inspector to answer it.
- It does not establish any Canadian requirement equivalent to, or different from, the US
  21 CFR 108/113/114 scheme.
- It does not price validation: neither a laboratory's fee for pH, water activity, or
  shelf-life studies, nor a process authority's engagement, was obtained.
- It does not price validation **in Canada**, which remains the open item. Every fee in the
  published-schedule section above is a US figure from a state extension service. Whether
  Ontario has an equivalent service, what it charges, and whether any Canadian laboratory will
  issue a process letter for a commercial high-acid product are all **unverified**. A Canadian
  operator may face no published route at all, which is a different problem from an expensive
  one.
- It does not establish the Canadian regulatory position of fermented products. The exclusion
  language cited is American; what Ontario requires or permits for commercially sold sauerkraut
  or kimchi is unread.
- It does not establish whether Canadian labelling, standards of identity (what may legally be
  called a "jam"), or bilingual requirements carry their own exemption structure like the US
  small-business nutrition labelling exemption.
- It does not establish whether BPCS or an equivalent is expected in Canada.
- It does not address labelling, standards of identity (e.g. what may legally be called a
  "jam"), or bilingual requirements, all of which carry their own compliance cost.
