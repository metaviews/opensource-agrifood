Last reviewed: 2026-09-11. This is the single place that says where the project is, what is finished, what is open, and what comes next. It replaces the "current phase" prose that used to live in the README. Update it whenever a workstream changes state; the README only points here.

## The project in one paragraph

Open Source Agrifood is a research and knowledge-building project asking who controls the systems — hardware, data, standards, models, seeds, governance — that make up open agrifood, and who benefits. It has three workstreams that share a repository but have different tempos: the **research corpus** (records, scans, syntheses, register), the **grocery concept** (a concept-development space that stress-tests an open grocery operation against real cases), and the **event** (*Building the Open-Source Future of Canadian Agrifood*, a Future Herd spectacular event, 29 October 2026, Discord). The event is a beneficiary of the research, not its organizing purpose.

Ten proposals, each with precedent, failure mode and test; five require statutory change, five can be done administratively.
- Support layer mapped (2026-09-11, at the user's direction): `upcycling/research/cfin-and-support-programmes.md`. CFIN is a national member network (26.3K+ members and followers; $23.7M awarded across 130+ projects; five Regional Innovation Directors) whose YODL platform is a searchable directory of co-packers, suppliers, consultants and lab spaces — three of the concept's outstanding questions in one place, free to join. None of its three funding programmes funds infrastructure; Foodtech Next funds demonstrations in operational environments, which makes a community pilot a plausible funded *host site* rather than an applicant. The Foodtech in Canada ecosystem report is gated to a paid tier, consistent with the project's recurring finding that the sector's own information is sold. Food 4.0 turns out to be CFIN's *proposed* action plan rather than enacted policy — but it carries the industry-side statement that food is "largely absent from Canada's core innovation agenda," which the policy document now cites instead of asserting the premise itself.

Two document findings changed the concept — Ontario's low-risk home-based exemption list is bakery-shaped and does not cover canned preserves, so the pilot needs an approved commercial premises; and the province's own manufacturing guide expects a thermal process authority, which narrows the openness ladder's stage 2 to shareable documentation architecture rather than shareable validated processes. The concept's most important open question is now whether a high-acid water-bath product requires process-authority validation at all; targets 3 and 4 (input availability and the commercial surplus market) remain fieldwork-bound and unstarted. **Second research pass completed the same day (2026-09-11): the minimum viable kitchen and the shared/ghost kitchen sector** (`upcycling/research/kitchen-mvp-and-shared-infrastructure.md`). It reframes the facility question as a sequencing question rather than a capital one — a compliant kitchen's expensive elements are building systems (drainage, grease interception, mechanical ventilation, flooring, lighting to Building Code), not cooking equipment, which is why renting inspected kitchen hours dominates building. Manning Canning Kitchens (Leaside, Toronto) is the closest Ontario precedent: a preserves company renting five individually let certified kitchens at $22–38.50/hr with two 30-gallon steam kettles, a piston filler and an industrial dishwasher, and it accepts concept-stage businesses. Published prices put break-even between hourly rental and a dedicated suite at roughly 91–160 production hours a month, which a seasonal glut-driven operation is unlikely to clear year-round. AgriKit, investigated at the user's direction, is a Canadian supplier of turnkey modular processing facilities built in 20- and 40-foot containers (CSA A277, 4–6 months to operation, free grant-writing support for non-profits, charities and Indigenous communities, no published price) — recorded as infrastructure to buy or rent and as a lead for the parent corpus's A1 workstream, not as an open artifact. The delivery-oriented ghost kitchen asset class is documented as largely failed (Kitchen United sold or closed every physical unit and pivoted to software; REEF mass closures; CloudKitchens at ~50% occupancy in early 2023; no major US operator funded since 2021), which makes it a source of cheap under-used equipped space rather than a source of suitable processing kitchens — the deciding question, unanswered, is whether a rented premises can hold health-unit approval to manufacture shelf-stable preserves.
