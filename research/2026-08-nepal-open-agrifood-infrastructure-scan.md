# Environmental Scan: Nepal open agrifood infrastructure and community systems

- Date: 2026-08-19
- Method: Bounded regional pass focused on Nepal; 8 targeted query sets; 9 institutional, academic, and project pages/PDFs read or extracted in full; no repository licence claims accepted without primary verification.
- Focus: South/SE Asia regional thin cell; Nepal as the named country cell. The pass tests whether Nepal’s open agrifood layer is best understood as open-source software, community biological commons, locally transferable hardware, or institutionally controlled digital infrastructure.

## Why this cell matters

The corpus has strong South Asian coverage through India’s DPI and language infrastructure, but Nepal is largely absent except as a destination of open biochar technology transfer. Nepal is therefore a useful test of whether the open agrifood layer appears through formal software projects or through community institutions, farmer-managed biological resources, locally manufactured tools, and public-interest systems that do not use open-source language.

## Headline Finding

**Nepal’s open agrifood layer is strongest as farmer-managed biological commons and locally transferable, low-cost technology; its digital layer is active but institutionally controlled, and its formal open-source software/licensing layer remains thin.**

The pass resolves the country cell into four named shapes:

1. **Community seed-bank commons**: locally managed seed institutions, a national association, farmer rights, seed exchange, traditional knowledge, and an online local-variety catalogue. This is a genuine commons/governance layer, but not an open-source licence system.
2. **Open-transfer biochar technology**: the existing Kon-Tiki record is materially strengthened by Nepal-specific field research and government-linked field trials. The design is transferable and farmer-scale, but not farmer-governed and still lacks a formal hardware licence.
3. **Locally owned post-harvest technology in formation**: SolarFood is developing locally manufactured solar dryers with women-led cooperatives. It is a promising open-transfer/local-manufacturing lead, not yet a verified open design or deployed commons.
4. **Public-interest digital infrastructure with closed governance**: DESIS 2.0 is a nationally institutionalized seed information and certification platform with real operational scope, APIs, mobile tools, and blockchain-backed tracking. Earlier project documentation described it as open-source software, but no current source code, licence, or open-data terms were located. It is therefore candidate public infrastructure, not curated open source.

The important regional finding is not that Nepal lacks digital agriculture. It is that **the most farmer-controlled layer is biological and organizational, while the most technologically advanced digital layer is state/institutionally governed and licence-opaque**.

## Currents

### 1. Community seed banks as a living biological commons

Nepal has a long-running community seed-bank movement promoted by LI-BIRD and partners. Community seed banks are collectively managed by farmers for maintenance, conservation, exchange, documentation, and local seed enterprise. The Community Seed Banks Association of Nepal (CSBAN) links these institutions, supports local varieties, and has developed an online catalogue and the “Our Seed” mobile app.

This is stronger farmer agency than a conventional open dataset: communities retain practical control over seed circulation, local knowledge, and some income-generating activity. It is not equivalent to OSSL or OSSI. The evidence points to customary and institutional commons governance, not a standard open licence.

### 2. Open technology transfer without formal hardware licensing

The Kon-Tiki kiln is already in the corpus, but the Nepal evidence is significant. The peer-reviewed PLOS ONE study includes Nepal Agroforestry Foundation researchers and Nepal field trials, documenting farmer-scale char quality and emissions. A Nordic Development Fund account of Nepal’s Sustainable Rural Ecology for Green Growth project documents field trials in 15 villages, farmer-made biochar fertilizer, farmer-to-farmer dissemination, and Ministry of Agriculture involvement.

This is a strong open-transfer case: the design is simple, reproducible, locally buildable, and adapted to a Global South production context. The governance is still originator- and project-led rather than farmer-governed, and the formal licence gap remains.

### 3. Women-led cooperative manufacture as an emerging openness pathway

The SolarFood project in Nepal and Bhutan is developing solar dryers intended to be locally manufactured and socially accepted. Its 2025 phase explicitly centers women-led agricultural cooperatives, local ownership, local manufacturing, and inclusive business models. The 2022 needs assessment included Nepal farmer interviews and identifies adoption, gender, local production, and training as design conditions.

The project matters because it may connect open physical design to ownership and value capture. At this stage, however, the project has not publicly established an open hardware licence, released design files, or demonstrated independent replication. It belongs in the lead/candidate layer, not as an open-source success claim.

### 4. Digital seed infrastructure as interface-open, governance-closed

DESIS began as a CIMMYT/USAID-supported digital seed information system and is now DESIS 2.0 under Nepal’s Seed Quality Control Center. The current platform describes seed certification and tracking, catalogue, inspection, and seed-balance functions. CIMMYT reports a catalogue of more than 80 crops and 800 varieties, integration across government and seed-sector actors, mobile inspection, QR/blockchain-backed traceability, and use across federal, provincial, and local levels.

A 2020 CIMMYT account called the underlying software open source. The current site exposes a service interface but no source repository, licence, API documentation, or open-data terms were located in this pass. The correct classification is therefore **institutional public-interest infrastructure with an unresolved openness claim**.

## Contested claims

### “Community seed banks are open-source seed systems.”

Asserting: Nepali CSBs conserve, exchange, document, and locally produce seed through farmer-managed institutions. Contesting: the evidence describes community governance and farmers’ rights, not an OSSL/OSSI-style licence, documented derivative obligations, or a standard open-data regime. Status: **biological commons, not open-source licensing**.

### “DESIS is open-source agricultural infrastructure.”

Asserting: the 2020 CIMMYT project account described DESIS as based on open-source software. Contesting: the current DESIS 2.0 site exposes a government service but no current source repository, licence, API terms, or open-data policy was located. Status: **historical open-source claim unresolved; current platform candidate public infrastructure**.

### “Local manufacturing makes SolarFood open.”

Asserting: SolarFood explicitly seeks local ownership, local manufacturing, and women-led cooperative business models. Contesting: no public design licence, source files, or independent replication evidence was located. Status: **promising open-transfer lead, not yet open source**.

## Gaps and residuals

1. CSBAN’s current membership, number of active seed banks, financing model, and “Our Seed” software/data terms require direct verification.
2. The current CSB guideline’s legal status and implementation should be checked against Nepal’s present seed regulation and federal/provincial agricultural policy.
3. DESIS 2.0 needs a source-and-licence audit: code ownership, developer/vendor, API documentation, data retention, farmer access, and export rights.
4. SolarFood’s 2025–2026 fieldwork and selected women-led cooperatives should be revisited when results, designs, costs, or business-model documents become public.
5. The Babai irrigation pilot is a strong adjacent public-interest deployment, but this pass did not establish an open software or data layer.
6. Nepali-language discovery was limited; this pass did not systematically search Nepali-language repositories, video, procurement notices, or local maker communities.
7. No verified Nepal-hosted open farm-management software, open agricultural hardware repository, or farmer-controlled digital data cooperative was identified in this pass.

## Actors (critical lens)

- **Farmer and community institutions**: CSBs, CSBAN, women seed custodians, seed enterprises, and water-user associations provide the strongest evidence of agency.
- **National public institutions**: Seed Quality Control Center, CCDABC, National Gene Bank, NARC, and the Department of Agriculture provide formal infrastructure and regulatory anchoring.
- **Research and development intermediaries**: LI-BIRD, CIMMYT, Bioversity/CIAT, IWMI, Kathmandu University, Ruralis, and project funders supply technical and institutional capacity.
- **Technology developers and vendors**: DESIS’s local technology provider and the SolarFood engineering partners are important but their ownership, licensing, and maintenance terms are not yet transparent.

## Interpretation

Nepal adds an important regional qualification to the corpus’s wider landscape finding. Open agrifood infrastructure does not necessarily arrive as a Git repository or OSI licence. In Nepal, a more durable open layer may be assembled from community seed institutions, shared agronomic knowledge, locally fabricable tools, farmer-to-farmer transfer, and public systems that are accessible without being open in the software sense.

This does not justify treating all community or public-interest infrastructure as open source. The distinctions matter: CSBAN is a biological/community commons; Kon-Tiki is open by publication and transfer; SolarFood is an emerging local-manufacturing project; DESIS is a public-interest digital platform with an unresolved openness claim.

## What this scan does not do

1. It does not claim that Nepal lacks open agrifood projects; it reports what was located and verified in one bounded English-language pass.
2. It does not establish current national scale for CSBAN, DESIS, SolarFood, or the Babai irrigation pilot.
3. It does not establish that community seed banks satisfy an open-source seed licence or that DESIS currently releases open code.
4. It does not measure farmer outcomes, gendered control, benefit sharing, or long-term maintenance in the field.
5. It does not substitute for Nepali-language and fieldwork verification.
